Prediction Markets Explained

9 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

A prediction market is an exchange where people trade contracts whose payoff depends on whether a real-world event happens, such as an election result, an inflation print or the price of Bitcoin at a set time. Most contracts pay a fixed amount, usually $1, if the outcome occurs and nothing if it does not, so the trading price works as a rough, real-time estimate of the event's probability. Prediction markets have grown from academic experiments into venues that trade billions of dollars a month, and they now sit in a contested space between finance, gambling and public forecasting.

Key takeaways

  • An event contract typically pays $1 if an outcome happens and $0 if it does not, so a price of 30 cents implies roughly a 30 percent chance.
  • Prices move as traders buy and sell, which lets the market combine many people's information into a single number.
  • Unlike a sportsbook, most prediction markets match traders against each other, and you can usually sell before the event resolves.
  • The rules that decide how a market resolves matter as much as the event itself, and ambiguous wording is a real risk.
  • Legal status differs by country and has changed quickly in 2025 and 2026, so check current rules and each platform's terms before trading.

How an event contract works

Every prediction market is built around a question with a defined set of outcomes and a defined deadline. The simplest form is binary: "Will X happen by date Y?" with two outcomes, YES and NO.

The $1 payout

On most platforms one YES share plus one NO share always adds up to a claim on exactly $1, because exactly one of them will win. That gives the contract three useful properties:

  1. Prices are bounded. A share can trade anywhere between $0 and $1 (often quoted as 0 to 100 cents) and never beyond.
  2. Price approximates probability. If YES trades at 62 cents, the market as a whole is acting as if the event has about a 62 percent chance, before fees and other distortions. The article on pricing and probability covers the adjustments.
  3. Maximum loss is known. A buyer can lose only what they paid. A YES buyer at 62 cents risks 62 cents to make 38 cents.

Worked example

Suppose a market asks whether a country's headline inflation rate for a given month will come in above 3.0 percent. YES trades at 37 cents.

  • You buy 100 YES shares for 100 × $0.37 = $37.00.
  • If the figure prints above 3.0 percent, each share pays $1, so you receive $100.00. Your profit is $100.00 − $37.00 = $63.00, before fees.
  • If it prints at or below 3.0 percent, the shares pay nothing and you lose $37.00.

If your own research says the true chance is 45 percent, the expected value per share is 0.45 × $1.00 − $0.37 = $0.08, or $8.00 across 100 shares. That positive number is only as good as your 45 percent estimate. If the market is right and the true chance is 37 percent, your expected profit is zero before fees and negative after them.

You do not have to hold to the end. If the price rises to 55 cents a week later, you can sell your 100 shares for $55.00 and lock in an $18.00 gain whatever the final number turns out to be.

Multi-outcome markets

Many questions have more than two answers: who will win an election with five candidates, or which range a figure will land in. Platforms usually handle these as a set of linked binary contracts, one per outcome. In a well-functioning market, the YES prices across all outcomes sum to roughly $1, and any big gap from $1 is either a fee effect or an arbitrage opportunity.

Order books and liquidity

Most large prediction markets run a central limit order book: buyers post bids, sellers post asks, and trades happen when they cross. The difference between the best bid and the best ask is the spread, and it can be wide on obscure markets. Some smaller venues use an automated market maker, a formula that quotes prices from a pool of funds. Either way, thin markets mean you may move the price against yourself when you trade size.

A short history

Early election betting

Betting on elections is old. Economic historians Paul Rhode and Koleman Strumpf documented large, organized markets on US presidential elections in New York in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, which often forecast winners well before modern polling existed. These markets faded as polling grew and as gambling laws tightened.

The Iowa Electronic Markets (1988)

The modern academic form began with the Iowa Electronic Markets, launched in 1988 by faculty at the University of Iowa. Traders put in small sums to buy contracts on US election results. The market ran under a no-action letter from the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) because of its research purpose and tiny stakes. Its track record against polls helped convince economists that markets could aggregate forecasts.

Corporate and government experiments

In the 2000s, several large companies ran internal prediction markets so employees could bet play money or small prizes on project deadlines and sales figures. A US defense research program called the Policy Analysis Market was canceled in 2003 after public criticism that it would let people bet on terrorist attacks, an early example of the ethical arguments that still surround the field.

Intrade and PredictIt

Intrade, based in Ireland, was the best-known real-money site of the 2000s. The CFTC sued it in 2012 for offering contracts to US customers without registration, and it shut down in 2013. PredictIt launched in 2014, operated with a New Zealand university under a CFTC no-action letter that capped how much each trader could put into a contract and how many traders a contract could have. Those limits were loosened under a 2025 agreement with the CFTC; check its current terms.

Polymarket and Kalshi

Two platforms came to dominate in the 2020s, with very different models:

  • Polymarket, founded in 2020, is crypto-based. It runs a central limit order book, settles trades on the Polygon blockchain using USDC-style stablecoin collateral, and resolves most markets through UMA's optimistic oracle. It became widely known during the 2024 US election. Its relationship with US regulators has changed several times; see the regulation article.
  • Kalshi is a CFTC-regulated designated contract market, the same category of exchange that lists futures. It operates in US dollars under federal oversight and won a court case in 2024 that let it list contracts on US congressional control. Its sports contracts have since been challenged by several US states.

Newer venues have followed, including Predict.fun on BNB Chain, which uses USDT collateral and runs short 5-minute and 15-minute crypto up/down rounds settled on a Chainlink price stream. The platforms comparison covers the main options neutrally.

Why prediction markets can forecast well

The wisdom of crowds

The core argument is that many independent, imperfect estimates, when combined, can be more accurate than most individual experts. Economist Friedrich Hayek argued in 1945 that prices pull together knowledge scattered among many people. A prediction market applies the same idea to a narrow question.

Three conditions help a crowd forecast well:

  • Diversity of information. Traders hold different pieces of the picture.
  • Independence. They do not all copy the same source.
  • Incentives. Real money rewards being right and penalizes being wrong, which discourages cheap talk.

Where they fall short

Markets are not oracles of truth. Known weaknesses include:

  • Thin liquidity. A small market can be moved by one trader with a few thousand dollars.
  • Biased participants. Traders who love a candidate or team may overpay for it.
  • The long-shot bias. In many betting and prediction markets, unlikely outcomes tend to trade above their true probability.
  • Manipulation. Someone may push a price to create a false impression. Deep, active markets resist this better than shallow ones.
  • Resolution risk. The market may settle on a technicality that differs from what most traders expected. See resolution and oracles.

Academic research, such as work by Justin Wolfers and Eric Zitzewitz, has generally found prediction markets to be competitive forecasters, often matching or beating polls and expert panels, but not infallible.

How they differ from betting and options

Versus a sportsbook

Feature Sportsbook Prediction market exchange
Counterparty The bookmaker Other traders
Pricing Set by the bookmaker, with a built-in margin Set by bids and asks
Exit before the event Sometimes via "cash out", at the book's price Usually yes, by selling in the order book
Limits on winners Books may limit consistently winning customers Exchanges generally do not, as they do not take the other side
Topics Mostly sports Politics, economics, crypto, culture and sports

A betting exchange such as those in the UK sits between the two: it matches backers and layers like a prediction market but is licensed as gambling.

Versus options

A binary event contract is close to what options traders call a cash-or-nothing digital option: it pays a fixed sum if a condition is met at expiry. Standard options differ in that their payoff grows with how far the underlying moves past the strike. Event contracts also cover things no options market does, such as election results or award winners. Note that "binary options" sold by offshore brokers to retail traders are a different and often predatory product; regulators in the EU and elsewhere have restricted them.

Risks to understand before trading

  • You can lose the full stake on every position. A contract bought at 90 cents goes to zero if the unexpected happens.
  • Most short-term traders lose money. Fees, spreads and better-informed counterparties work against casual traders, as in other markets.
  • Platform and custody risk. On crypto platforms you hold stablecoins in a wallet, which brings wallet security and stablecoin risks.
  • Legal risk. Access rules vary by country and US state, and they change. Using a platform where it is not permitted can breach its terms and local law.
  • Gambling-like behavior. Short rounds that settle every few minutes can feel like a slot machine. If trading stops feeling like a choice, support for problem gambling is available in most countries, and the article on handling losses and tilt covers warning signs.

Common mistakes

  • Treating price as certainty. A 90 cent contract still loses one time in ten if the price is fair.
  • Skipping the rules. Traders often lose on the exact wording, deadline or data source rather than on the event itself.
  • Ignoring fees and spreads. On a cheap long shot, a 2 cent spread is a large share of the price.
  • Oversizing near-certain bets. Many small wins can be wiped out by one full loss.
  • Assuming a platform is legal where you live. Check its terms and local law first.

FAQ

Are prediction markets legal?

It depends on where you live and which platform you use. In the US, CFTC-regulated exchanges such as Kalshi offer event contracts, but several states have challenged sports contracts in court as of November 2026. Many other countries treat these markets as gambling or block certain platforms, so check local rules and each platform's terms.

How do prediction markets make money?

Most platforms earn trading fees, often charged to takers, and some earn from deposits, withdrawals or interest on customer funds. Exchange-style platforms do not need you to lose for them to earn, unlike a traditional bookmaker that profits from its built-in margin.

Is a prediction market price the same as a probability?

Roughly, but not exactly. Fees, the bid-ask spread, the long-shot bias and the cost of tying up money until resolution all push prices away from true probabilities, especially at the extremes near 0 and 100 cents.

What happens if a market's outcome is unclear?

The platform's resolution process decides. Crypto platforms may use an oracle with a dispute mechanism, while regulated exchanges apply their published rules and data sources. Some markets can resolve to an unexpected outcome or be split 50/50, which is why reading the rules matters.
