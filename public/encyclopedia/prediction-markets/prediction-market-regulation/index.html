Prediction Market Regulation

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Prediction market regulation decides who may run event contract markets, who may trade on them and which events they may cover. In the US, event contracts listed on federally regulated exchanges fall under the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC), but states argue that many of them, especially sports contracts, are gambling that states control. Other countries mostly treat prediction markets as gambling or block certain platforms. This area has changed month by month in 2025 and 2026, so everything below is dated, and you should check current rules and each platform's terms before trading.

Key takeaways

  • In the US, event contracts on a CFTC-registered exchange are regulated as derivatives, and the CFTC can block contracts it finds contrary to the public interest.
  • Kalshi won a 2024 court case that let it list contracts on US congressional control, and its sports contracts have since been challenged by several states.
  • As of November 2026, federal appeals courts had split on whether states can apply gambling laws to sports event contracts, and New Jersey had asked the US Supreme Court to decide.
  • Polymarket settled with the CFTC in 2022 and blocked US users, then re-entered the US through an acquired exchange with a more limited product.
  • Outside the US, many regulators treat prediction markets as gambling, and several have restricted access to crypto-based platforms.

Why prediction markets are hard to classify

An event contract looks like a financial derivative: it is standardized, traded on an exchange and settles on a measurable outcome. It also looks like a bet: you pay a stake and win a fixed amount if something happens. Which label applies decides which regulator is in charge, what licenses are needed, who may trade and how winnings are taxed. Different courts and countries have answered the question differently, which is why the rules are unsettled.

The United States

The federal framework

The Commodity Exchange Act gives the CFTC authority over futures, options and swaps, and the CFTC treats event contracts listed on its registered exchanges as part of that category, alongside futures. An exchange registered as a designated contract market (DCM) can list new contracts by certifying that they comply with the rules.

Since a 2010 amendment under the Dodd-Frank Act, the CFTC may prohibit event contracts that involve terrorism, assassination, war, gaming or unlawful activity, or similar activity, if it determines they are contrary to the public interest. Its rule implementing this power is often called Rule 40.11. Much of the legal fight over prediction markets is about what "gaming" covers.

CFTC rulemaking in 2026

In March 2026 the CFTC asked for public comment on event contracts traded on prediction markets, and in June 2026 it proposed changes to how it reviews whether event contracts are contrary to the public interest. Reports on the proposal described separate treatment for some categories, such as contracts on economic indicators, elections and sports. As of the most recent reports available when this article was written, those rules had not been finalized. Check the CFTC's current rules.

Kalshi and election contracts

Kalshi became a CFTC-designated contract market in 2020. When it sought to list contracts on which party would control the US Congress, the CFTC blocked them in 2023, arguing they involved gaming and were against the public interest. Kalshi sued. In September 2024 a federal district court in Washington, DC ruled in Kalshi's favor, and Kalshi began listing the contracts shortly before the 2024 election. The CFTC later dropped its appeal in 2025.

Sports contracts and the states

Kalshi began listing contracts on sports outcomes in early 2025, and other operators, including some brokerage apps, followed. Several state gambling regulators sent cease-and-desist letters, arguing that the contracts were unlicensed sports betting. Kalshi and others sued, arguing that federal law preempts state gambling law for contracts on a CFTC-regulated exchange.

As of November 2026, the courts were divided:

Court Timing (2026) Reported outcome
Third Circuit (New Jersey) April Sided with Kalshi, finding federal law likely preempts state gambling law for these contracts
Ninth Circuit (Nevada) Mid-2026 Found the sports contracts were not swaps and that state gambling law could apply; Kalshi sought rehearing
Sixth Circuit (Ohio, Tennessee) September Allowed the states to enforce their gambling laws
Second Circuit (New York, Connecticut) November Declined to block state enforcement while appeals continue

In September 2026 New Jersey asked the US Supreme Court to review the issue. Whether the Court takes the case, and how it rules, will shape the market nationally. Until then, availability of sports contracts may differ by state and can change quickly. These summaries describe preliminary rulings and appeals in fast-moving cases; check current reporting and each platform's state availability.

Polymarket's US history

  • January 2022. The CFTC found that Polymarket had offered event-based binary options to US users without registering. Polymarket agreed to pay a $1.4 million civil penalty, wind down non-compliant markets and block US users.
  • 2024. Polymarket grew rapidly around the US election while officially closed to US users. In November 2024 federal agents searched its chief executive's home as part of investigations.
  • 2025. Those investigations were reported closed without charges. In July 2025 Polymarket agreed to buy QCEX, a CFTC-licensed exchange and clearing house, and later in 2025 it received CFTC staff relief and launched a separate, more limited US product.
  • 2026. In April 2026 the company was reported to be seeking CFTC approval to open its main international exchange to US users. As of the reports available in November 2026, that had not been confirmed.

Check Polymarket's current terms for who may use which product, and see the platforms comparison for how its model differs from Kalshi's.

Other US venues

PredictIt has operated since 2014 under a CFTC no-action letter for an academic research market, with limits on stakes and traders. The CFTC moved to withdraw that letter in 2022, leading to litigation, and a 2025 agreement loosened the limits. The Iowa Electronic Markets operate under a similar arrangement.

Other jurisdictions in general terms

  • United Kingdom. Betting, including on politics, is legal through bookmakers and betting exchanges licensed by the Gambling Commission. Financial spread betting falls under the Financial Conduct Authority. Crypto prediction markets without a UK gambling license operate in a gray or prohibited zone.
  • European Union. Gambling is regulated country by country. After the 2024 US election, France's gambling regulator moved against Polymarket, which then restricted French users. The EU's MiCA regulation covers crypto-asset service providers but does not settle whether a prediction market is gambling.
  • Asia-Pacific and elsewhere. Some regulators, including Singapore's, have blocked Polymarket as unlicensed gambling, and several other countries restricted crypto prediction markets in 2024 and 2025. A lack of specific rules does not mean a market is permitted.

See regulators and investor protection for how financial regulators work in general, and betting exchange trading for the gambling-licensed model.

Market integrity rules

On CFTC-regulated exchanges, manipulation and fraud are prohibited, and exchange rules bar trading on material non-public information, such as an athlete trading on their own game. Crypto platforms set their own terms. Anywhere, insider trading or price manipulation can bring account bans and legal action.

What changing rules mean for traders

Worked example: a market closes in your state

You hold 300 YES contracts on a sports event, bought at 40 cents for $120.00. Your state wins a court ruling, and the platform switches your account to "close only" for sports markets.

  • The best bid is now 37 cents, because many traders in the same position are selling.
  • Selling all 300 returns 300 × $0.37 = $111.00, a loss of $9.00 before fees.
  • Holding to resolution may still be allowed, or the platform may settle positions on its own terms. The outcome depends on the platform's rules and the court order.

Regulatory risk rarely shows up in the price until it happens, so size positions on contested markets with this in mind.

A practical checklist

  1. Check whether the platform accepts users in your country and, in the US, your state.
  2. Read its terms on restricted regions and what happens to open positions if access changes.
  3. Never use a VPN or false details to get around geoblocks or identity checks; it breaches terms and may break the law.
  4. Keep records of every trade for tax purposes; see trading taxes overview, as treatment varies by country.
  5. Recheck the rules regularly, because they have changed several times a year recently.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming "available in my app" means "legal for me." Apps and websites can lag behind court orders and regulations.
  • Relying on old articles. Rulings from even a few months ago may have been overturned or superseded.
  • Ignoring tax obligations. Unclear classification does not remove the duty to report gains.

FAQ

Are prediction markets legal in the US?

Event contracts on CFTC-regulated exchanges such as Kalshi are legal under federal law, but as of November 2026 several states were challenging sports contracts in court, and federal appeals courts had reached conflicting decisions. Availability can differ by state, so check the platform's current terms.

Is Kalshi legal in every state?

Kalshi operates under federal CFTC regulation, but its sports contracts have faced state challenges, and as of November 2026 some courts had allowed states to enforce gambling laws while cases continue. Its non-sports contracts have faced less challenge. Check its current availability for your state.

Is Polymarket legal in the US?

Polymarket blocked US users after a 2022 CFTC settlement, then bought a CFTC-licensed exchange in 2025 and launched a separate, limited US product. As of reports available in November 2026, its main international exchange was still seeking approval to serve US users. Check its current terms.

Why do states want to regulate prediction markets?

States license and tax sports betting, and they argue that sports event contracts are bets that should follow the same rules, including age limits and consumer protections. Operators argue that federal law gives the CFTC exclusive authority over contracts on registered exchanges.
