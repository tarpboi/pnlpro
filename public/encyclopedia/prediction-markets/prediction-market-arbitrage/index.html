Prediction Market Arbitrage

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Prediction Markets

Prediction market arbitrage means buying a combination of contracts that pays a fixed amount whatever happens, for less than that amount. Because every binary contract pays $1 or $0, a set of positions that covers every outcome is worth exactly $1, so any chance to buy that set for less looks like locked-in profit. In practice, fees, partial fills, money tied up until resolution and small differences in how two markets are worded mean that true risk-free arbitrage is rare and short-lived.

Key takeaways

  • Complementary arbitrage buys every outcome of one market, such as YES and NO, for less than $1 in total.
  • Cross-venue arbitrage buys YES on one platform and NO on another when the two prices add up to less than $1.
  • Fees on both legs often consume most of the gross spread, so the math must be done after costs.
  • Capital is locked until resolution, which can turn a 2 percent gain into a poor annual return.
  • The biggest hidden risk is rule mismatch: two markets that look identical can resolve differently, and then both legs can lose.

Why arbitrage exists in prediction markets

Gaps open because prediction markets are fragmented. Different platforms have different users, collateral, fee structures and access rules, and money cannot move between them instantly. A crypto venue settling in stablecoins and a regulated exchange settling in dollars may price the same election differently for days because few traders can, or are allowed to, use both. Within a single platform, gaps open briefly when news hits and one side of a book reprices before the other.

Complementary outcome arbitrage

Binary markets

In a binary market, one YES share plus one NO share always pays $1. If the YES ask plus the NO ask is below $1, buying both locks in the difference. On most platforms the YES and NO books are matched together, so this rarely appears, and when it does it lasts seconds.

Multi-outcome markets

In a market with several mutually exclusive outcomes, exactly one YES pays $1. If the YES asks across all outcomes sum to less than $1, buying one of each locks in a profit. The reverse also exists: in a market with N outcomes, holding NO on every outcome pays N − 1 dollars, because all but one NO wins. If the NO asks sum to less than N − 1, buying them all locks in the gap.

Watch for outcomes you might miss. If a market lists five named candidates and "Other," you must buy all six. Missing one turns the arbitrage into a bet.

Cross-venue arbitrage

The more common form pairs two platforms. If Venue A sells YES cheaply and Venue B sells NO cheaply on the same event, buying both covers every outcome.

Worked example with fees and lockup

An event trades on two venues. Venue A offers YES at 42 cents. Venue B offers NO at 53 cents. You buy 1,000 of each.

Gross:

  • Cost: 1,000 × $0.42 + 1,000 × $0.53 = $420.00 + $530.00 = $950.00.
  • Payout: exactly one side wins, paying 1,000 × $1.00 = $1,000.00.
  • Gross profit: $50.00, or 5.26 percent of $950.

After fees (assumed for this example):

  • Venue A charges 1 cent per contract: 1,000 × $0.01 = $10.00.
  • Venue B charges 0.07 × price × (1 − price) per contract: 0.07 × 0.53 × 0.47 = $0.017437, so 1,000 × $0.017437 = about $17.44.
  • Total cost: $950.00 + $10.00 + $17.44 = $977.44.
  • Net profit: $1,000.00 − $977.44 = $22.56, or 2.31 percent.

After time:

  • If the event resolves in four months, the 2.31 percent is earned over a third of a year. As a simple annual rate, that is about 2.31 × 3 = 6.9 percent.
  • If cash earns 4 percent a year elsewhere, the extra return is about 2.9 percentage points a year, for taking on the execution, platform and rule risks below.

Deposit and withdrawal fees, blockchain transaction costs and any currency conversion between dollars and stablecoins reduce this further. More than half of the gross spread went to fees in this example, which is typical.

The risks

Rule mismatch

Two markets can look identical and still resolve differently, because of a different deadline, time zone, data source or definition. This is the most dangerous risk, because it can make both legs lose.

Example. Venue A asks whether a nominee will be "confirmed by March 31." Venue B asks whether the same nominee will be "sworn in by March 31." A trader buys NO on A and YES on B, treating them as the same event. The nominee is confirmed on March 30 and sworn in on April 2.

  • A's NO loses, because the confirmation happened before the deadline.
  • B's YES loses, because the swearing-in came after it.

Both legs pay zero, and the trader loses the full $977.44 from the example above. Read both rule sets line by line; the article on resolution and oracles lists what to check.

Leg risk

If one order fills and the other does not, you hold a one-sided bet. Prices may move before you can complete the second leg, especially on thin order books. Checking depth on both sides before trading and sizing to the smaller book reduce this risk.

Settlement and platform risk

  • The two venues may settle on different days, so money from one leg is stuck while you wait for the other.
  • Platforms can pause markets, change rules or fail. On crypto venues, stablecoin depegs and smart contract bugs are extra risks.
  • A disputed resolution on one venue can delay or change its payout while the other has already settled.

Access and legality

Many traders cannot legally use both venues in a pair, because of country or state restrictions. Getting around a geoblock to complete an arbitrage usually breaches the platform's terms and can lead to frozen funds. The platforms comparison and regulation article cover access in general terms.

Competition

Automated traders scan for these gaps continuously. By the time a gap is visible to a person refreshing two web pages, it has usually closed or shrunk below the fee threshold.

Near-arbitrage and correlated markets

Some trades look like arbitrage but are bets on a relationship. Examples include "Candidate X wins" against "Party Y wins," or a market on a monthly figure against a market on a quarterly figure. These can move together most of the time and diverge in exactly the cases that matter. Treat them as statistical relationships, not locked profits.

Common mistakes

  • Calculating gross instead of net. Always subtract fees on both legs, plus deposit, withdrawal and transaction costs.
  • Trusting matching titles. Compare full rules, deadlines and sources.
  • Ignoring the annualized return. A small locked gain over a long period can be worse than holding cash.
  • Sizing past the thinner book. One leg fills, the other does not, and you are directional.
  • Forgetting an outcome. Every possible outcome, including "Other" or a tie, must be covered.

FAQ

Is prediction market arbitrage risk-free?

No. It is only risk-free if both legs fill at the expected prices and both markets resolve under identical rules. Fees, partial fills, rule differences, platform failures and disputes can all turn an apparent arbitrage into a loss.

How does Polymarket–Kalshi arbitrage work?

When the same event trades on both platforms, a trader can buy YES on the cheaper venue and NO on the other if the two prices sum to less than $1. Profit depends on fees on both legs, the time until resolution and whether both platforms define and resolve the event the same way. Access rules may also prevent one person from using both.

What is the difference between arbitrage and hedging?

Arbitrage aims to lock in a profit regardless of outcome. Hedging reduces the risk of an existing position, usually at a cost. A cross-venue arbitrage is a hedge that happens to cost less than it pays, but a hedge with mismatched rules is neither.

How much money do you need for prediction market arbitrage?

The edge per trade is usually a few cents per contract after fees, so meaningful profit needs size, and capital is split across venues and locked until resolution. Small accounts often find that fixed costs such as withdrawals and transaction fees take most of the gain.
