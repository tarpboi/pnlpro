Prediction Market Strategies Overview

6 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Prediction Markets

Prediction market strategies are repeatable approaches to finding trades where the price differs from an outcome's true probability, or where a trader can earn from the market's structure without predicting anything. The main families are information edge, resolution sniping, arbitrage, market making, short-term crypto up/down rounds and copy trading. Each has a distinct source of profit and a distinct way of losing money, and none of them works reliably for most participants once fees, spreads and competition are counted.

Key takeaways

  • Every strategy needs an edge: better information, better speed, better pricing or a structural gap such as two prices that should match.
  • High win rates do not mean low risk; strategies that win small and often can lose large and rarely.
  • Arbitrage and market making avoid betting on outcomes but carry execution, inventory and rule risks.
  • Short crypto rounds are dominated by speed, and slow traders tend to be on the losing side.
  • Copying other wallets brings delay, slippage and selection bias that can turn a good record into a poor one.

Information edge

The classic approach is to know something the price does not yet reflect, or to process public information better.

  • Domain expertise. A meteorologist trading weather markets or an analyst who follows a country's politics closely may estimate probabilities better than generalists.
  • Faster reading of primary sources. Official releases, court filings and live results often move prices before most traders notice.
  • Models. Aggregating polls, building a simple forecast from economic data or using base rates from similar past events.

The risk is overconfidence. Most traders believe their estimates are better than the market's, and most are wrong, which is why cognitive biases matter here. Trading on material non-public information can be illegal on regulated venues and breaches most platforms' terms elsewhere.

Resolution sniping

Resolution sniping buys outcomes that look nearly certain, often at 95 to 99 cents, shortly before the market settles, aiming to collect the last few cents. The edge comes from traders who sell early to free up capital, and from thin late liquidity.

Worked example: the break-even math

You make 20 trades, each spending $100 on YES shares at 95 cents.

  • Each trade buys $100 ÷ $0.95 = 105.26 shares.
  • A win pays 105.26 × $1.00 = $105.26, a profit of $5.26.
  • A loss pays $0, a loss of $100.

If 19 of 20 win, profit is 19 × $5.26 = $99.94, and the one loss costs $100.00. The net result is about −$0.06, essentially zero, before fees. At a 95 cent entry, you break even at a 95 percent win rate and need to beat it, after fees, to profit. One extra loss in 20 trades turns the run into a loss of about $105.

The main risks are surprises and rule technicalities. The resolution and oracles article explains how these happen, and the blog guide on resolution sniping covers the strategy in more depth.

Arbitrage

Arbitrage looks for prices that cannot all be right at once:

  • Complementary outcomes. If YES and NO on the same market can be bought for less than $1 combined, or all outcomes of a multi-outcome market for less than $1, the set pays $1 for sure.
  • Cross-venue. The same event trades at different prices on two platforms.

These trades do not depend on who wins, but they are not riskless. Fees, partial fills, capital tied up until resolution and differences in how two platforms word and resolve a question can erase or reverse the profit. See prediction market arbitrage for worked examples and the blog guide on Kalshi–Polymarket arbitrage.

Market making

A market maker posts both a bid and an ask and aims to earn the spread between them as other traders buy and sell. Some platforms offer liquidity rewards for resting orders near the midpoint.

The two main risks are inventory, which builds up when the market moves one way, and adverse selection, which means being filled mostly by traders who know more. Both get worse near news and near resolution. The article on market making on prediction markets covers the textbook view, and the blog guide on prediction market making discusses automation.

Short-term crypto up/down rounds

Several platforms run recurring rounds asking whether a coin will be higher or lower at the end of a short window. On Predict.fun, 5-minute and 15-minute rounds settle on a Chainlink price stream. The fair value of "Up" changes second by second with the underlying price and the time left, so these markets reward traders who can price them quickly.

Speed is the dominant factor. Traders reacting manually tend to buy after the move has already happened, from faster participants. Fees, the spread and small differences between the settlement feed and exchange prices also matter. The rhythm of a new round every few minutes can feel like gambling; if it starts to, step back, and know that support for problem gambling exists in most countries. The blog guide on BTC up/down markets covers this niche.

Copy trading

On crypto platforms, trades are recorded on a public blockchain, so anyone can see what a given wallet bought. Copy trading follows wallets with strong records.

Problems include:

  • Delay and slippage. By the time you copy, the price has often moved.
  • Survivorship bias. The wallets that look best are partly the lucky ones out of many.
  • Hidden context. A trader may be hedging elsewhere, or market making, so a single trade is not a recommendation.
  • Traps. A wallet may build a following and then trade against its copiers.

See copy and social trading for how to evaluate track records, and the blog guide on Polymarket copy trading.

Choosing and testing a strategy

  1. Name your edge in one sentence. If you cannot, you probably do not have one.
  2. Do the fee math first. Many apparent edges disappear after costs; see prediction market pricing.
  3. Size small and evenly. The Kelly criterion and position sizing explain why oversizing ruins good strategies.
  4. Test before scaling. Paper trade or use small stakes, and keep a record of every trade.
  5. Review honestly. Compare your results against simply holding cash, and stop strategies that do not work.

Automated approaches need their own safeguards; see building trading bots.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing win rate with profitability. A 95 percent win rate at 95 cent prices is break-even.
  • Chasing every apparent arbitrage. Many gaps exist because the two contracts are not actually the same.
  • Copying blindly. A wallet's past profit says little about the next trade you copy late.
  • Trading short rounds by hand. You are usually slower than the other side.
  • Ignoring capital lockup. Money stuck until a distant resolution date has an opportunity cost.

FAQ

What is the best strategy for prediction markets?

There is no single best strategy. Each one depends on a specific edge, such as better information, faster execution or structural price gaps, and most traders do not have a durable edge after fees. Testing at small size is the only way to find out whether an approach works for you.

Is resolution sniping risk-free?

No. It wins often but loses the full stake when a near-certain outcome fails or resolves on a technicality. At a 95 cent entry price, you need to win more than 95 percent of the time just to break even.

Can you copy trade on Polymarket?

Because Polymarket trades settle on a public blockchain, anyone can view a wallet's activity and follow it. Copying brings delay, slippage and selection bias, and some wallets may deliberately exploit followers, so past results are a weak guide.

Do you need a bot to trade prediction markets?

Not for slower strategies such as information-based trading. Speed-sensitive approaches such as short crypto rounds, market making and arbitrage are largely automated, and manual traders tend to be at a disadvantage in them.
