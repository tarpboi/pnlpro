Prediction Market Pricing and Implied Probability

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Prediction Markets

In a prediction market, the price of a contract that pays $1 if an event happens can be read as the market's estimate of that event's probability: 25 cents means about a 25 percent chance. The reading is only approximate, because spreads, fees, the time value of money and systematic biases all push prices away from true odds. Knowing how to adjust for these is the basis of every trading decision on these platforms, from simple speculation to arbitrage.

Key takeaways

  • A contract's price divided by its $1 payout gives the implied probability, before costs.
  • YES and NO prices on the same binary market are complements that should add up to about $1.
  • When the prices of all outcomes add up to more than $1, the excess is the overround, and dividing by the total removes it.
  • Fees, spreads and the cost of locking up money until resolution raise the probability you need to break even.
  • Long shots tend to be overpriced in betting and prediction markets, so a cheap price is not automatically a bargain.

Price as implied probability

A binary contract pays $1 if the outcome is YES and $0 otherwise. If you pay price P and the true probability is q, your expected profit per contract, before fees, is:

Expected profit = q × $1 − P

The price at which expected profit is zero is P = q. That is why price and probability line up: if traders thought the chance was higher than the price, they would buy until it rose, and vice versa.

In practice there is no single price. There is a best bid (the highest price someone will pay) and a best ask (the lowest price someone will sell at). The midpoint between them is the usual "headline" probability. If YES is bid at 60 cents and offered at 62 cents, the midpoint is 61 cents, or 61 percent. A buyer pays 62 cents and a seller receives 60 cents, so the spread is a real cost.

YES and NO are complements

Because exactly one side of a binary market pays $1, one YES share plus one NO share is always worth $1 at resolution. That means:

  • Buying NO at 40 cents is economically the same as selling YES at 60 cents.
  • A YES bid of 60 cents implies a NO ask of 40 cents, and a YES ask of 62 cents implies a NO bid of 38 cents.

Many platforms show both books but match them together, so the two always mirror each other. If you ever see YES ask plus NO ask below $1 on the same market, buying both locks in a profit before fees. That rarely lasts, and the arbitrage article explains why.

Overround in multi-outcome markets

In a market with several outcomes, the YES asks across all outcomes usually add up to slightly more than $1. The excess is called the overround, or vig in betting language. It is the cost of buying every outcome at once.

Worked example: removing the overround

A three-candidate race shows these YES asks:

Candidate YES ask Normalized probability
A $0.52 0.52 ÷ 1.04 = 50.0%
B $0.31 0.31 ÷ 1.04 = 29.8%
C $0.21 0.21 ÷ 1.04 = 20.2%
Total $1.04 100.0%

The asks sum to $1.04, an overround of 4 percent. Dividing each price by $1.04 gives probabilities that sum to 100 percent. This simple proportional method is common, though it slightly overstates long shots if the overround is not spread evenly.

Converting between odds formats

Sportsbooks and betting exchanges quote odds instead of prices. The conversions use implied probability p:

Format From probability Example at p = 0.25 Example at p = 0.60
Price (cents) 100 × p 25 cents 60 cents
Decimal odds 1 ÷ p 4.00 1.67
Fractional odds (1 − p) ÷ p 3/1 2/3
American odds If p < 0.5: +100 × (1 − p) ÷ p; if p ≥ 0.5: −100 × p ÷ (1 − p) +300 −150

To go the other way, decimal odds of 4.00 give p = 1 ÷ 4.00 = 0.25. American odds of −150 give p = 150 ÷ (150 + 100) = 0.60, and +300 gives p = 100 ÷ (300 + 100) = 0.25.

A typical sportsbook line of −110 on both sides of a game implies 110 ÷ 210 = 52.4 percent for each side. The two sides sum to 104.8 percent, a built-in margin of about 4.8 percent. Comparing that with a prediction market's midpoint is a quick way to see where prices disagree.

How fees change fair value

Fees raise the probability you need for a trade to break even. The shape of the fee matters:

  • Flat percentage of trade value. A 1 percent taker fee on a 50 cent purchase costs half a cent per share.
  • Probability-shaped fees. Some regulated venues, Kalshi among them, have used a taker fee proportional to P × (1 − P), which is largest near 50 cents and smallest near the extremes. With a coefficient of 0.07, the fee at 50 cents is 0.07 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 1.75 cents per contract, and at 90 cents it is 0.07 × 0.9 × 0.1 = 0.63 cents.
  • No trading fee. Some markets charge nothing to trade but may charge for deposits, withdrawals or blockchain transactions.

Fee schedules change and differ by market type, so check each platform's current schedule.

Worked example: break-even probability

You buy YES at 50 cents and pay a 1.75 cent fee. Your all-in cost is 51.75 cents, so you need the event to happen more than 51.75 percent of the time to profit on average. If you also plan to sell before resolution, you pay the spread and possibly a second fee, raising the bar further. Read more about cost drag in trading costs and fees.

Time value: why near-certain contracts trade below $1

Money tied up in a contract cannot earn interest elsewhere. For a long-dated market, even a near-certain outcome trades at a discount.

Worked example

An outcome has a 98 percent chance of happening, and the market resolves in six months. If cash can earn 4 percent a year elsewhere, six months of interest is about 2 percent. A rough fair price is:

0.98 ÷ 1.02 = 0.961, or about 96 cents.

A trader who buys at 96 cents and is right earns 4 cents, or about 4.2 percent over six months. Weighted by the 2 percent chance of losing everything, the expected return is about 0.98 ÷ 0.96 − 1 = 2.1 percent, roughly what cash would have earned with no risk at all. This is one reason "free money" at 95 cents is often not free. The blog guide on resolution sniping discusses the short-dated version of this trade.

The long-shot bias

Studies of horse racing and other betting markets going back decades have found that long shots tend to be overpriced and favorites slightly underpriced. Researchers have reported similar patterns in some prediction markets. Proposed reasons include:

  • People enjoy the small chance of a big payoff and overpay for it.
  • Traders overweight small probabilities, a pattern described in behavioral economics.
  • Selling a long shot (buying NO at 97 cents) ties up a lot of capital for a small gain, so fewer traders do it.
  • A minimum price tick of 1 cent means an outcome with a true 0.3 percent chance cannot trade below 1 cent.

The practical point is that a contract at 3 cents does not mean a 3 percent chance. The true figure may be lower. Being aware of this is part of avoiding cognitive biases.

Putting it together: edge and sizing

Your edge is the gap between your probability estimate and your all-in cost:

Edge per contract = your probability − (price + fees + expected spread cost)

If you think an outcome has a 40 percent chance, the ask is 33 cents and the fee is 1 cent, your estimated edge is 0.40 − 0.34 = 6 cents per contract. On a $1 payout, that is a good-looking number, but it depends entirely on your 40 percent being better than the market's 33 percent. Most traders overestimate their edge. The Kelly criterion gives a framework for sizing such bets, and risk/reward and expectancy covers the general math.

Common mistakes

  • Using the last trade as the price. The last trade may be stale. Look at the current bid, ask and depth.
  • Ignoring the spread on cheap contracts. A 1 cent spread on a 4 cent contract is a 25 percent round-trip cost.
  • Forgetting the overround. Reading raw prices in a multi-outcome market overstates every outcome a little.
  • Assuming long shots are value. The historical evidence points the other way on average.
  • Leaving out time value. A long-dated 95 cent contract may pay less than a savings account after accounting for risk.

FAQ

How do you calculate implied probability from a prediction market price?

Divide the price by the payout. A YES share costing 35 cents on a $1 payout implies 35 percent. In a multi-outcome market, divide each price by the sum of all prices to remove the overround.

Why do YES and NO prices add up to more than $1?

Because you buy at the ask on both sides, and each ask sits above the midpoint. The gap is the bid-ask spread. At the midpoints, YES and NO add up to about $1.

How do I convert American odds to a probability?

For negative odds, divide the odds by the odds plus 100: −150 becomes 150 ÷ 250 = 60 percent. For positive odds, divide 100 by the odds plus 100: +300 becomes 100 ÷ 400 = 25 percent.

What is the long-shot bias?

It is the tendency for unlikely outcomes to trade at prices above their true probability, seen in horse racing and many betting markets. It suggests that cheap contracts are often worse value than they look.
