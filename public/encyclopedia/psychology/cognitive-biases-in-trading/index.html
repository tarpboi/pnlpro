Cognitive Biases in Trading

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Trading Psychology
  • Trading Psychology Basics
  • Cognitive Biases
  • FOMO & Revenge Trading
  • Trading Journal
  • Discipline & Routine
  • Handling Losses & Tilt

Cognitive biases are systematic errors in thinking that push people toward predictable mistakes, especially when decisions are fast, uncertain and emotional. In trading they show up as holding losers too long, selling winners too early, trading too much and seeing patterns that are not there. Much of this was documented by behavioral finance research, starting with the work of Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in the 1970s. Knowing the biases does not make you immune, but it lets you design rules that work around them.

Key takeaways

  • Biases are built into human judgment; the practical defense is rules and records, not willpower.
  • Loss aversion and the disposition effect together explain why many traders cut winners short and let losers run.
  • Overconfidence leads to overtrading and oversizing, both of which cost money.
  • Recency bias, the gambler's fallacy and hindsight bias distort how traders read their own results.
  • A trading journal and pre-set exit rules are the most effective general countermeasures.

Loss aversion

Loss aversion is the tendency to feel a loss more strongly than a gain of the same size. In Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory, losses weigh roughly twice as heavily as equal gains in many experiments.

In trading: a trader sees a position approach the stop and moves the stop further away, because taking the loss feels worse than the risk of a bigger one. Or they avoid entering valid trades after a few losses.

Countermeasure: set hard stop orders at entry, and size positions so each loss is small enough to accept calmly. See stop-loss strategies.

The disposition effect

The disposition effect is the tendency to sell winning positions too early and hold losing ones too long. Hersh Shefrin and Meir Statman named it in 1985, and Terrance Odean's study of brokerage accounts found that investors realized gains much more readily than losses, and that the winners they sold tended to go on to outperform the losers they kept.

In trading: the effect directly damages expectancy. Closing winners at +0.5R while letting losers run to −2R can make a strategy with a high win rate lose money overall. Risk/reward and expectancy shows how.

Countermeasure: define exits before entry, and review trades by their R multiple rather than by whether they were "green".

Confirmation bias

Confirmation bias is the habit of seeking, noticing and remembering information that supports what you already believe, while discounting what contradicts it.

In trading: once in a long position, a trader reads bullish posts, dismisses negative news and interprets ambiguous price action as strength. Social media feeds that echo existing views make this worse.

Countermeasure: before entering, write down what would prove the trade wrong, and set the stop there. Actively look for the best argument against the trade.

Overconfidence

Overconfidence is overestimating your skill, the accuracy of your information or your ability to predict outcomes. Brad Barber and Terrance Odean's research on US brokerage accounts linked it to excessive trading: the most active traders earned lower returns after costs. Their related work found that men traded considerably more than women and that the extra trading reduced their net returns.

In trading: overconfidence shows up as trading too often, sizing too large after a winning streak and believing one's edge is larger than the evidence supports. In position sizing it is especially costly, because overestimating your win rate leads directly to overbetting. See Kelly criterion.

Countermeasure: use fixed position sizing rules and compare your actual journal statistics with your beliefs.

Recency bias

Recency bias is giving too much weight to recent events and too little to the longer record.

In trading: after three losses a trader abandons a strategy that has worked over hundreds of trades. After a strong week they assume the next week will be the same and increase size.

Countermeasure: decide in advance how many trades are needed before judging a strategy, and judge it over that sample. Knowing how common losing streaks are helps; see drawdown and risk of ruin.

Anchoring

Anchoring is relying too heavily on a reference number, often the first one encountered, when making later judgments.

In trading: the most common anchor is your own entry price. A trader who bought at $50 and now sees $40 waits to get "back to breakeven", although the market does not know or care about their entry. Getting from $40 back to $50 needs a 25 percent rise. Other anchors include all-time highs ("it was $100 last year, so $60 is cheap") and round numbers.

Countermeasure: ask: "If I had no position, would I buy this here, today, with this stop?" If not, the position is being held for the anchor, not the analysis.

Sunk cost fallacy

The sunk cost fallacy is letting money or effort already spent influence decisions that should depend only on the future.

In trading: "I've already lost $800 on this, so I can't sell now," or adding to a losing trade to lower the average price. It also appears outside individual trades: sticking with an expensive course, tool or strategy because of what it cost.

Countermeasure: evaluate every open position on current information. The money already lost is gone whether you hold or sell.

Gambler's fallacy

The gambler's fallacy is believing that a random process is "due" to reverse after a streak, such as expecting heads after several tails.

In trading: after five losses a trader feels a win is overdue and doubles the size. If trade outcomes are roughly independent, the next trade's odds are unchanged. Doubling after every loss, known as a martingale, is especially dangerous.

Worked example. A trader risks $100 and doubles after each loss. After five straight losses they have lost $100 + $200 + $400 + $800 + $1,600 = $3,100, and the sixth bet risks $3,200. If it wins at 1:1, the net profit for the whole sequence is only $100. If it loses, the total loss is $6,300. Five-loss streaks are common over a few hundred trades, so this approach risks large losses for small gains.

The opposite error, the "hot hand" belief that a winning streak will continue, also leads to oversizing.

Countermeasure: use fixed fractional sizing, which naturally reduces size after losses rather than increasing it. See position sizing.

Hindsight bias

Hindsight bias is the feeling, after an outcome is known, that it was predictable all along. Psychologist Baruch Fischhoff documented it in the 1970s.

In trading: reviewing a chart after the fact, the breakout looks obvious and the trader concludes they "should have known". This inflates confidence in pattern-reading and leads to self-blame for missing trades that were genuinely uncertain at the time.

Countermeasure: record your analysis and expectations before the outcome, in a trading journal, and compare them with what happened.

Why knowing isn't enough

Studies of biases consistently find that people who know about them still show them, especially under time pressure and emotion. The effective defenses are structural:

  • Pre-commitment: entry, stop, target and size decided before the trade.
  • Records: a journal that captures your reasoning and emotional state at the time.
  • Fixed rules: sizing formulas and daily loss limits that don't depend on how you feel.
  • Automation: where practical, orders placed in advance or executed by a rule-based system.
  • Review: a regular, data-based look at your behavior. See trading psychology basics.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming you are less biased than other traders, which is itself a form of overconfidence.
  • Recognizing a bias after the trade and changing nothing in the rules.
  • Over-correcting, for example refusing to ever take quick profits even when the plan calls for it.
  • Blaming biases for a strategy that simply has no edge.

FAQ

What is the most common bias in trading?

Loss aversion and the related disposition effect are among the most common and costly. They lead traders to hold losing positions and close winning ones early, which shrinks average wins and enlarges average losses.

What is the disposition effect in trading?

It is the tendency to sell investments that have gained in value too soon while holding on to those that have lost value. Research on brokerage accounts has found that the winners investors sold tended to outperform the losers they kept.

How do I avoid confirmation bias when trading?

Before entering, write down what would prove the trade wrong and place your stop there. Seek out the strongest argument against your position, and avoid information sources that only repeat your view.

Is the gambler's fallacy relevant to trading?

Yes. Traders often feel a win is due after several losses and raise their size. If trade outcomes are roughly independent, past results do not change the odds of the next trade, so increasing size after losses only increases risk.
