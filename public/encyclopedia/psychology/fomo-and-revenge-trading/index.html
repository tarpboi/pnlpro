FOMO and Revenge Trading

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Trading Psychology
  • Trading Psychology Basics
  • Cognitive Biases
  • FOMO & Revenge Trading
  • Trading Journal
  • Discipline & Routine
  • Handling Losses & Tilt

FOMO trading means entering a trade because you are afraid of missing a move, rather than because your setup is present. Revenge trading means taking trades, often larger and faster than usual, to win back money you just lost. Both replace the trading plan with an emotional impulse, and both tend to happen in clusters that can do more damage in an hour than weeks of normal losses.

Key takeaways

  • FOMO usually leads to late entries with wide stops, poor reward-to-risk and buying near short-term tops.
  • Revenge trading usually follows a loss and involves larger size, lower-quality setups and faster decisions.
  • Common triggers include social media, sharp price moves, watching others profit, and losses or near-misses.
  • The most effective defenses are rules set in advance: a daily loss limit, a maximum number of trades, and a fixed sizing formula.
  • If the urge to win back losses feels compulsive, treat it as a warning sign and consider talking to someone.

What FOMO trading looks like

FOMO, the fear of missing out, appears when a market is moving fast and you are not in it. The thought is "this is going without me", and the action is entering immediately at whatever price is available.

Typical signs:

  • Entering after a large move has already happened, without a setup.
  • Using a market order because waiting feels unbearable.
  • Not knowing where the stop should go.
  • Entering a market you don't normally trade because it is trending on social media.

Worked example: the cost of chasing

A stock is consolidating with a breakout level at $20.00. Your plan is to buy the breakout with a stop at $19.50, risking $0.50 per share. With a $100 risk budget, that is 200 shares.

You miss the breakout. The stock runs to $26.00, and FOMO kicks in. If the logical stop is still below the breakout, at $19.50, the risk per share is now $6.50, thirteen times larger.

  • Keeping 200 shares would risk 200 × $6.50 = $1,300, thirteen times your budget.
  • Keeping the $100 budget allows only 15 shares, and the trade has to rise another $6.50 just to make 1R.

Either way, the trade is far worse than the one you planned. A fast move also often retraces part of its gain soon after, which is exactly when late buyers tend to be stopped out.

What revenge trading looks like

Revenge trading follows a loss, or a run of losses, and is driven by the need to get back to even. It often comes with anger at the market, a sense of being cheated or a fixation on the daily P&L number.

Typical signs:

  • Entering a new trade within moments of a stop-out.
  • Doubling the size "to make it back faster".
  • Trading setups you would normally skip.
  • Removing or widening stops.
  • Continuing after hitting your daily loss limit.

Worked example: how fast it escalates

A trader with a $10,000 account normally risks $100 per trade (1 percent) and has a 3 percent daily loss limit.

  1. Three losing trades: −$300. Account: $9,700. The daily limit says stop here.
  2. Instead, the trader risks 3 percent "to make it back": $291. It loses. Account: $9,409.
  3. Frustrated, they risk 5 percent: about $470. It loses. Account: about $8,939.

The day's loss is now about $1,061, or 10.6 percent, more than three times the planned limit, and the account needs an 11.9 percent gain just to return to $10,000. Had the trader stopped at step 1, the loss would have been 3 percent, needing a 3.1 percent recovery. The math of recovery is covered in drawdown and risk of ruin.

Triggers

Trigger Why it works Usually leads to
Social media posts of big wins Creates a sense that everyone is profiting except you; posts are a biased sample of winners FOMO entries, oversizing
Large candles or sudden spikes Fast movement grabs attention and creates urgency Chasing
Price alerts and push notifications Interrupts your plan with someone else's move Impulse trades
A recent loss The pain of loss drives the urge to undo it Revenge trades
A near-miss Watching a trade you skipped run to target FOMO on the next setup
Being "up" for the day, then giving some back Anchoring to the day's high-water mark Revenge trades to restore it
Boredom Wanting action when no setup exists Low-quality trades

Many of these are connected to the biases in cognitive biases in trading, especially loss aversion and anchoring.

Social media deserves a special note. Screenshots of large gains are selected precisely because they are unusual. You rarely see the losses, the account size or the many people who tried the same thing and lost. Some posts are paid promotions or outright fabrications. See trading education and gurus.

Rules that stop FOMO

  1. No setup, no trade. If the entry conditions in your trading plan are not met, you don't enter, regardless of how fast the market is moving.
  2. Use limit orders at planned prices. Pre-placed orders remove the need to decide in the moment.
  3. Set a maximum distance from the setup. For example: "no entry more than 0.5 ATR beyond the breakout level". If price is past it, the trade is missed.
  4. Keep a "missed trades" list. Write down setups you missed instead of chasing them. Reviewing them later is useful; chasing them is not.
  5. Curate your information. Mute accounts and channels that mainly post gains, and turn off notifications for markets you don't trade.
  6. Remember there is always another trade. Markets produce new setups every day. Missing one move costs nothing.

Rules that stop revenge trading

  1. A hard daily loss limit. When it is hit, the trading day is over. Some platforms let you set this as an automatic lock.
  2. A maximum number of trades per day, which limits the damage from rapid-fire re-entries.
  3. A cooling-off period after each loss, such as 15 minutes away from the screen before the next trade.
  4. A fixed sizing formula that cannot be changed during the session. Size only goes up at a scheduled review, never to recover a loss. See position sizing.
  5. A "two losses, take a break" rule. After two consecutive losses, stand up and walk away for a set time.
  6. Stop looking at the P&L number during the session. Some traders hide it and watch only R multiples or price.
  7. Write before you trade. If you want to trade after a loss, write down the setup and why it fits the plan. If you can't, you don't trade.

Automation helps here too. Orders placed in advance, broker-side loss limits and automated systems all reduce the number of decisions you make while upset. See discipline and routine.

After an episode

If you do revenge trade or chase, the useful response is to record it, not to punish yourself or try to win it back.

  • Log what triggered it, what you did and what it cost.
  • Look for patterns: time of day, market, prior result, how much sleep you had.
  • Add or tighten a rule that addresses the trigger.
  • Take the rest of the day off.

The longer process of recovering from a large loss is covered in handling losses and tilt.

When it might be more than a habit

Regular urges to chase losses, trading with money set aside for other things, hiding trading from family, or feeling unable to stop are warning signs of a gambling problem rather than a trading-discipline issue. Help is available in most countries through national gambling helplines, support groups such as Gamblers Anonymous, and health professionals, and reaching out early makes a difference.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a successful revenge trade as proof the approach works. It reinforces a habit that eventually leads to a large loss.
  • Setting a daily loss limit but allowing "one more" trade.
  • Raising size to recover faster after a drawdown.
  • Watching markets you don't trade, which creates FOMO without any plan to act on it properly.
  • Relying on willpower alone instead of rules and automated limits.

FAQ

What is revenge trading?

Revenge trading is taking impulsive trades, often with larger size, to quickly win back money just lost. It usually follows a loss or losing streak and tends to make losses much larger, because it replaces planned setups and sizing with emotion.

How do I stop revenge trading?

Set a hard daily loss limit and stop when it is hit, cap the number of trades per day, take a fixed break after each loss, and never change your position size during a session. Writing down why a trade fits your plan before entering also helps.

What is FOMO in trading?

FOMO, the fear of missing out, is the urge to enter a trade because a market is moving without you, rather than because your setup is present. It typically leads to late entries, wide stops and poor reward-to-risk.

Is it ever okay to chase a trade?

Most trading plans treat chasing as a rule violation, because late entries usually have much worse risk and reward than the planned entry. Some traders allow re-entry on a defined pullback or a new setup, which is different from chasing because it follows written rules.
