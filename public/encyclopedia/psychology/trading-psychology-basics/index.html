Trading Psychology Basics

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Trading Psychology
  • Trading Psychology Basics
  • Cognitive Biases
  • FOMO & Revenge Trading
  • Trading Journal
  • Discipline & Routine
  • Handling Losses & Tilt

Trading psychology is the study of how emotions, habits and thinking patterns affect a trader's decisions. Two traders with the same strategy can get very different results because one follows the rules and the other hesitates, chases, cuts winners early or lets losers run. Psychology does not create an edge, but poor psychology can destroy one, and it is often the difference between what a strategy could earn and what the trader actually earns.

Key takeaways

  • Fear and greed show up as specific behaviors, such as skipping valid trades, exiting winners early, overtrading and oversizing.
  • Every trade outcome is uncertain, so a good decision can lose and a bad decision can win.
  • Judging trades by whether you followed your process, rather than by whether they made money, leads to better learning.
  • Thinking in probabilities means expecting losing streaks and evaluating results over many trades, not one.
  • Psychology matters most for discretionary traders, but systematic traders face it too when they are tempted to override their rules.

Why psychology matters

Most trading decisions are made under three conditions that the human brain handles poorly: uncertainty, real money at stake and fast-changing information. People are generally better at avoiding immediate pain than at following a plan whose payoff only appears over hundreds of decisions.

Research on individual investors supports this. In a well-known series of studies, Brad Barber and Terrance Odean found that the US households who traded most actively earned noticeably lower returns after costs than those who traded least, a pattern they linked to overconfidence. Terrance Odean also found that investors were more likely to sell winning stocks than losing ones, and that the winners they sold went on to outperform the losers they kept. Behaviors like these are covered in cognitive biases in trading.

The broader evidence that most retail traders lose money, discussed in what is trading, has many causes, including costs and a lack of edge. Psychology is one of them, and it is the one the trader controls most directly.

Fear and greed

"Fear and greed" is shorthand for the two emotional pulls that distort trading decisions. They are easier to manage when you can name the specific behavior each produces.

Emotion Typical behaviors Cost
Fear of losing Skipping valid setups after a loss; stops so tight they are hit by noise; refusing to take a planned loss in the hope it comes back Missed winners; small losses turning into large ones
Fear of missing out Chasing a move after it has already run; entering without a setup Poor entries; buying near tops
Fear of giving back profit Closing winners far too early Average win shrinks; expectancy falls
Greed Oversizing; adding to winners without a plan; removing targets Large drawdowns when the trade reverses
Need to be right Holding losers; averaging down; arguing with the market One loss can erase many wins

FOMO and revenge trading covers two of the most damaging patterns in detail.

Fear after losses, greed after wins

Emotions follow results. After a run of losses, traders often become hesitant, skip their best setups and then watch those setups work without them. After a run of wins, they often feel invincible, raise their size and loosen their rules, just before the next normal losing streak. Both reactions respond to recent outcomes, which in a probabilistic game are largely noise.

Living with uncertainty

No trading method knows what the next trade will do. Even strong strategies win only part of the time. That means:

  • A good decision can lose. You followed every rule and got stopped out.
  • A bad decision can win. You broke your rules on an impulse and made money.

The second case is more dangerous than the first, because it rewards and reinforces bad habits.

Worked example: what a positive edge feels like

Imagine a strategy that wins 40 percent of the time, with winners of 2R and losers of 1R. R is the amount risked per trade. Its expectancy is 0.40 × 2 − 0.60 × 1 = +0.2R per trade, a genuinely profitable system before costs. Here is how ten-trade stretches of this strategy are distributed:

Winners out of 10 Result Chance
0 −10R 0.6%
1 −7R 4.0%
2 −4R 12.1%
3 −1R 21.5%
4 +2R 25.1%
5 +5R 20.1%
6 +8R 11.1%
7 or more +11R or better 5.5%

About 38 percent of all ten-trade stretches lose money, and about one in six loses 4R or more, even though the strategy has an edge. A trader who judges the system, or themselves, after any single ten-trade stretch will often reach the wrong conclusion. Risk/reward and expectancy explains these numbers further.

Process vs outcome thinking

Outcome thinking judges a decision by its result. Process thinking judges it by whether it was a sound decision given what was known at the time. Poker players call the error of judging decisions purely by results "resulting", a term popularized by the former professional poker player Annie Duke.

A simple grid shows why the distinction matters:

Good outcome Bad outcome
Good process Earned result: repeat it Bad luck: repeat it anyway
Bad process Lucky: do not repeat it Deserved loss: fix the process

Only the process column is under your control. If you review only by outcome, you will drop good habits after unlucky losses and keep bad habits after lucky wins.

Practical ways to build process thinking:

  • Grade each trade on rule-following, for example from A to F, separately from its profit or loss.
  • Track a "rules followed" percentage alongside P&L in your trading journal.
  • Set process goals, such as "take every valid setup and no others this week", rather than profit goals.

The probabilistic mindset

A probabilistic mindset treats each trade as one sample from a long series rather than a test of skill or a verdict on your worth. Its core beliefs are:

  1. Any single trade can win or lose, regardless of how good the setup looks.
  2. A strategy's edge shows up only over many trades.
  3. Losing streaks are a normal part of any strategy and can be estimated in advance.
  4. The job is to execute the edge consistently and size so that bad streaks are survivable.

Casinos and insurers work this way: they accept that individual outcomes are random and manage the aggregate. Traders who adopt the same view tend to react less to individual losses, which makes rule-following easier. Sizing small enough that no single trade matters emotionally helps a great deal; see position sizing and drawdown and risk of ruin.

A probabilistic mindset does not mean accepting any losses at all. If your results stay below expectations over a large sample, that is evidence your edge may be smaller than you thought, or missing.

Discretionary vs systematic traders

Discretionary traders make each decision by judgment: they read the chart, the news and the order flow and decide in real time. Psychology affects every step: which setups they see, how they size, when they exit. For them, psychology is often the main constraint on performance.

Systematic traders follow fixed rules, often automated. Their in-trade decisions are mostly removed, but psychology still enters at key points: turning the system off during a drawdown, tweaking it after a few losses, overriding signals that feel wrong, or raising size after a good month. Many systematic strategies fail not because the rules stop working but because the trader stops following them.

Automation can be a useful discipline tool, as covered in discipline and routine, but it shifts the psychological challenge rather than removing it.

Building better habits

  • Write a plan before trading. Decisions made in advance are less emotional. See trading plan.
  • Reduce size until losses feel boring. Emotional pressure grows with the amount at risk.
  • Set hard limits, such as a daily loss limit, that stop trading before emotions take over. See handling losses and tilt.
  • Keep a journal that includes your emotional state, so patterns become visible.
  • Look after the basics: sleep, food and breaks affect decision quality more than most traders admit.
  • Review weekly, not trade by trade, to avoid overreacting to noise.

Common mistakes

  • Judging a strategy by the last few trades.
  • Treating a profitable rule break as a success, which reinforces the behavior.
  • Trading larger size than you can emotionally tolerate, which makes every other psychological problem worse.
  • Believing willpower alone is enough, instead of building rules and limits that work when willpower fails.
  • Blaming psychology for a strategy with no edge. Perfect discipline cannot make a losing system profitable.

FAQ

What is trading psychology?

Trading psychology is how emotions, biases and habits affect trading decisions, such as when to enter, exit and how much to risk. It covers fear, greed, discipline, reactions to losses and the ability to follow a plan under uncertainty.

How do I control fear and greed when trading?

Name the specific behaviors they cause, then build rules that prevent them: a written plan, fixed position sizing, hard stops and a daily loss limit. Trading smaller size also reduces emotional pressure, making the rules easier to follow.

What does it mean to think in probabilities?

It means treating each trade as one of many, accepting that any single trade can lose, and judging your strategy and yourself over a large number of trades. It helps traders stay consistent through normal losing streaks.

Is trading psychology more important than strategy?

Both are necessary. Without an edge, good psychology only slows losses. With an edge, poor psychology can still produce losses through rule breaks, oversizing and abandoning the strategy during normal drawdowns.
