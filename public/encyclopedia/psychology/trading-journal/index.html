Trading Journal

6 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Trading Psychology
  • Trading Psychology Basics
  • Cognitive Biases
  • FOMO & Revenge Trading
  • Trading Journal
  • Discipline & Routine
  • Handling Losses & Tilt

A trading journal is a record of every trade you take, along with the reasoning, the execution and the result. Over time it becomes the main source of evidence about what is working, what isn't, and which mistakes cost the most. Without a journal, traders tend to remember their best trades, forget their worst ones and judge their performance by feel.

Key takeaways

  • Record every trade, including the plan, the actual execution, the result in R and your state of mind.
  • Tags for setups and mistakes turn a list of trades into data you can sort and analyze.
  • A weekly review, done the same way each time, is where the journal pays off.
  • Key metrics include win rate, average win and loss, expectancy, profit factor and maximum drawdown.
  • The most useful finding is often how much rule breaks cost compared with the strategy itself.

Why keep a journal

Memory is a poor record of trading. Hindsight bias makes past decisions look more obvious than they were, and recency bias gives the last few trades too much weight. A written record captures what you knew and felt at the time, which is the only fair basis for judging a decision. See cognitive biases in trading.

A journal also answers practical questions:

  • Is the strategy profitable, after costs, over a meaningful sample?
  • Which setups make money and which don't?
  • Which mistakes recur, and what do they cost?
  • Are results better at certain times of day, in certain markets or in certain moods?

What to record

Before the trade

  • Date, time and market
  • Setup name (from your trading plan)
  • Direction (long or short)
  • Planned entry, stop and target
  • Planned risk in money and the resulting position size
  • Reason for the trade in one or two sentences
  • Screenshot of the chart at entry
  • Emotional state and energy level, for example on a 1 to 5 scale

After the trade

  • Actual entry and exit prices and times
  • Fees and other costs
  • Result in money and in R (profit or loss divided by initial risk)
  • Exit reason: stop, target, trailing stop, time stop, discretionary
  • Whether the plan was followed (yes or no, and what was different)
  • Mistake tags, if any
  • Screenshot at exit
  • One lesson, if there is one

Optional advanced fields

  • MAE (maximum adverse excursion): how far the trade moved against you before closing. Helps judge whether stops are too tight or too loose.
  • MFE (maximum favorable excursion): how far it moved in your favor. Helps judge whether targets and trailing stops leave too much on the table.
  • Market context: trend, volatility, news.

Tagging setups and mistakes

Tags are what make a journal analyzable. Keep the list short and consistent.

Setup tags describe the trade type: breakout, pullback, range fade, news, and so on.

Mistake tags describe rule breaks:

  • Entered without a setup
  • Entered late (chased)
  • Moved stop
  • Oversized
  • Exited early without a rule
  • Traded after daily loss limit
  • Revenge trade

Context tags can capture conditions: high-volatility day, trading tired, after a big win, pre-news.

Over time you can sort by tag and see, for example, that "range fade" trades lose money, or that trades tagged "tired" have a much lower win rate.

Metrics to compute

Metric Formula What it tells you
Win rate Winners ÷ total trades How often trades win
Average win / average loss Gross profit ÷ winners; gross loss ÷ losers The payoff ratio
Expectancy Net profit ÷ number of trades (or average R) Average result per trade
Profit factor Gross profit ÷ gross loss Above 1.0 means net profitable over the sample
Maximum drawdown Largest peak-to-trough fall in equity Worst pain so far
Rule adherence Trades with plan followed ÷ total trades Discipline
Cost of mistakes Net result of trades with mistake tags What rule breaks cost

Expectancy and the relationship between win rate and payoff are explained in risk/reward and expectancy. Drawdown is covered in drawdown and risk of ruin.

Worked example: reviewing 20 trades

A trader risks $100 per trade (1R = $100) and reviews the last 20 trades.

  • 9 winners, totaling +$1,620 (average +$180, or +1.8R)
  • 11 losers, totaling −$1,045 (average −$95, or −0.95R)

The metrics:

  • Net profit: $1,620 − $1,045 = $575
  • Win rate: 9 ÷ 20 = 45%
  • Expectancy: $575 ÷ 20 = $28.75 per trade, or about +0.29R
  • Check with the formula: 0.45 × $180 − 0.55 × $95 = $81.00 − $52.25 = $28.75
  • Profit factor: $1,620 ÷ $1,045 ≈ 1.55

Now the tags:

Tag Trades Net result
Setup: breakout 12 +$720
Setup: pullback 8 −$145
Mistake: moved stop 2 −$260
Mistake: entered late 3 −$190

The five trades with mistake tags lost $450 combined. The other 15 trades made $575 + $450 = $1,025. In this sample, rule breaks cost the trader nearly half of what the rules themselves earned. The pullback setup is also negative, which is worth watching, although eight trades is far too few to drop it.

Twenty trades is a small sample, and the figures above could change a lot over the next 20. The value is in spotting patterns to watch, not in drawing firm conclusions.

Weekly review process

A consistent review takes 30 to 60 minutes. One workable sequence:

  1. Check completeness. Every trade logged, screenshots attached, tags applied.
  2. Update metrics for the week and for the running total.
  3. Review rule adherence. List every trade where the plan was not followed and what it cost.
  4. Review the best and worst trade by R and by process grade.
  5. Look at mistake and context tags for recurring patterns.
  6. Pick one thing to improve next week, written as a specific behavior, such as "no entries more than 0.5 ATR past the breakout level".
  7. Note any proposed plan changes for the monthly review rather than changing rules mid-week.

The routine around this is covered in discipline and routine.

Common mistakes

  • Logging only some trades, usually skipping the embarrassing ones, which ruins the data.
  • Recording P&L but not the plan, so you can't tell good losses from bad ones.
  • Too many fields, which makes journaling a chore that gets abandoned.
  • Never reviewing, which turns the journal into an archive rather than a tool.
  • Drawing conclusions from a handful of trades.
  • Leaving out costs, which can make a losing strategy look profitable.

FAQ

What should I write in a trading journal?

At minimum: date, market, setup, direction, planned entry, stop and target, position size, actual entry and exit, costs, result in money and R, whether you followed the plan, and a note on your emotional state. Screenshots and mistake tags make reviews far more useful.

Is a spreadsheet good enough for a trading journal?

Yes. A spreadsheet with consistent columns and tags is enough for most traders and lets you compute every key metric. Dedicated journal software can save time by importing trades automatically, but the habit of reviewing matters more than the tool.

How often should I review my trading journal?

Many traders do a short daily log and a structured weekly review, plus a monthly look at whether the trading plan needs changes. The weekly review is usually where patterns and costly mistakes become visible.

What metrics should I track as a trader?

Win rate, average win and average loss, expectancy, profit factor, maximum drawdown and the percentage of trades where you followed your plan. Tracking the net result of trades with mistake tags shows what rule breaks are costing you.
