Writing a Trading Plan

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

A trading plan is a written set of rules that defines what you trade, when you trade it, how you enter and exit, how much you risk and how you review your results. It turns trading from a series of in-the-moment decisions into a repeatable process that can be measured and improved. The plan does not make a strategy profitable, but without one it is almost impossible to tell whether losses come from the strategy or from not following it.

Key takeaways

  • A trading plan should be specific enough that another person could follow it and make the same decisions.
  • The core sections are markets, setups, entry rules, exit rules, position sizing, risk limits, routine and review.
  • Risk limits, such as risk per trade, daily loss limit and maximum drawdown, should be written as numbers, not intentions.
  • A plan is only useful if you track whether you followed it, which is what a trading journal is for.
  • Change the plan on a set schedule based on evidence, not in the middle of a trading session.

Why write it down

Decisions made under pressure tend to be worse than decisions made in advance. When a position is moving against you, it is tempting to widen the stop, add to the loser or take a trade that doesn't fit. A written plan sets the rules while you are calm and gives you something concrete to check your behavior against.

A plan also makes results interpretable. If you follow consistent rules for 100 trades, the results tell you something about the strategy. If every trade was handled differently, the results tell you very little.

What goes in a trading plan

1. Goals and constraints

  • Why you are trading and what you realistically expect. Given the evidence that most retail traders lose money, a reasonable first goal is often to follow the plan consistently and lose little while learning, not to reach a profit target.
  • Capital allocated to trading, which should be money you can afford to lose.
  • Time available, which shapes the trading style you can realistically use.

2. Markets and instruments

List exactly what you trade: for example, "the 20 most liquid US large-cap stocks", "EUR/USD and GBP/USD" or "BTC and ETH perpetuals". Note any you avoid, such as low-priced stocks or newly launched tokens. Fewer markets usually means better knowledge of how each behaves.

3. Trading hours

When you trade and when you don't. For example: "first 90 minutes after the US open only" or "no new positions after 20:00 UTC". Include rules for major scheduled events. See market hours and sessions and news trading.

4. Setups

Describe each setup you trade with objective conditions. "A strong-looking chart" is not a setup. "Price above the 50-day moving average, pulling back to the 20-day on below-average volume" is. Limit yourself to one or two setups at first.

5. Entry rules

What exactly triggers an entry: the order type, the price, and any confirmation required. For example: "buy stop 0.1 percent above the high of the first candle that closes back above the 20-day average". Order types covers the options.

6. Exit rules

  • Stop-loss: where it goes and why. See stop-loss strategies.
  • Take-profit: a fixed R target, a structure level, a trailing stop or a combination.
  • Time stop: how long you will hold a trade that goes nowhere.
  • Event exits: whether you close before earnings, weekends or major data releases.

7. Position sizing

The formula you use, written out. For example: "risk 0.5 percent of current equity per trade; shares = risk amount ÷ (entry − stop), rounded down". See position sizing.

8. Risk limits

Limit Example
Risk per trade 0.5% of equity
Maximum daily loss 1.5% of equity (3 losing trades), then stop for the day
Maximum weekly loss 3% of equity, then stop until next week
Maximum open risk 2% across all positions
Correlated positions Count as one; maximum 1% combined
Drawdown rule At 10% from peak, halve size; at 15%, stop and review
Maximum leverage Your own cap, below the broker's

The reasoning behind each limit is covered in risk management fundamentals.

9. Routine

What you do before, during and after each session: reviewing the news calendar, marking levels, checking open positions, and logging trades. The discipline and routine article covers this in depth.

10. Rules for not trading

Conditions under which you don't trade at all: after hitting the daily loss limit, when tired or ill, after a large personal upset, or during events your strategy is not designed for.

11. Record keeping and review

What you record for each trade and when you review. For example: "log every trade the same day; review all trades every weekend; review the plan itself once a month". See trading journal.

Worked example: a one-page plan

Here is a condensed plan for a hypothetical part-time stock swing trader with a $15,000 account.

Markets: US stocks with average daily volume above 1 million shares and price above $20.

Setup: stock in an uptrend (above its rising 50-day average) pulls back to the 20-day average; enter when it closes back above the prior day's high.

Entry: buy at the open after the signal day.

Stop: just below the pullback low.

Target: sell half at 2R; trail the rest under the 20-day average.

Time stop: exit if the trade has not reached 1R within 10 trading days.

Events: no holding through earnings.

Sizing: risk 0.5 percent of equity per trade. At $15,000, that is $75. For an entry at $30.00 with a stop at $29.25, the risk per share is $0.75, so the position is $75 ÷ $0.75 = 100 shares, worth $3,000.

Limits:

  • Maximum open risk: 4 trades, or $300 (2 percent).
  • Weekly loss limit: $450 (3 percent); no new trades until the next week.
  • Drawdown: at $1,500 below the account's peak (10 percent), cut risk to 0.25 percent per trade; at $2,250 (15 percent), stop trading and review.

Routine: scan for setups on Sunday and each evening; place orders before the open; check positions once at midday and once after the close.

Review: every Saturday, log rule-following for each trade, compute win rate, average R and expectancy, and note any rule breaks.

None of these numbers is special. The point is that each rule is concrete and checkable.

Template outline

You can copy this outline and fill in each line:

  1. Purpose and expectations: why I trade; realistic goals for the next 3 months
  2. Capital: amount allocated; maximum I am willing to lose before stopping entirely
  3. Markets: instruments I trade; instruments I avoid
  4. Hours: when I trade; events I avoid
  5. Setups: name, conditions, chart example for each
  6. Entry: trigger and order type for each setup
  7. Exits: stop rule, target rule, trailing rule, time stop, event exits
  8. Sizing: formula, risk percentage, rounding
  9. Risk limits: per trade, daily, weekly, open risk, correlation, drawdown, leverage
  10. Routine: pre-session, during session, post-session
  11. No-trade rules: conditions under which I don't trade
  12. Journal: fields recorded for every trade
  13. Review: weekly trade review; monthly plan review; criteria for changing the plan

Testing and changing the plan

Before committing real money, test the setups against historical data or in a paper account, then trade them at small size. Backtesting explains how to test rules without fooling yourself. Change the plan only at scheduled reviews and only based on a meaningful number of trades. Changing rules after every loss makes it impossible to know what works.

Common mistakes

  • Vague rules, such as "enter on strength" or "cut losers quickly", that can be interpreted differently each time.
  • No numbers on risk limits, so they bend when tested.
  • Too many setups and markets at the start.
  • Writing the plan and never measuring adherence, so it becomes a document nobody reads.
  • Rewriting the plan during a losing streak instead of at a scheduled review.
  • Copying someone else's plan without testing whether it suits your time, capital and temperament.

FAQ

What should a trading plan include?

At minimum: the markets you trade, your setups, entry and exit rules, how you size positions, risk limits such as risk per trade and maximum daily loss, your routine and how you review results. Each rule should be specific enough to check whether you followed it.

How long should a trading plan be?

Many effective plans fit on one to three pages. Length matters less than clarity: a short plan with precise rules is more useful than a long one full of general intentions.

How often should I update my trading plan?

Most traders review the plan on a fixed schedule, such as monthly or quarterly, using journal data from a reasonable number of trades. Changing it after every loss makes it impossible to tell which rules work.

Is a trading plan the same as a trading strategy?

No. A strategy is the method for finding and taking trades. A trading plan includes the strategy but adds position sizing, risk limits, routine, record keeping and review, which together govern how the strategy is executed.
