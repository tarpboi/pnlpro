Risk/Reward and Expectancy

6 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Expectancy is the average amount a trading strategy makes or loses per trade over many trades. It combines two numbers that are often discussed separately: how often trades win, and how large winners are compared with losers. A strategy with positive expectancy can still lose for long stretches, but one with negative expectancy loses over time no matter how often it wins.

Key takeaways

  • An R multiple expresses a trade's result as a multiple of the amount risked; risking $200 and making $500 is a +2.5R trade.
  • Expectancy = (win rate × average win) − (loss rate × average loss).
  • The break-even win rate for a given reward-to-risk ratio R is 1 ÷ (1 + R), before costs.
  • A high win rate means nothing on its own; it has to be judged together with the size of wins and losses.
  • Costs reduce expectancy on every trade and raise the win rate needed to break even.

R multiples

R is the initial risk on a trade: the amount you lose if the stop-loss is hit. Expressing results in R makes trades of different sizes comparable.

  • You risk $200 and the trade makes $500: +2.5R
  • You risk $200 and get stopped out for $200: −1R
  • You risk $200 and the price gaps through your stop for a $300 loss: −1.5R

The reward-to-risk ratio of a planned trade is the distance to the target divided by the distance to the stop. A trade with a stop $2 away and a target $6 away has a planned ratio of 3:1, often written as 3R. People also write it the other way around, as a "1:3 risk-reward". Both mean the potential gain is three times the potential loss.

Planned R and realized R differ. Targets are not always reached, stops slip, and many trades are closed early. Expectancy should be calculated from realized results, which is one reason to keep a trading journal.

The expectancy formula

In money terms:

Expectancy = (W × average win) − ((1 − W) × average loss)

where W is the win rate as a decimal.

In R terms, if the average loss is about 1R:

Expectancy (R) = (W × average win in R) − (1 − W)

Expectancy in R tells you the average return per unit of risk. Multiply it by your dollar risk per trade, and then by the number of trades, to get the expected result over a period, before considering variation.

Example: a low win rate that makes money

A strategy wins 40 percent of the time. Winners average 2.5R and losers average 1R.

  • Expectancy = 0.40 × 2.5 − 0.60 × 1 = 1.0 − 0.6 = +0.4R per trade

If 1R is $100, the strategy makes $40 per trade on average. Over 100 trades, the expected result is about $4,000, although the actual result could be noticeably higher or lower.

Why a high win rate can still lose

Consider two systems over 100 trades.

System A System B
Win rate 70% 35%
Average win $100 $300
Average loss $300 $100
Expectancy per trade 0.70 × 100 − 0.30 × 300 = −$20 0.35 × 300 − 0.65 × 100 = +$40
Expected result over 100 trades −$2,000 +$4,000

System A wins seven trades out of ten and loses money. System B loses nearly two trades out of three and makes money. System A feels better to trade most days, which is exactly why strategies like it are common.

High-win-rate, negative-expectancy patterns often come from:

  • Taking profits quickly and letting losers run. This is the disposition effect, described in cognitive biases in trading.
  • Selling options or other tail risk for small, frequent gains, with occasional large losses.
  • Averaging down or martingale sizing, which turns most losing trades into small wins until one becomes a very large loss.
  • Buying near-certain outcomes. In a prediction market, buying YES at $0.90 wins $0.10 and loses $0.90. The break-even win rate is 90 percent before fees, so a 90 percent win rate only breaks even. See prediction market pricing.

Break-even win rate table

For a strategy whose winners average R times its losers, the win rate needed to break even before costs is:

Break-even win rate = 1 ÷ (1 + R)

Average win ÷ average loss (R) Break-even win rate
0.33 (risk 3 to make 1) 75.0%
0.5 66.7%
1.0 50.0%
1.5 40.0%
2.0 33.3%
3.0 25.0%
4.0 20.0%

A strategy is only profitable if its actual win rate is above the break-even figure for its actual payoff ratio. A 45 percent win rate is profitable at 1.5R and losing at 1R.

How costs move the break-even point

Costs, such as spreads, commissions, fees, funding and slippage, make every winner smaller and every loser larger. If round-trip costs average c, measured in R, the break-even win rate becomes:

Break-even win rate with costs = (1 + c) ÷ (1 + R)

For a 2R strategy with costs of 0.1R per trade, the break-even win rate rises from 33.3 percent to 1.1 ÷ 3 = 36.7 percent. For strategies with small targets, such as scalping, costs can be a large fraction of R and can turn a profitable-looking system into a losing one. Trading costs and fees works through this in detail.

Related measures

  • Profit factor = gross profit ÷ gross loss. Above 1.0 means the strategy made money over the sample. For System B above, profit factor = (0.35 × 300) ÷ (0.65 × 100) = 105 ÷ 65 ≈ 1.62.
  • Average R per trade is the same as expectancy in R, calculated from journal data.
  • Win rate and payoff ratio are the two ingredients; neither is meaningful alone.

Expectancy is an average, not a promise

A positive expectancy describes the long run. Over short samples, results vary a lot. A strategy that wins 40 percent of the time at 2R has an expectancy of +0.2R per trade, yet over any ten trades it finishes negative about 38 percent of the time, because three or fewer winners out of ten is common. That variation is why sizing is kept small, as covered in position sizing and drawdown and risk of ruin.

Estimates of expectancy also need a reasonable sample, often a hundred trades or more, and they can be badly inflated by backtests that were fitted to past data.

Common mistakes

  • Judging a strategy by win rate alone. Without the payoff ratio, a win rate says nothing about profitability.
  • Using planned R instead of realized R. Targets that are rarely hit make planned ratios look better than reality.
  • Ignoring costs, which shift the break-even point, sometimes by a lot.
  • Drawing conclusions from 10 or 20 trades. Small samples are dominated by luck.
  • Cutting winners early to "lock in" a win, which lowers the average win and can make expectancy negative.

FAQ

What is a good risk-reward ratio for trading?

There is no single good ratio, because it depends on the win rate. A 1:1 ratio can be profitable with a win rate above 50 percent, and a 3:1 ratio can be profitable with a win rate above 25 percent, before costs. What matters is the combination, measured by expectancy.

How do you calculate trading expectancy?

Multiply the win rate by the average win, then subtract the loss rate multiplied by the average loss. For example, 0.40 × $250 − 0.60 × $100 = $40 per trade. Use realized results from a decent number of trades, after costs.

What win rate do I need to be profitable?

Before costs, the break-even win rate is 1 divided by (1 + R), where R is your average win divided by your average loss. At 2R you need more than 33.3 percent; at 1R you need more than 50 percent. Costs push these figures higher.

What is an R multiple?

An R multiple is a trade's profit or loss divided by the amount initially risked. If you risk $100 and make $300, the trade is +3R. Using R makes it easy to compare trades and strategies that use different position sizes.
