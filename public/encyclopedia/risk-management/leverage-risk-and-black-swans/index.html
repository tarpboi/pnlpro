Leverage Risk and Black Swans

8 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Risk & Money Management

A black swan is a rare, extreme event that most market participants did not expect and that has an outsized impact, a term popularized by Nassim Nicholas Taleb in his 2007 book of the same name. Leverage is what turns such events from painful into account-ending, because it multiplies losses and leaves little room before a forced exit. The episodes below show the recurring ways traders get wiped out: prices gapping past stops, liquidity disappearing, and the broker or exchange itself failing.

Key takeaways

  • Market returns have fat tails: very large moves happen far more often than a normal distribution predicts.
  • At high leverage, an ordinary-sized adverse move can wipe out the margin, and an extreme one can leave the trader owing money.
  • In a crisis, stops can fill far from their price, liquidity can vanish and correlations can jump toward one.
  • Counterparty failure, as in the FTX collapse, can cause total losses that have nothing to do with the trader's market view.
  • The main defenses are modest leverage, position sizes that survive gaps, defined-risk instruments and limits on capital held at any one venue.

Why tails matter

Many risk models assume returns follow a bell curve, where moves of five or more standard deviations should be almost impossible. Real markets produce them repeatedly. On October 19, 1987, the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell about 22.6 percent in a single day. Currency pegs break, commodities hit prices nobody had modeled, and crypto assets can lose almost all their value in days.

How leverage turns a bad day into ruin

Leverage multiplies both the position's exposure and the damage from a move. The adverse move that wipes out the posted margin, before fees and maintenance margin, is roughly 1 ÷ leverage:

Leverage Adverse move that wipes out the margin
2x 50%
5x 20%
10x 10%
20x 5%
50x 2%
100x 1%

In practice, liquidation happens sooner, because exchanges and brokers close positions when equity falls to a maintenance level. The mechanics are in leverage and margin and, for crypto, crypto liquidations.

Hypothetical example. A trader with $10,000 holds a currency position worth 20 times the account. The stop is 1 percent away, so the planned loss is about 20 percent of the account, already large. Then a surprise policy decision moves the price 15 percent before any order can be filled. The loss is 20 × 15% = 300 percent of the account. The trader has lost the $10,000 deposit and, without negative balance protection, may owe the broker another $20,000.

Case studies

2015: the Swiss franc unpeg

In September 2011 the Swiss National Bank (SNB) set a minimum exchange rate of 1.20 francs per euro and defended it for more than three years. Many traders treated the floor as near-certain and bought EUR/CHF close to it with high leverage, expecting little downside.

On January 15, 2015, the SNB abandoned the floor without warning. EUR/CHF fell from about 1.20 to well below 0.90 within minutes before settling near parity. There were almost no buyers on the way down, so stop orders filled far below their prices, or in a gap. Many retail clients ended with negative balances. FXCM, a large US retail broker, needed a $300 million emergency loan from Leucadia, and Alpari UK went into insolvency.

Lessons: pegs and policy floors can end suddenly; a "can't lose" trade at high leverage is dangerous; and a stop is not a guaranteed price. EU and UK rules introduced from 2018 onward now require negative balance protection for retail CFD clients, but protections vary by country and product, so check the current rules where you trade. See CFDs and spread betting.

2020: oil futures below zero

As pandemic lockdowns collapsed fuel demand in early 2020, crude oil storage filled up, especially at Cushing, Oklahoma, the delivery point for WTI futures. On April 20, 2020, the day before it expired, the May 2020 WTI futures contract settled at −$37.63 a barrel. Holders of long contracts who could not take physical delivery had to pay others to take the contracts off their hands.

Some retail investors, including buyers of an oil-linked product sold by Bank of China, suffered heavy losses, and some retail platforms struggled because their systems were not built for negative prices.

Lessons: futures have expiry and delivery mechanics that can dominate price near expiry; "it can't go below zero" is not a safe assumption for every instrument; and products that roll futures can behave very differently from the spot price. See contango, backwardation and rollover.

2021: the GameStop short squeeze

GameStop was one of the most heavily shorted US stocks, with reported short interest above 100 percent of the shares available to trade. In January 2021, heavy buying by retail traders, much of it discussed on online forums, together with options activity and short covering, drove the share price from under $20 at the start of the month to an intraday high of $483 on January 28 (prices before the company's 2022 stock split). Melvin Capital, a hedge fund with a large short position, suffered heavy losses and received a $2.75 billion capital injection from Citadel and Point72.

On January 28, Robinhood and several other brokers restricted purchases of GameStop and some other stocks after clearinghouse deposit requirements jumped. Many buyers near the top took large losses as the price fell back.

Lessons: a short position has theoretically unlimited loss; crowded trades can unwind violently in either direction; and brokers can change what you are allowed to trade in the middle of an event. See short selling.

2022: the LUNA and UST collapse

TerraUSD (UST) was an algorithmic stablecoin meant to hold $1 through a mint-and-burn mechanism with its sister token, LUNA, rather than through cash reserves. Demand was boosted by a lending protocol on the same network that offered deposit yields of around 20 percent.

In May 2022, large withdrawals and sales of UST pushed it below its peg. The mechanism created vast amounts of new LUNA as holders redeemed, and LUNA fell from around $80 to fractions of a cent within about a week. Tens of billions of dollars of market value disappeared. The losses spread to crypto lenders and funds, and Three Arrows Capital, Celsius and Voyager all collapsed in the following months. The project's co-founder later faced criminal charges.

Lessons: yields that seem too good usually carry hidden risk; a "stable" asset is only as stable as what backs it; and contagion spreads through lenders and leveraged funds. See stablecoins.

2022: the FTX collapse

FTX was one of the largest crypto exchanges in the world. In early November 2022, a news report raised questions about the balance sheet of Alameda Research, a trading firm owned by FTX's founder. A rival exchange then announced it would sell its holdings of FTX's token. Customers rushed to withdraw, FTX halted withdrawals, and it filed for bankruptcy on November 11, 2022. Customer deposits had been used by Alameda. Founder Sam Bankman-Fried was convicted of fraud in 2023 and sentenced to 25 years in prison in 2024.

Customers lost access to their funds overnight, regardless of how well their trades had gone, and any recovery came through a long bankruptcy process.

Lessons: counterparty risk is real even at large, well-known venues; exchange tokens and in-house trading firms create conflicts of interest; and money held on an exchange is a loan to that exchange. See CEX vs DEX.

Other examples worth knowing

  • 1998, Long-Term Capital Management: a highly leveraged hedge fund rescued by a bank consortium organized by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York.
  • 2018, "Volmageddon": a volatility spike destroyed some short-volatility products in a day. See volatility and VIX.
  • 2021, Archegos Capital: a family office with leveraged positions held through swaps defaulted on margin calls; its banks lost more than $10 billion combined.
  • August 2024, the yen carry unwind: a sharp yen rally forced leveraged carry positions to close. See carry trade.

Recurring mechanisms

Mechanism What happens Example
Gap risk Price jumps past stops with no trading in between Franc unpeg, earnings gaps
Liquidity vanishing Buyers or sellers disappear; spreads widen enormously Franc unpeg, small caps in a panic
Correlation spike Diversified positions fall together March 2020
Forced selling Margin calls and liquidations push prices further Archegos, crypto liquidation cascades
Counterparty failure Broker, exchange or issuer cannot pay FTX, Alpari UK
Rule changes Trading restrictions or product changes mid-event GameStop buying restrictions
Model failure "Impossible" prices occur Negative oil

Lessons for retail traders

  1. Use far less leverage than the maximum available. The maximum is the broker's limit, not a recommendation.
  2. Size for the gap, not just the stop. Ask what happens if price opens 10 or 20 percent beyond your stop.
  3. Prefer defined risk around binary events, such as options spreads, or simply stay flat.
  4. Know whether your account can go below zero.
  5. Limit venue exposure, and withdraw profits you don't need for trading.
  6. Watch total exposure across correlated positions. See correlation and portfolio risk.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a long period of calm as proof of safety. Tail risk is invisible until it isn't.
  • Selling tail risk for small, steady gains without sizing for the rare large loss.
  • Trusting "can't lose" trades and high fixed yields. Pegs, floors and stable yields have broken repeatedly.
  • Adding leverage after a winning streak, just before conditions change.

FAQ

What is a black swan event in trading?

It is a rare, extreme and largely unexpected event with a major impact on prices, such as the 2015 Swiss franc unpeg or the 2020 negative oil price. The term was popularized by Nassim Nicholas Taleb. Such events tend to look obvious only in hindsight.

Can you lose more than your deposit when trading with leverage?

Yes, in some products and jurisdictions. If price gaps past your stop by more than your margin covers, the account can go negative and you may owe the broker. Many regulators, including those in the EU and UK, require negative balance protection for retail CFD clients, but rules differ by country and product.

Why did oil prices go negative in 2020?

Demand collapsed during pandemic lockdowns and storage near the WTI delivery point was almost full. Just before the May 2020 contract expired, holders who could not take delivery paid others to take their contracts, and the price settled at −$37.63 a barrel on April 20, 2020.

How can traders protect themselves from black swans?

There is no complete protection, but low leverage, position sizes that survive large gaps, defined-risk instruments such as options, avoiding concentrated bets on single events and limiting money held at any one broker or exchange all reduce the damage.
