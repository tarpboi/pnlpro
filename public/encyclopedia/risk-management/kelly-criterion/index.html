Kelly Criterion

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

The Kelly criterion is a formula for choosing what fraction of your capital to bet on an opportunity with a positive edge so that your wealth grows as fast as possible over the long run. It was published by John L. Kelly Jr. of Bell Labs in 1956 and later applied to blackjack and investing by Edward Thorp. It is widely respected as a theoretical guide, but in trading it is very sensitive to errors in your estimate of the edge, which is why most practitioners bet only a fraction of what it recommends.

Key takeaways

  • For a simple win-or-lose bet, the Kelly fraction is f* = p − q ÷ b, where p is the win probability, q = 1 − p and b is the net payout per unit staked.
  • For a binary contract bought at price c that you believe has true probability p, the Kelly fraction simplifies to (p − c) ÷ (1 − c).
  • Betting more than the Kelly fraction lowers long-run growth, and betting about twice Kelly can reduce it to roughly zero or below.
  • Half Kelly keeps about three quarters of the growth rate with much smaller drawdowns.
  • Kelly is only as good as your probability estimate; overestimating your edge leads directly to overbetting.

What Kelly maximizes

Kelly sizing maximizes the expected logarithm of wealth, which is equivalent to maximizing the long-run compound growth rate. It does not maximize expected profit on the next bet. A strategy that maximized expected profit would bet everything on every favorable opportunity and would eventually go broke after a single loss.

The key properties are:

  • With no edge, the Kelly fraction is zero: don't bet.
  • The larger the edge relative to the payout, the more you bet.
  • Kelly never bets the whole bankroll unless a win is certain, so a fixed-fraction Kelly bettor never quite reaches zero in theory.

The formula for binary bets

For a bet that either wins b units per unit staked or loses the stake:

f = p − q ÷ b*

or equivalently f = (b × p − q) ÷ b*, which can be read as "expected profit per unit staked, divided by the payout odds".

  • p = probability of winning
  • q = 1 − p = probability of losing
  • b = net odds (profit per $1 risked if you win)

Example 1: even-money bet

You have a bet that pays 1 to 1 (b = 1) and wins 60 percent of the time.

f* = 0.60 − 0.40 ÷ 1 = 0.20, so bet 20 percent of the bankroll each time.

Here is how the long-run growth rate per bet changes with the fraction bet:

Fraction bet Long-run growth per bet (approx.)
5% +0.9%
10% (half Kelly) +1.5%
20% (full Kelly) +2.0%
30% +1.5%
40% (double Kelly) −0.2%

The growth rate here is the expected log growth, 0.6 × ln(1 + f) + 0.4 × ln(1 − f), which approximates the typical compound rate per bet. Two things stand out. Half Kelly gives up only about a quarter of the growth. And double Kelly, with the same 60 percent edge, produces slightly negative growth: overbetting can turn a winning edge into a losing outcome.

Kelly for prediction-market contracts

On a binary event contract that pays $1 if the event happens, buying YES at price c means risking c to win 1 − c. The net odds are b = (1 − c) ÷ c. Substituting into the formula gives a simple result:

f = (p − c) ÷ (1 − c)*

where p is your estimate of the true probability.

Example 2: buying YES

A contract trades at $0.40. After careful research you believe the true probability is 55 percent.

  • b = 0.60 ÷ 0.40 = 1.5
  • f* = 0.55 − 0.45 ÷ 1.5 = 0.55 − 0.30 = 0.25
  • Check with the shortcut: (0.55 − 0.40) ÷ (1 − 0.40) = 0.15 ÷ 0.60 = 0.25

Full Kelly says stake 25 percent of the bankroll. With a $2,000 bankroll, that is $500, or 1,250 contracts at $0.40. Half Kelly would be $250, or 625 contracts.

Example 3: buying NO

The YES side trades at $0.70, so NO costs about $0.30. You believe the true probability of YES is only 60 percent, so NO has a 40 percent chance.

Applying the same shortcut to the NO contract: f* = (0.40 − 0.30) ÷ (1 − 0.30) = 0.10 ÷ 0.70 ≈ 0.143, or about 14 percent of the bankroll.

These examples ignore fees and the spread. Fees reduce the effective payout, so subtract them before calculating b, or the formula will overstate the bet. Pricing, the spread and fee effects are covered in prediction market pricing. Also note that 25 percent of a bankroll on a single event is a very large bet by most traders' standards, which leads to the main problem with Kelly.

The continuous form

For an investment with a continuous range of returns, such as a stock index, a common approximation is:

f = (μ − r) ÷ σ²*

where μ is the expected annual return, r is the risk-free rate and σ is the annual volatility (standard deviation of returns).

Example 4: a leveraged index position

Suppose you expect an asset to return 8 percent a year above the risk-free rate, with 20 percent annual volatility.

  • f* = 0.08 ÷ 0.20² = 0.08 ÷ 0.04 = 2.0

Full Kelly says hold 200 percent of your capital in the asset, using 2x leverage. Under the model's assumptions, the excess growth rate at full Kelly is (μ − r)² ÷ (2σ²) = 0.0064 ÷ 0.08 = 8 percent a year. At half Kelly (1x, no leverage) the excess growth is 6 percent, or 75 percent of the maximum, with half the volatility. At double Kelly (4x), the excess growth falls to zero.

The model assumes returns are roughly normally distributed and that the parameters are known. Real markets have fat tails and sudden gaps, which make leveraged Kelly positions riskier than the formula suggests. See leverage risk and black swans.

Why traders use fractional Kelly

Estimation error

The biggest problem is that p, or μ and σ, are estimates. In a casino game the odds are known. In trading they are guessed from data or judgment, and traders tend to overestimate their edge.

In the continuous example, suppose the true excess return is 4 percent, not 8 percent. True Kelly is then 1.0, so the trader holding 2.0 is at double Kelly, and their expected excess growth is roughly zero while still suffering large swings.

In the binary case, suppose you believe a contract priced at $0.50 has a 55 percent chance, so Kelly says bet 10 percent. If the true probability is 50 percent, you have no edge, and betting 10 percent each time produces an expected log growth of about −0.5 percent per bet. Overconfidence about edge, discussed in cognitive biases in trading, turns directly into overbetting.

Edges found by searching through many backtests are especially likely to be inflated. See overfitting and data snooping.

Drawdowns

Even with a correctly estimated edge, full Kelly is a rough ride. Under idealized continuous assumptions, a full-Kelly bettor has about a 50 percent chance of seeing the bankroll fall to half its value at some point. At half Kelly, the chance of a halving falls to about 12.5 percent. Few people can stick with a strategy through a 50 percent drawdown, and the drawdown and risk of ruin article shows how quickly recovery gets harder.

Fractional Kelly in practice

Many professional bettors and traders use a quarter to half Kelly, or simply treat the Kelly fraction as a maximum that should never be exceeded. Because the growth curve is flat near its peak and drops steeply past it, betting less than Kelly costs little growth, while betting more costs a lot. That asymmetry, together with estimation error, makes undersizing the safer mistake.

Kelly with multiple positions

The simple formulas assume one bet at a time. When several positions are open at once, especially correlated ones, applying full Kelly to each leads to far too much total exposure. Proper multi-asset Kelly sizing requires an estimate of the correlations, which adds another source of error. A practical approach is to cap total exposure and treat correlated positions as one bet. See correlation and portfolio risk.

Common mistakes

  • Plugging in an optimistic win rate. A few percentage points of overestimate can double the recommended bet.
  • Ignoring fees and spreads, which reduce the real payout and the real edge.
  • Applying full Kelly to every position at once, creating large combined exposure.
  • Using Kelly on strategies with unknown or unbounded losses, such as positions that can gap far past a stop.
  • Treating Kelly as a target rather than a ceiling.

FAQ

What is the Kelly criterion formula?

For a bet that pays b to 1 with win probability p, the Kelly fraction is f* = p − (1 − p) ÷ b. For example, a 60 percent chance at even money gives 0.60 − 0.40 = 0.20, or 20 percent of the bankroll. For continuous returns, an approximation is excess return divided by variance.

Why use half Kelly instead of full Kelly?

Half Kelly keeps roughly 75 percent of the long-run growth rate with about half the volatility and much shallower drawdowns. It also protects against the common problem of overestimating your edge, which can push a full-Kelly bettor into overbetting.

How do you use Kelly in prediction markets?

If you buy a contract at price c and believe its true probability is p, the Kelly fraction is (p − c) ÷ (1 − c). Buying at $0.40 with a 55 percent belief gives 0.15 ÷ 0.60 = 25 percent of the bankroll. Most traders use a fraction of this and adjust for fees first.

Can the Kelly criterion lose money?

Yes. Kelly maximizes long-run growth only if your probability estimates are correct. With an overestimated edge, or simply bad luck over a short period, a Kelly bettor can suffer large drawdowns or losses.
