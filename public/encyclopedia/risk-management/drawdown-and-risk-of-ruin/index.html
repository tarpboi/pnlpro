Drawdown and Risk of Ruin

8 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

A drawdown is a decline in account equity from a peak to a later low, and the maximum drawdown is the largest such decline over a period. Risk of ruin is the probability that losses become so large that the trader can no longer continue, either because the account is gone or because it has fallen below a level they set as unacceptable. Both depend heavily on position size, which is why sizing is often described as the most important risk decision.

Key takeaways

  • Maximum drawdown is measured from the highest equity point to the lowest point that follows it, not from the starting balance.
  • The gain needed to recover a drawdown is the loss divided by one minus the loss, so deep drawdowns become very hard to recover from.
  • Long losing streaks are far more common than intuition suggests, even for profitable strategies.
  • Risk of ruin falls sharply as risk per trade gets smaller, and with no edge, ruin becomes close to certain over enough trades.
  • Monte Carlo simulation shuffles or resamples trade results to show the range of drawdowns a strategy could plausibly produce.

Measuring drawdown

Drawdown is always measured from a peak. Suppose an account starts at $10,000, grows to $12,000, then falls to $9,000 before recovering.

  • Maximum drawdown = ($12,000 − $9,000) ÷ $12,000 = 25 percent
  • Measured from the starting balance, the account is only down 10 percent, but that understates the pain. The trader watched a quarter of the peak value disappear.
  • Recovery needed = $3,000 ÷ $9,000 = 33.3 percent to get back to the $12,000 peak.

Two related measures matter too:

  • Drawdown duration: how long the account stays below its previous peak. Some strategies, particularly trend-following ones, can spend many months under water.
  • Average drawdown: the typical depth of declines, which gives a sense of normal discomfort rather than the worst case.

Recovery math

Because each loss shrinks the base, the percentage gain needed to recover is always larger than the percentage lost:

Required gain = drawdown ÷ (1 − drawdown)

Drawdown Gain to recover
10% 11.1%
20% 25.0%
30% 42.9%
40% 66.7%
50% 100%
75% 300%

The curve steepens quickly beyond about 30 percent. The risk management fundamentals article covers why this asymmetry drives most risk rules.

How streaks become drawdowns

With fixed fractional sizing, each loss removes a percentage of current equity. After n straight losses at risk r per trade, equity is (1 − r)^n of where it started.

Risk per trade 5 losses 10 losses 15 losses 20 losses
0.5% 2.5% 4.9% 7.2% 9.5%
1% 4.9% 9.6% 14.0% 18.2%
2% 9.6% 18.3% 26.1% 33.2%
5% 22.6% 40.1% 53.7% 64.2%
10% 41.0% 65.1% 79.4% 87.8%

Real drawdowns usually come from a losing stretch with a few winners mixed in, not one unbroken streak, but the table shows the scale of damage that each risk level allows.

Losing streak probabilities

The chance that a particular trade starts a streak of k losses is (1 − W)^k, where W is the win rate. With a 50 percent win rate, the chance that the next five trades all lose is 0.5^5, or about 3.1 percent. That sounds rare, but a trader takes many trades, and each one is a fresh chance to start a streak.

The more useful question is: how likely is at least one streak of a given length over a run of trades? Assuming each trade is independent, the answers are:

Probability of at least one losing streak of k or more, over 100 trades

Win rate 5 losses 6 losses 8 losses 10 losses
60% 46% 21% 3.6% 0.6%
50% 81% 55% 17% 4.4%
40% 98% 87% 49% 21%

Over 200 trades at a 40 percent win rate, the chance of at least one streak of eight or more losses rises to about 75 percent, and ten or more to about 38 percent.

A trend-following strategy that wins 35 or 40 percent of the time should therefore expect streaks of eight or more losses as a normal part of its life. A trader who does not know this tends to abandon a sound strategy, or change it, at exactly the wrong time.

Real trades are often less independent than coin flips. Market regimes change, and a strategy that suits trending markets can lose repeatedly in a sideways market. That tends to make long streaks somewhat more likely than the table shows, not less.

Risk of ruin

Risk of ruin is the probability of reaching a loss level from which you cannot or will not continue. "Ruin" does not have to mean a zero balance. Many traders define it as a 50 percent drawdown, or as the loss level that would breach a prop firm's rules.

Three things drive it:

  1. Edge: the strategy's expectancy per trade. See risk/reward and expectancy.
  2. Payoff profile: how large and how frequent wins and losses are.
  3. Risk per trade: the fraction of capital exposed on each trade.

A simple formula for intuition

The classic "gambler's ruin" result applies to a simplified game: each bet wins or loses one fixed unit, with win probability p above 50 percent, and you keep betting indefinitely. If you start with N units, the probability of eventually losing them all is:

Risk of ruin = (q ÷ p)^N, where q = 1 − p

With p = 0.55, q ÷ p ≈ 0.818:

Starting units (N) Risk per bet as % of start Risk of eventual ruin
5 20% about 37%
10 10% about 13%
20 5% about 1.8%
50 2% about 0.004%

Two lessons follow. Smaller bets relative to capital cut the risk of ruin dramatically. And if p is 50 percent or less, meaning no edge, the formula breaks down because ruin becomes certain over an unlimited number of bets. Small size slows down a losing strategy; it cannot rescue it.

Real trading differs from this model in several ways, including variable payoffs, fixed fractional sizing and costs, so the numbers are illustrative. Fixed fractional sizing in theory never reaches zero, which is why it is more useful to ask about the probability of a deep drawdown.

Monte Carlo simulation

Monte Carlo simulation estimates the range of possible outcomes by generating thousands of random trade sequences consistent with a strategy's statistics. You can resample your actual trade results in random order, or generate trades from an assumed win rate and payoff. Each sequence produces its own equity curve and maximum drawdown, and together they show what is typical and what is a bad but plausible case.

As an illustration, here is a simple simulation of 200 trades from a strategy with a 45 percent win rate, winners of 1.5R and losers of 1R, a modest positive expectancy of +0.125R per trade, using fixed fractional sizing and 40,000 random runs:

Risk per trade Typical (median) max drawdown Chance of a 30%+ drawdown Chance of a 50%+ drawdown
1% about 12% under 1% close to 0%
2% about 22% about 19% under 1%
5% about 49% about 97% about 48%
10% about 79% nearly 100% about 99%

The edge is identical in every row. At 10 percent risk, most runs still finished the 200 trades with a profit, but nearly every one went through a drawdown of half the account along the way, which few people could sit through without abandoning the plan. These figures come from one set of assumptions and will vary slightly between runs. A real strategy's statistics are also estimates, and the true edge may be smaller. The Kelly criterion article explains why betting too large a fraction can turn a positive edge into poor long-run growth.

Monte Carlo results are only as good as their inputs. If the trade sample came from an overfit backtest, the simulation will be confidently wrong. It also assumes the future resembles the past, which may not hold when market conditions change.

Using this in practice

  • Set a maximum acceptable drawdown first, for example 20 percent, then choose a risk per trade that makes reaching it unlikely given your strategy's realistic losing streaks.
  • Plan for a streak longer than any you have seen. A backtest's worst streak is one draw from a distribution, not the worst case.
  • Reduce size during drawdowns. Fixed fractional sizing does this automatically. Some traders also cut risk by half after a set drawdown.
  • Define in advance what happens at each drawdown level, such as a review at 10 percent and a full stop at 20 percent. See handling losses and tilt.

Common mistakes

  • Measuring drawdown from the starting balance instead of from the peak.
  • Assuming the backtest's worst drawdown is the worst that can happen.
  • Underestimating streaks, then abandoning a strategy during a statistically normal losing run.
  • Increasing size to recover faster, which raises the chance of a much deeper drawdown.
  • Thinking small size fixes a negative edge. It only makes the decline slower.

FAQ

What is a good maximum drawdown for a trader?

It depends on the strategy and the person, but many traders aim to keep maximum drawdown below about 20 to 25 percent, because recovery gets much harder beyond that. Prop firm accounts often impose limits of around 5 to 10 percent, which require much smaller risk per trade.

How do you calculate risk of ruin?

For a simple game with fixed bets, win probability p above 50 percent and N betting units, the risk of eventual ruin is (q ÷ p) raised to the power N, where q = 1 − p. Real trading is more complex, so most traders estimate it with Monte Carlo simulation using their own trade statistics.

How many losing trades in a row is normal?

It depends on the win rate and the number of trades. Over 100 independent trades with a 50 percent win rate, there is roughly an 81 percent chance of at least one streak of five losses and about a 17 percent chance of eight. Lower win rates make long streaks much more common.

What is a Monte Carlo simulation in trading?

It is a method that generates thousands of random sequences of trades based on a strategy's win rate and payoffs, or on its actual past trades in random order. The results show the range of possible drawdowns and returns rather than a single historical path.
