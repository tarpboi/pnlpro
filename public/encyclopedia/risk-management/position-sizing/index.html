Position Sizing

8 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Position sizing is the process of deciding how many shares, lots or contracts to trade so that a losing trade costs a pre-set amount. It turns a risk rule, such as "risk 1 percent per trade", into an actual order size. Sizing is the main lever a trader controls over drawdowns, and it matters as much as choosing which trades to take.

Key takeaways

  • Position size should come from two inputs: how much you are willing to lose and how far away your stop-loss is.
  • The core formula is position size = amount at risk ÷ risk per unit, where risk per unit is the distance from entry to stop.
  • Fixed fractional sizing (risking a set percentage of equity) shrinks positions after losses and grows them after gains.
  • Leverage changes how much margin a position needs, not how much the trade risks; the risk is set by size and stop distance.
  • Always round down, and remember that spreads, fees and slippage add to the planned loss.

The basic formula

Every sizing method rests on the same relationship:

Position size = Amount at risk ÷ Risk per unit

  • Amount at risk is the money you lose if the stop is hit, for example 1 percent of a $20,000 account, or $200.
  • Risk per unit is the loss on one share, one lot or one coin between your entry price and your stop price.

The stop comes first. You decide where the trade idea is proven wrong, which sets the stop, and then you size the position to fit your risk budget. Choosing the size first and then placing the stop wherever the money runs out is backwards and tends to produce stops in arbitrary places. Stop placement is covered in stop-loss strategies.

A useful rearrangement shows how large the position will be as a share of the account:

Position as % of account = Risk % ÷ Stop distance %

Risking 1 percent with a stop 2 percent away gives a position worth 50 percent of the account. Risking 1 percent with a stop 0.5 percent away gives a position worth 200 percent of the account, which requires leverage.

Sizing methods

Fixed fractional (percent risk)

Fixed fractional sizing risks the same percentage of current equity on every trade. The well-known "1 to 2 percent rule" is a version of this. Its main property is that it adjusts automatically: after losses, the dollar risk per trade falls, which slows the decline; after gains, it rises.

Because each loss is a percentage of a smaller balance, fixed fractional sizing makes it mathematically impossible to lose the whole account through stopped-out trades alone, although gaps, slippage and leverage can still cause losses larger than planned.

Fixed dollar

Fixed dollar sizing risks the same dollar amount on every trade, for example $100, regardless of account balance. It is simple and easy to track in R multiples, where 1R equals the fixed dollar risk. The downside is that it does not shrink risk during a drawdown, so a long losing streak hurts more than with fixed fractional. Many traders use fixed dollar risk and reset the amount at set intervals, such as monthly.

Fixed units

Some traders always buy 100 shares or one contract. This is the simplest method but the weakest, because the actual risk varies with the stop distance and the volatility of each instrument. A one-contract trade can risk $50 one day and $500 the next.

Volatility-based (ATR) sizing

Volatility-based sizing sets the stop as a multiple of a volatility measure, usually the Average True Range (ATR), and then sizes the position so the dollar risk stays constant. In volatile conditions the stop is wider and the position smaller; in quiet conditions the stop is tighter and the position larger. This keeps risk roughly equal across instruments with very different price swings. ATR itself is explained in ATR and volatility indicators.

Comparison

Method How size is set Strength Weakness
Fixed fractional Set % of current equity at risk Shrinks risk in drawdowns, scales with growth Slow to recover after losses
Fixed dollar Same $ risk every trade Simple, clean R tracking Does not adapt to drawdowns
Fixed units Same number of shares or contracts Very simple Risk varies wildly trade to trade
ATR-based Stop = multiple of ATR, size to fit $ risk Normalizes risk across instruments Depends on the ATR setting chosen
Kelly-based Fraction based on estimated edge Theoretically growth-optimal Very sensitive to estimation error

The Kelly criterion is a separate approach that sizes by estimated edge; most traders who use it scale it down heavily.

Worked examples

Stocks

Account: $20,000. Risk per trade: 1 percent, so $200.

You plan to buy a stock at $50.00 with a stop at $47.50.

  • Risk per share = $50.00 − $47.50 = $2.50
  • Shares = $200 ÷ $2.50 = 80 shares
  • Position value = 80 × $50 = $4,000, or 20 percent of the account

If the stop is hit, the loss is 80 × $2.50 = $200, plus commissions and any slippage.

ATR version. Suppose the stock's 14-day ATR is $1.20 and you place the stop two ATRs below entry. The stop distance is $2.40. Shares = $200 ÷ $2.40 = 83.3, rounded down to 83 shares, a position worth $4,150. The loss at the stop is 83 × $2.40 = $199.20.

Forex lots

In forex, size is set in lots. A standard lot is 100,000 units of the base currency, a mini lot is 10,000 and a micro lot is 1,000. For pairs quoted in US dollars, such as EUR/USD, one pip (0.0001) on a standard lot is worth $10. Pip values for other pairs are covered in pips, lots and leverage.

Account: $10,000. Risk: 1 percent, so $100.

You buy EUR/USD at 1.0850 with a stop at 1.0825, which is 25 pips.

  • Risk per standard lot = 25 pips × $10 = $250
  • Lots = $100 ÷ $250 = 0.40 standard lots (four mini lots, or 40,000 units)

At 30:1 leverage, the margin needed is about 40,000 × 1.0850 ÷ 30 = $1,447. The margin does not change the risk: the trade still loses about $100 at the stop.

A yen pair. On USD/JPY, a pip is 0.01, so one pip on a standard lot is 1,000 yen. At a rate of 150.00, that is about $6.67. With a 30-pip stop, risk per standard lot is about $200, so a $100 risk budget allows 0.50 lots.

Crypto perpetual futures

Account: $5,000. Risk: 1 percent, so $50.

You go long BTC on a perpetual futures contract at $60,000 with a stop at $58,800, which is 2 percent below entry.

  • Risk per BTC = $1,200
  • Position size = $50 ÷ $1,200 = 0.0417 BTC
  • Notional value = 0.0417 × $60,000 ≈ $2,500

The position is half the account's value. At 10x leverage, it ties up about $250 of margin. At 2x, about $1,250. Either way the loss at the stop is about $50. What leverage changes is how close the liquidation price is, which must stay well beyond the stop. See perpetual futures and crypto liquidations.

Fees matter at this scale. At an example taker fee of 0.05 percent on each side, opening and closing $2,500 costs about $2.50, which is 5 percent of the $50 risk budget, before funding payments.

Binary contracts in prediction markets

On an event contract that pays $1 if an outcome happens and $0 if not, the most you can lose on a held-to-resolution position is the price paid. Buying YES at $0.40 risks $0.40 per contract. With a $100 risk budget and no plan to exit early, that is 250 contracts. If you plan to sell at $0.30 when the thesis breaks, the risk per contract is $0.10, but thin order books can make that exit unreliable. Pricing is covered in prediction market pricing.

How leverage fits in

Leverage is often confused with risk. In the crypto example, a trader who used the full 10x on the whole $5,000 account would control $50,000 of BTC. A 2 percent move to the same stop would cost $1,000, or 20 percent of the account, on a single trade.

The rule is: size the position from the stop and the risk budget first, then check that the margin required is available and that the liquidation price, if any, is comfortably beyond the stop. Treat the leverage a broker offers as a ceiling, not a target. Leverage and margin explains margin calls and liquidation.

Common mistakes

  • Sizing before choosing the stop. This leads to stops placed by budget rather than by where the trade is wrong.
  • Using maximum leverage by default. High leverage multiplies the damage from a gap or a moved stop.
  • Forgetting costs. Spreads, commissions, funding and slippage make the real loss larger than the planned one. See trading costs and fees.
  • Rounding up. Round position sizes down so the real risk never exceeds the budget.
  • Ignoring correlation. Three full-size positions in closely related markets carry roughly three times the risk of one.
  • Increasing size to win back losses. This is how disciplined sizing turns into a large drawdown.

FAQ

How do I calculate position size?

Divide the amount you are willing to lose by the distance from your entry to your stop per unit. For example, risking $200 with a $2.50 stop distance per share gives 80 shares. Then confirm you have the margin or cash for that position.

What is the 2 percent rule in position sizing?

The 2 percent rule means risking no more than 2 percent of account equity on a single trade, measured as the loss if the stop is hit. Many traders use 1 percent or less, especially while learning or when holding several positions at once.

Does higher leverage mean I am risking more?

Not by itself. Risk is set by position size and stop distance. Higher leverage lets you open a larger position with less margin, and it brings the liquidation price closer, so it makes oversized positions easier to take.

Should position size change with volatility?

Many traders think so. With volatility-based sizing, a more volatile market gets a wider stop and a smaller position, keeping the dollar risk the same. This stops one volatile instrument from dominating the account's results.
