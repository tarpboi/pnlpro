Stop-Loss Strategies

8 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

A stop-loss is a pre-set exit point that closes a trade when price moves against it by a chosen amount. Where you put the stop defines how much the trade risks, how often it gets stopped out and how large the position can be. Good stop placement is based on the point where the trade idea is proven wrong, not on how much money you feel like losing.

Key takeaways

  • A stop belongs at the price that invalidates your trade idea; position size is then adjusted to fit the risk budget.
  • Common methods are structure stops, volatility (ATR) stops, percentage stops and time stops, and each suits different strategies.
  • Stop orders do not guarantee a price: gaps and thin markets can cause fills well beyond the stop.
  • Trailing stops lock in gains as a trade moves in your favor but give back part of the move when they are hit.
  • Take-profit placement affects your win rate and average win, so it has to be judged together with the stop.

What a stop-loss does

A stop-loss caps the loss on a trade under normal conditions and removes the need to make a decision while under pressure. Mechanically, most stop-losses are stop orders: when the market trades at or through the stop price, the order becomes a market order and fills at the next available price. A stop-limit order becomes a limit order instead, which controls the price but may not fill at all in a fast market. The order types themselves are explained in order types.

Stops and sizing work together. Once the stop is set, the position size follows from the risk budget, as shown in position sizing. A wide stop means a smaller position, not more money at risk.

Where to place stops

Structure-based stops

A structure stop sits beyond a price level that should hold if the trade idea is right. For a long trade, that might be just below a recent swing low or below a support zone. For a short, just above a swing high or resistance. If price breaks that level, the reason for the trade is gone.

The practical refinement is to leave a buffer beyond the level instead of placing the stop exactly on it. Obvious levels attract clusters of orders, and price often probes slightly past them before reversing. Levels are covered in support and resistance.

Volatility (ATR) stops

An ATR stop is placed a multiple of the Average True Range away from entry, for example 1.5 or 2 times the 14-period ATR. It adapts to conditions: in volatile markets the stop is wider, in quiet ones tighter. The aim is to keep the stop outside normal random movement so that ordinary noise does not trigger it. See ATR and volatility indicators.

Percentage stops

A percentage stop is a fixed distance from entry, such as 5 percent below the purchase price. It is simple and common among longer-term stock traders. Its weakness is that it ignores both volatility and market structure: 5 percent may be noise for a small-cap stock and a large move for a utility.

Time stops

A time stop closes a trade that has not worked within a set period, for example three days for a swing trade or 30 minutes for an intraday breakout. Many setups that are going to work tend to move fairly quickly. A trade that goes nowhere ties up capital and attention, and it may also be quietly signaling that the idea was wrong.

Mental stops vs hard stops

A hard stop is an actual order resting with the broker or exchange. A mental stop is a price in your head where you intend to exit manually.

Mental stops have some uses in very thin markets, where a resting stop can be filled at a poor price by a brief spike. But they depend on discipline and on being at the screen. The common failure is hesitation: price hits the level, the trader hopes for a bounce, and a small loss becomes a large one. For most retail traders, hard stops are the safer default.

Gaps through stops

A stop-loss is not a guaranteed exit price. When a market closes at one price and opens at a very different one, a stop order fills at or near the opening price, not at the stop.

Worked example. You buy 100 shares at $100 with a stop at $95, planning to risk $500. The company reports poor earnings after the close, and the stock opens the next morning at $88. Your stop triggers at the open and fills around $88. The loss is about 100 × $12 = $1,200, more than double the plan.

Gaps happen most around earnings, economic releases, weekends and, in forex, the Sunday open. Crypto trades all week, but it can still move several percent in seconds during liquidation cascades. Ways to manage gap risk include smaller size ahead of known events, closing positions before the event, using options for defined risk and, where available, guaranteed stops. Earnings trading and gap trading cover these situations in more detail.

Guaranteed stops

Some CFD and spread-betting providers offer guaranteed stop-loss orders. These fill at exactly the stop price, even through a gap, and the provider charges a premium, often only when the stop is triggered. Terms differ widely by provider and country, and some require a minimum distance from the current price, so check the current conditions before relying on one. See CFDs and spread betting.

Stop hunting

"Stop hunting" describes price moving briefly through a level where many stops are likely to sit, such as just below a round number or a recent low, triggering them and then reversing. Part of this is mechanical. A cluster of stop orders is a pool of market orders waiting to be triggered, and large traders who need liquidity may push price toward it. Part of it is simply noise around obvious levels.

With a dealing-desk broker that takes the other side of client trades, a conflict of interest exists in principle, which is one reason to use well-regulated firms. The practical defense is the same either way: avoid the most obvious price, allow a buffer, and size so the wider stop still fits the risk budget.

Trailing stops

A trailing stop moves in your favor as the trade progresses and never moves back. It can trail by a fixed amount, a percentage, an ATR multiple, or under each new swing low or high.

Worked example. You buy a stock at $40. The ATR is $1.00 and you trail the stop at 3 ATR, or $3, below the highest price since entry. The initial stop is $37. The stock rises to $48, so the stop moves up to $45. If price then falls to $45, you exit with a gain of $5 per share. You captured most of the move but gave back $3 from the high. A tighter trail gives back less but gets hit more often by normal pullbacks.

Trailing stops suit trend-following strategies, where a few large winners pay for many small losses. They tend to perform poorly in choppy, sideways markets.

Take-profit placement

The exit on winning trades matters as much as the stop. Common approaches:

  • Fixed R multiple. Exit at a set multiple of the risk, such as 2R. Simple to test and to journal.
  • At structure. Exit just before the next resistance (for longs) or support (for shorts).
  • Partial exits. Take part of the position off at a first target and trail the rest.
  • Trailing only. No fixed target; let the trailing stop end the trade.

Closer targets raise the win rate but cut the average win. Farther targets do the opposite. Neither is better in itself. What matters is the combination, which is measured by expectancy. See risk/reward and expectancy.

Common mistakes

  • Moving the stop further away once price approaches it. This is the most common way a planned small loss becomes a large one.
  • Stops too tight for the market's volatility, so normal noise stops the trade out before the idea has a chance.
  • Choosing the stop by dollar amount instead of by where the trade is wrong.
  • Moving to breakeven too early, which often turns potential winners into scratch trades on an ordinary pullback.
  • Assuming the stop price is guaranteed and holding oversized positions through earnings, news or weekends.
  • No stop at all on leveraged positions, where a single bad move can trigger liquidation.

FAQ

Where is the best place to put a stop-loss?

There is no single best place. A common principle is to put the stop where the trade idea is clearly wrong, such as beyond a recent swing low for a long, with a small buffer and enough room for normal volatility. Then size the position so that stop distance fits your risk budget.

Is a trailing stop better than a fixed stop?

Neither is better in all cases. Trailing stops help capture large trending moves but give back part of the gain and get hit often in choppy markets. Fixed stops and targets are simpler and suit strategies that aim for a defined move.

Why did my stop-loss fill at a worse price?

A standard stop order becomes a market order once triggered and fills at the next available price. If the market gapped or moved very fast, or there were few orders near your stop, the fill can be well beyond the stop price. This is called slippage.

Should I use mental stops?

For most traders, hard stops resting with the broker are safer, because mental stops depend on discipline and on being present. Mental stops are sometimes used in very thin markets, but they fail when the trader hesitates or is away from the screen.
