Correlation and Portfolio Risk

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Correlation measures how closely two markets tend to move together, and portfolio risk is the combined risk of all positions held at the same time. A trader who risks 1 percent on each of five closely related trades has not taken five small risks; they have taken one risk of about 5 percent. Understanding correlation stops traders from building concentrated bets by accident, especially in crises, when assets that usually move independently start falling together.

Key takeaways

  • Positions that are highly correlated behave like a single larger position, so their risks should be added together.
  • Diversification lowers risk only when holdings are imperfectly correlated, and the benefit levels off as more holdings are added.
  • Correlations between risky assets tend to rise sharply during market stress, just when diversification is needed most.
  • Exposure limits by asset, sector and theme, plus a cap on total open risk, are simple ways to control correlation risk.
  • Beta converts a stock position into its equivalent exposure to the broad market, which makes hedging easier to size.

What correlation measures

The correlation coefficient runs from −1 to +1.

  • +1: the two move in lockstep in the same direction.
  • 0: no consistent linear relationship.
  • −1: they move in lockstep in opposite directions.

Correlation is estimated from past returns, usually daily or weekly, over a chosen window. It is a summary, not a law. It describes the average relationship over that window and says little about how the two will behave in an extreme move. Two assets with a correlation of 0.3 can still crash together on the same day.

Correlated positions are one big bet

A common example in forex: a trader goes long EUR/USD, long GBP/USD, long AUD/USD and short USD/CHF, risking 1 percent on each. Each looks like a separate idea. In practice, all four are bets against the US dollar. A strong US data release or a shift in Federal Reserve expectations can stop out all four at once, for a 4 percent loss on what was effectively one trade. The forces that move these pairs together are covered in intermarket analysis.

Other common hidden concentrations:

  • Several technology stocks, plus a Nasdaq-tracking ETF.
  • Several altcoins, which tend to move with Bitcoin.
  • Long stock index futures and long high-beta crypto, which have often traded as one "risk-on" position in recent years.
  • Several prediction-market positions that all depend on the same election result.

Worked example: adding risk across positions

Suppose you hold two positions, each with a typical daily swing (one standard deviation of P&L) of $100. The typical swing of the combined position is:

Combined risk = √(a² + b² + 2 × ρ × a × b)

where a and b are the individual risks and ρ is the correlation.

Correlation (ρ) Combined typical swing
+1.0 $200
+0.8 about $190
+0.5 about $173
0 about $141
−1.0 $0

At a correlation of 0.8, holding two positions gives almost no diversification: $190 instead of $200. Only at low or negative correlations does combining positions meaningfully reduce typical swings.

This formula describes ordinary day-to-day variation. For stop-loss risk, the worst case is still the sum of both stops, $200 in this example if each stop is $100 away, because correlated positions can and do hit their stops together.

The limits of diversification

Adding more holdings reduces risk, but only up to a point. For an equal-weighted portfolio of N holdings, each with the same volatility and the same average pairwise correlation, the portfolio's volatility as a share of a single holding's volatility is √(ρ + (1 − ρ) ÷ N).

Average correlation 1 holding 5 holdings 10 holdings 20 holdings Floor (very many)
0 100% 45% 32% 22% 0%
0.2 100% 60% 53% 49% 45%
0.5 100% 77% 74% 72% 71%
0.8 100% 92% 91% 90% 89%

With an average correlation of 0.5, going from 10 holdings to an unlimited number barely helps: risk falls from 74 percent to 71 percent of a single holding. The remaining risk is the common factor that all holdings share, such as the broad stock market. Diversification removes risk that is specific to individual holdings; it cannot remove risk they all have in common.

Correlations spike in crises

Correlations between risky assets are not stable. During calm periods, different stocks, sectors and asset classes often move fairly independently. In a sell-off, many investors sell at once to cut risk, meet margin calls or raise cash, and assets that seemed unrelated fall together.

Historical examples include the 2008 financial crisis, when most risky assets fell together, and March 2020, when the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic triggered sharp falls across stocks, credit, commodities and crypto. Even gold, often treated as a safe haven, fell sharply for several days in mid-March 2020 as investors sold what they could to raise cash. In 2022, Bitcoin and other crypto assets traded closely with technology stocks as interest rates rose.

The practical lesson: a portfolio that looks diversified based on calm-period correlations may behave like a single position in exactly the conditions where losses are largest. This is one of the mechanisms behind the blowups described in leverage risk and black swans.

Exposure limits

Simple rules can keep correlation risk in check without any formal portfolio math:

Limit Example rule
Total open risk ("portfolio heat") No more than 5% of equity at risk across all open stops
Per-theme limit No more than 2% risk on positions driven by the same factor (e.g. "short USD", "long AI stocks")
Per-asset-class limit No more than half of open risk in one asset class
Per-venue limit No more capital at one broker or exchange than you can afford to lose
Event limit No more than a set amount riding on any single scheduled event

When a new trade would breach a limit, the choices are to skip it, reduce its size, or close a correlated position to make room.

Hedging

A hedge is a position meant to offset part of the risk of another. Common forms:

  • Index hedge: shorting an index future or ETF against a portfolio of long stocks, leaving mostly the stock-specific performance.
  • Pairs: long one stock and short a closely related one, betting on the relative move rather than the market direction.
  • Options: buying puts to cap downside, at the cost of the premium. See options basics.
  • Currency hedges: offsetting the FX exposure of a foreign asset.

Hedges cost money, through premiums, spreads, financing or forgone gains, and they can fail when the relationship between the hedge and the position changes. An imperfect hedge can leave you losing on both sides.

Beta

Beta measures how much a stock tends to move relative to a broad market index. A beta of 1.5 means the stock has historically moved about 1.5 percent for each 1 percent move in the index, on average.

Worked example. You hold $20,000 of a stock with beta 1.2 and $10,000 of a stock with beta 0.8.

  • Beta-weighted exposure = $20,000 × 1.2 + $10,000 × 0.8 = $24,000 + $8,000 = $32,000
  • Portfolio beta = $32,000 ÷ $30,000 ≈ 1.07

To roughly neutralize market exposure, you would short about $32,000 of an index ETF or futures equivalent. What remains is your bet on these stocks beating the market. Betas are estimated from history and change over time, so a beta hedge is approximate. Index products are covered in ETFs and index trading.

Common mistakes

  • Counting correlated trades as separate risks and ending up with several times the intended exposure.
  • Trusting calm-period correlations to hold during a crash.
  • Over-diversifying into many highly correlated holdings, which adds complexity without reducing risk.
  • Hedging with an instrument that only loosely tracks the position, leaving a mismatch that can lose on both sides.
  • Sizing each position with full Kelly or full risk budget and ignoring the total. See position sizing.

FAQ

What does correlation mean in trading?

Correlation measures how consistently two markets move together, on a scale from −1 to +1. A high positive correlation means they tend to rise and fall together, so holding both adds up to a larger bet on the same move.

Why do correlations go to one in a crisis?

In a sell-off, many investors sell at the same time to reduce risk, meet margin calls or raise cash, often selling whatever is liquid. That shared selling pressure makes normally unrelated assets fall together, so measured correlations rise sharply.

How many positions do I need to be diversified?

It depends on how correlated they are. With low correlations, most of the benefit comes from the first 10 to 20 holdings. With high correlations, adding more holdings barely reduces risk, because the shared market factor remains.

What is beta in a portfolio?

Beta measures how much a stock or portfolio has tended to move for each 1 percent move in a market index. Multiplying each position's value by its beta gives its market-equivalent exposure, which helps size an index hedge.
