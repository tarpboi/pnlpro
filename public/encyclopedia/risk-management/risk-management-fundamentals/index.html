Risk Management in Trading

10 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Risk management is the set of rules a trader uses to control how much money can be lost on a single trade, in a single day and across the whole account. It decides how large each position is, where it is closed if wrong, and when trading stops altogether. Entries get most of the attention, but risk management is what determines whether a trader survives long enough for any edge to show up in the results.

Key takeaways

  • A trader's long-term result depends on the size of losses at least as much as on how often trades win.
  • Losses and gains are not symmetric: a 50 percent loss needs a 100 percent gain just to get back to even.
  • Most retail traders cap risk at a small fixed percentage of the account per trade, often 0.5 to 2 percent, and set a maximum daily loss.
  • Market risk is only one kind of risk; liquidity, gap, counterparty and operational risks can cause losses that a stop-loss does not prevent.
  • Risk rules only work if they are written down before trading and followed when it is uncomfortable to do so.

Why risk management matters more than entries

New traders often search for the perfect entry signal. In practice, no entry method wins every time, and even good strategies go through long runs of losing trades. What separates an account that survives a bad run from one that does not is usually not the entry. It is how much was risked on each trade.

Consider two traders using the same strategy, which wins 50 percent of the time and makes as much on a winner as it loses on a loser before costs. One risks 1 percent of the account per trade. The other risks 20 percent. Both hit a run of five losses in a row, which in a 50/50 strategy happens regularly over a few hundred trades. The first trader is down about 4.9 percent. The second is down about 67 percent and would need a gain of roughly 205 percent to recover. Same strategy, same luck, very different outcomes.

This is also why the evidence on retail trading is sobering. Studies of retail day traders, such as Barber, Lee, Liu and Odean's work on Taiwan and Chague, De-Losso and Giovannetti's study of Brazilian futures day traders, found that the large majority lost money over time. In the EU, brokers offering contracts for difference (CFDs) must disclose the share of retail accounts that lose money, and the figures are typically 70 to 80 percent or higher. Good risk management does not create an edge, but poor risk management can destroy one, and it makes losses far worse when there is no edge at all. The what is trading article covers the wider evidence.

The asymmetry of losses: survival math

The most important number in risk management is the gain needed to recover from a loss. Because a loss shrinks the base you are compounding from, the recovery percentage is always larger than the loss. The formula is:

Required gain = loss ÷ (1 − loss)

Loss from peak Gain needed to recover
5% 5.3%
10% 11.1%
20% 25.0%
25% 33.3%
30% 42.9%
40% 66.7%
50% 100%
60% 150%
75% 300%
90% 900%

Small losses are easy to recover from. Large ones are not. A trader who loses 50 percent must then double the remaining money, which most professional funds would consider an exceptional multi-year result. This is why the first job of risk management is to keep drawdowns in the shallow part of this table. The drawdown and risk of ruin article goes deeper into recovery math and losing streaks.

The core rules

Risk per trade

Risk per trade is the amount you lose if the trade hits its stop-loss. It is not the size of the position. A $10,000 position with a stop 2 percent away risks about $200, plus slippage and costs.

Many traders express this as a fixed percentage of account equity, commonly 0.5 to 2 percent. The idea is that no single trade, and no realistic run of trades, can do serious damage. If you risk 1 percent per trade, ten straight losses leave you down about 9.6 percent. If you risk 5 percent, the same ten losses cost about 40 percent.

Risk per trade connects three things: account size, the distance to the stop and the position size. Working out the position size from the first two is covered in position sizing, and choosing where the stop goes is covered in stop-loss strategies.

Maximum daily and weekly loss

A daily loss limit is a hard stop for the trading day. Once losses for the day reach the limit, you stop opening new trades until the next session. Common choices are two to four times the per-trade risk, for example a 3 percent daily limit for someone risking 1 percent per trade. Some traders also set a weekly or monthly limit.

Daily limits matter because losses tend to cluster. After several losing trades, judgment often gets worse, not better, and traders start to chase losses. A pre-set limit takes that decision out of the moment. The psychology behind this is covered in handling losses and tilt.

Total open risk

If you hold several positions at once, add up what you would lose if every stop were hit. This total is sometimes called portfolio heat. Five positions at 1 percent risk each is 5 percent of open risk, and if those positions are closely related, they can all lose together. The correlation and portfolio risk article explains why five correlated trades behave like one big trade.

Reward relative to risk

Risk management also means only taking trades where the expected reward justifies the risk, given your win rate. A strategy that wins 70 percent of the time can still lose money if the losers are much larger than the winners. The tools for checking this, R multiples and expectancy, are in risk/reward and expectancy.

Worked example: one account, three sets of rules

Assume a $10,000 account and a strategy with a 45 percent win rate, where winners average 1.5 times the amount risked. This is a modestly profitable system before costs: on average it makes 0.45 × 1.5 − 0.55 × 1 = 0.125 times the amount risked per trade.

Now suppose the trader hits a bad stretch of eight losses in a row. Each trade risks a fixed percentage of current equity.

Risk per trade Equity after 8 losses Drawdown Gain needed to recover
1% $9,227 7.7% 8.4%
2% $8,508 14.9% 17.5%
5% $6,634 33.7% 50.7%
10% $4,305 57.0% 132%

The strategy is identical in every row. Only the sizing changes. At 1 to 2 percent the drawdown is unpleasant but recoverable. At 10 percent the trader needs to more than double the remaining money, and most people abandon a strategy, or start breaking rules, long before that.

With a 45 percent win rate, the chance of losing any particular trade is 55 percent, so a run of eight losses is not rare. Over 200 trades, the chance of at least one streak of eight or more losses is roughly 50 percent. Planning for streaks like this is part of the job.

Types of risk

Price moving against you is the most visible risk, but it is not the only one.

Type of risk What it means Example Typical defenses
Market risk Losses from price moving against the position A stock falls after you buy it Stops, position sizing, diversification
Gap risk Price jumps past your stop with no trading in between A stock closes at $50 and opens at $42 after bad news Smaller size into news and weekends, options, guaranteed stops where offered
Liquidity risk You cannot exit at or near the expected price A small-cap or thin crypto token has few buyers when you want to sell Trade liquid markets, size relative to volume, limit orders
Leverage risk Borrowed exposure magnifies losses and can trigger forced liquidation A 10x position is wiped out by a 10 percent move Lower leverage, isolated margin, wider buffers
Counterparty risk The broker, exchange or other party fails or refuses to pay An exchange freezes withdrawals and goes bankrupt Regulated venues, limits on funds held at any one venue, self-custody for crypto
Operational risk Mistakes and system failures A typo adds an extra zero to an order; a bot keeps trading after a bug Order confirmations, size limits, kill switches, checklists
Model or strategy risk Your edge was never real, or it stops working A backtested strategy fails live because it was overfit Out-of-sample testing, small live size, ongoing review
Regulatory and legal risk Rules change in ways that affect your trading Leverage caps cut, a product delisted in your country Diversified access, keep up with rule changes

Gap risk and liquidity risk

A stop-loss order is an instruction to exit, not a promise of a price. When a market gaps over a weekend, around earnings or on surprise news, a stop order usually fills at the first available price, which can be far beyond the stop. In thin markets the same thing happens without any gap: there simply are not enough buyers or sellers near your price. The cost of crossing the spread and moving the price is covered in bid-ask spread and liquidity.

Leverage and counterparty risk

Leverage does not change the odds of a trade, but it changes how much damage a move can do and how little room you have before a forced exit. Leverage and margin covers the mechanics. Counterparty risk was illustrated by the 2022 collapse of the FTX exchange, where customers lost access to their funds. Historical blowups like this are covered in leverage risk and black swans.

Operational risk

Operational errors are often the most embarrassing losses and among the most avoidable. Common ones include entering the wrong size, trading the wrong symbol, leaving an order working by accident, losing an internet connection with an open position and, for automated traders, a bug that sends orders in a loop. Simple controls, such as a maximum order size, an order confirmation step and a written checklist, prevent many of them.

Putting it together: a minimal risk framework

A basic, workable framework for a retail trader might look like this:

  1. Risk per trade: a fixed 0.5 to 1 percent of equity, calculated before entry.
  2. Stop on every trade: decided before entry, based on where the trade idea is proven wrong.
  3. Daily loss limit: for example 3 percent, after which trading stops for the day.
  4. Maximum open risk: for example 5 percent across all positions, with closely related positions counted together.
  5. Leverage cap: a personal maximum below what the broker allows.
  6. Venue limits: no more money at any single broker or exchange than you can afford to lose if it fails.
  7. Review: a weekly look at whether the rules were followed, recorded in a trading journal.

These rules belong in a written trading plan, so they are set when you are calm rather than in the middle of a trade.

Common mistakes

  • Sizing by conviction. Making a trade larger because it "feels certain" concentrates risk in exactly the trades where judgment may be weakest.
  • Moving or removing stops. Widening a stop to avoid taking a loss turns a small, planned loss into a large, unplanned one.
  • Confusing position size with risk. A small position with no stop can lose more than a large position with a tight stop.
  • Ignoring costs. Spreads, commissions, funding and slippage add to every loss and reduce every gain. See trading costs and fees.
  • Adding to losers. Averaging down without a plan increases risk on a trade that is already going wrong.
  • Treating risk rules as optional after a winning streak. Raising size sharply after a few wins often hands the gains back during the next normal losing run.

FAQ

What is the 1 percent rule in trading?

The 1 percent rule means risking no more than 1 percent of your account equity on any single trade, measured as the loss if the stop-loss is hit. It does not mean the position is 1 percent of the account. It keeps a run of losses from causing serious damage, and some traders use 0.5 or 2 percent instead.

How much should I risk per trade as a beginner?

Many educators suggest 0.5 to 1 percent of the account per trade while learning, because beginners tend to make more mistakes and hit longer losing streaks. The exact figure matters less than choosing one in advance and sticking to it.

Why does a 50 percent loss need a 100 percent gain to recover?

After a 50 percent loss, the account is half its former size. Getting back to the starting value means doubling what remains, which is a 100 percent gain. In general, the required gain is the loss divided by one minus the loss.

Does a stop-loss protect me from all losses?

No. A stop-loss limits losses in normal conditions, but prices can gap past it, and in thin markets it may fill well below the stop price. It also does nothing about broker failure, operational errors or leverage that is too high.
