ATR and Volatility Indicators

Volatility indicators measure how much a market typically moves, without saying which way. The most widely used is the Average True Range (ATR), introduced by J. Welles Wilder in his 1978 book New Concepts in Technical Trading Systems. Traders use ATR and related measures to set stops that fit the market's normal noise, to size positions so that each trade carries similar risk, and to recognize when a market has shifted from calm to turbulent.

Key takeaways

  • True range captures the full move of a bar, including any gap from the previous close; ATR is its smoothed average.
  • ATR is measured in price units, so it is not directly comparable between assets priced differently.
  • ATR-based stops and sizing adapt risk to current conditions instead of using fixed distances.
  • Historical volatility, the annualized standard deviation of returns, is the statistical counterpart to ATR.
  • Volatility clusters: calm periods tend to be followed by calm, and turbulent periods by turbulence, which makes recent volatility a useful, if imperfect, guide.

True range and ATR

A bar's simple range is high minus low. That misses gaps. If a stock closes at 102 and opens the next day at 106, the overnight move is real risk even though it does not show up in the day's high-low range.

True range (TR) is the largest of:

  1. Current high − current low
  2. |Current high − previous close|
  3. |Current low − previous close|

ATR is an average of true range over a lookback period, most commonly 14 bars. Wilder used his own smoothing method, which works like an exponential average:

ATR today = [(ATR yesterday × 13) + TR today] ÷ 14

Some platforms use a simple or exponential average instead, so ATR values can differ slightly between tools.

Worked example: true range

Day Previous close High Low H − L |H − prev C| |L − prev C| True range
A 100.00 103.00 99.00 4.00 3.00 1.00 4.00
B 102.00 108.00 105.00 3.00 6.00 3.00 6.00

On day B the stock gapped up. Its high-low range was only 3.00, but its true range is 6.00 because it traded as much as 6.00 above the prior close.

If the 14-day ATR was 3.50 before day B, the new ATR is [(3.50 × 13) + 6.00] ÷ 14 = (45.50 + 6.00) ÷ 14 = 51.50 ÷ 14 ≈ 3.68.

Using ATR for stops

A stop placed inside a market's normal noise gets hit by random movement. ATR gives a way to place stops relative to that noise. A common approach is a stop some multiple of ATR, often 1.5 to 3, away from the entry or from a structural level. Another is the chandelier or ATR trailing stop, which trails a fixed number of ATRs behind the highest high since entry.

ATR multiples are a tool for calibrating distance, not a replacement for judgment about where the trade idea is proven wrong. Combining structure and ATR is discussed in stop-loss strategies.

Using ATR for position sizing

If every trade uses a 2-ATR stop, the dollar risk per share differs widely between a quiet stock and a volatile one. Volatility-based sizing fixes this by sizing each position so the dollar risk is the same.

Worked example: ATR sizing

An account of $25,000 risks 1 percent per trade, which is $250.

Volatile stock Quiet stock
Entry 107.00 50.00
ATR 3.68 0.80
Stop distance (2 × ATR) 7.36 1.60
Stop price 99.64 48.40
Shares = $250 ÷ stop distance 33 (33.97 rounded down) 156 (156.25 rounded down)
Position value $3,531 $7,800

Both trades risk a little under $250 if the stop fills at its price ($242.88 and $249.60, after rounding down to whole shares). The volatile stock gets a smaller position. This is the core idea behind the sizing used by many trend-following systems. Full formulas, including forex lots and leveraged positions, are in position sizing. Note that stops can fill worse than planned in gaps and fast markets, so actual losses can exceed the planned amount.

Historical volatility

Historical (or realized) volatility is the standard deviation of an asset's returns, usually log returns of daily closes, expressed as an annual percentage. To annualize daily volatility, multiply by the square root of the number of trading periods in a year: about 252 trading days for stocks and futures, 365 for crypto, which trades every day.

Example. A stock's daily returns over the past 20 days have a standard deviation of 1.5 percent. Annualized volatility is 1.5% × √252 ≈ 1.5% × 15.87 ≈ 23.8 percent.

Historical volatility looks backward. Implied volatility, derived from option prices, is the market's forward-looking estimate. Comparing the two is a basic part of options trading.

ATR as a percentage

Because ATR is in price units, a 2.00 ATR means something different for a 20 stock than for a 400 stock. Dividing ATR by price gives ATR percent, which makes comparisons across assets fairer. A 20 stock with an ATR of 1.00 moves about 5 percent a day; a 400 stock with an ATR of 6.00 moves about 1.5 percent.

Keltner Channels

Keltner Channels draw bands around a moving average at a multiple of ATR. The original version from Chester Keltner in 1960 used a different construction; the modern form, popularized later, typically uses a 20-period exponential moving average with bands at 2 × ATR above and below.

Example. A 20-EMA at 50.00 and an ATR of 1.20 give bands at 50.00 ± 2.40, so 47.60 to 52.40.

Keltner Channels look similar to Bollinger Bands, which use standard deviation instead of ATR. Bollinger Bands react more sharply to sudden price spikes; Keltner Channels are smoother. When Bollinger Bands contract inside Keltner Channels, some traders call it a "squeeze," a sign of unusually low volatility that may precede a larger move.

Volatility regimes and clustering

Volatility is not constant. Large moves tend to be followed by large moves, of either sign, and small moves by small moves. Benoit Mandelbrot described this pattern in commodity prices in the 1960s, and Robert Engle's ARCH model (1982) and Tim Bollerslev's GARCH extension (1986) formalized it; Engle shared the 2003 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for this work on time-varying volatility. Volatility clustering is one of the best-documented features of financial returns.

Practical implications:

  • Recent volatility is informative. A market that has been wild for a week is more likely to stay wild than to calm down overnight.
  • Regimes shift. Strategies that work in low-volatility, trending conditions can struggle in high-volatility, choppy conditions, and vice versa. Some traders reduce size when ATR expands sharply.
  • Calm can end suddenly. Long quiet periods can lull traders into larger positions just before volatility returns. See leverage risk and black swans.

Common mistakes

  • Reading ATR as direction. Rising ATR means bigger moves, not up moves.
  • Comparing raw ATR across assets. Use ATR percent.
  • Using one ATR multiple everywhere. A multiple that suits a liquid index future may be too tight for a small-cap stock or a meme coin.
  • Forgetting gaps. An ATR stop does not protect you from a gap that opens beyond it.
  • Ignoring the lookback. A 14-day ATR still reflects last week's calm after a sudden shock; it catches up only gradually.

FAQ

What is a good ATR multiple for a stop-loss?

Many traders use 1.5 to 3 times ATR, but there is no universally correct value. The right multiple depends on the market, the timeframe and the strategy, and should be tested rather than assumed.

What does a high ATR mean?

A high ATR means the asset has been making large moves recently, measured in price units. It says nothing about direction and should be compared to the asset's own history or converted to a percentage of price.

What is the difference between ATR and standard deviation?

ATR averages the size of each bar's full range, including gaps, in price units. Standard deviation measures how widely returns are spread around their average and is the basis for historical volatility and Bollinger Bands.

What period should I use for ATR?

Fourteen periods is the most common default, following Wilder. Shorter periods react faster to change but are noisier; longer periods are smoother but slower to reflect a new volatility regime.
