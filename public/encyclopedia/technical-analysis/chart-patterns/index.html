Chart Patterns

Chart patterns are recurring shapes that price traces over many bars, such as head and shoulders, double tops, triangles and flags. Traders group them into reversal patterns, which are said to mark the end of a trend, and continuation patterns, which are said to mark a pause within one. Each pattern comes with a conventional entry point, usually a breakout of a boundary, and a "measured move" target. They are useful for structuring trades, but they fail often, are easy to see in hindsight, and have only limited support in rigorous testing.

Key takeaways

  • A chart pattern is not complete until price breaks its defining boundary, such as the neckline of a head and shoulders.
  • Measured-move targets project the pattern's height from the breakout point; they are rough guides, not forecasts.
  • Many patterns are combinations of support and resistance and trend structure, described with a name.
  • Pattern failures are common, and a failed pattern can produce a sharp move in the opposite direction.
  • Patterns are subjective, so defining them precisely and testing them is the only way to know if they add anything for you.

How chart patterns are traded

Most patterns share the same trade logic:

  1. Identify the shape as it forms, using swing highs and lows.
  2. Wait for confirmation, usually a candle close beyond the pattern's boundary (the neckline, trendline or range edge). Before the break, it is only a potential pattern.
  3. Enter on the breakout or on a retest of the broken boundary.
  4. Place a stop inside the pattern, where the breakout idea would be clearly wrong.
  5. Set a target with the measured move, adjusted for nearby levels.

Volume is often used as a secondary check: classic texts describe volume declining as a pattern forms and expanding on the breakout. See volume analysis.

Reversal patterns

Head and shoulders

A head and shoulders top forms after an uptrend. It has three peaks: a left shoulder, a higher peak (the head) and a lower right shoulder. The neckline connects the lows between the peaks. The pattern completes when price closes below the neckline.

In structural terms, the right shoulder is a lower high, and the neckline break is a lower low, which is a break of structure. The inverse head and shoulders is the mirror image at the end of a downtrend.

Double and triple tops and bottoms

A double top is two peaks at roughly the same price with a trough between them. It completes when price closes below the trough. A double bottom is the reverse. Triple tops and bottoms add a third test. All of them describe price failing repeatedly at the same level and then breaking the other side of the range.

Rounding bottoms

A slow, U-shaped transition from decline to advance. They are more subjective than most patterns and usually span many weeks or months.

Continuation patterns

Flags and pennants

A flag follows a sharp, near-vertical move (the pole). Price then drifts in a small rectangle sloping against the move. A pennant is the same idea with a small symmetrical triangle instead of a rectangle. A breakout in the direction of the pole is said to resume the trend. These are short-term patterns, typically lasting days on a daily chart.

Rectangles

Price moves sideways between flat support and resistance. A rectangle is simply a range; the pattern view adds the expectation of a breakout, more often in the direction of the prior trend.

Cup and handle

A rounded decline and recovery (the cup) back to near the prior high, followed by a shallow pullback (the handle), then a breakout above the rim. It is popular among growth-stock traders.

Patterns that can go either way

Triangles

  • Ascending triangle: flat resistance, rising lows. Often read as bullish because buyers keep stepping in higher.
  • Descending triangle: flat support, falling highs. Often read as bearish.
  • Symmetrical triangle: falling highs and rising lows converging. Direction is unclear until it breaks.

Triangles reflect narrowing volatility. The breakout direction is less predictable than the name suggests, which is why traders wait for it.

Wedges

Both boundaries slope in the same direction while converging.

  • Rising wedge: higher highs and higher lows, but the lows rise faster. Traditionally read as bearish, because momentum is fading.
  • Falling wedge: traditionally read as bullish.

Worked examples: measured moves

Head and shoulders top

A stock forms a left shoulder at $54, a head at $60 and a right shoulder at $54.50. The neckline is flat at $50.

  • Pattern height = head - neckline = $60 - $50 = $10
  • Measured target = neckline - height = $50 - $10 = $40

A short seller entering on a close at $49.60 might place a stop above the right shoulder at $55.00. Risk = $55.00 - $49.60 = $5.40. Reward to the $40 target = $49.60 - $40 = $9.60. Reward-to-risk = $9.60 / $5.40, about 1.8.

A tighter stop just above the neckline, say at $51.50, reduces risk to $1.90 but is far more likely to be hit if price retests the neckline, which is common.

Double bottom

Lows at $30.00 and $30.20, with a peak between them at $34.50.

  • Height = $34.50 - $30.00 = $4.50
  • Target = $34.50 + $4.50 = $39.00

Bull flag

A pole runs from $20 to $26, a $6 move. The flag drifts down to $24.80, and price breaks out at $25.

  • Target = $25 + $6 = $31

These targets are conventions. Price often stops short at a prior resistance level, and sometimes goes much further. Many traders take partial profits at the measured move and trail a stop on the rest.

Failure rates and confirmation

Chart patterns fail frequently. A "failure" usually means price breaks out, then quickly reverses back through the boundary, or never reaches its target. Practitioner studies that catalog thousands of patterns have reported that failure rates vary widely by pattern, market and how "failure" is defined, and that many patterns underperform the measured-move expectation.

Some points are widely agreed:

  • Unconfirmed patterns are guesses. Many potential double tops turn into ordinary pullbacks in an uptrend because price never breaks the middle trough.
  • Failed patterns can be informative. When a well-watched head and shoulders breaks its neckline and then reverses sharply higher, traders who shorted the break are forced to buy back, which can add to the move. Some traders deliberately trade these failures. See price action trading.
  • Trend context matters. Continuation patterns in the direction of a strong higher-timeframe trend are commonly considered more reliable than reversal patterns against it.

What the evidence says

Lo, Mamaysky and Wang (2000) built an algorithm to detect patterns such as head and shoulders, double tops and bottoms, triangles and rectangles in US stock prices. They found that several patterns carried some incremental information about subsequent returns, which suggests the shapes are not pure noise. Their study did not show that trading the patterns produced profits after costs. Other research has reached mixed or negative conclusions, and results are sensitive to how patterns are defined.

The main obstacle is subjectivity. Two traders looking at the same chart will often disagree about whether a pattern exists, where the neckline is, and when it broke. Converting a pattern into exact rules and backtesting it is the only way to measure it, and doing that well requires care to avoid overfitting.

Common mistakes

  • Trading before the breakout. Anticipating a pattern means taking many trades on shapes that never complete.
  • Seeing patterns in random data. Random price series regularly produce convincing head and shoulders and triangles.
  • Treating the target as a promise. The measured move is a rough guide, and nearby levels often matter more.
  • Using stops that are too tight. Retests of the breakout level are common and often trigger stops placed right at the boundary.
  • Ignoring the larger trend. A small bearish pattern inside a strong weekly uptrend is fighting the dominant flow.
  • Moving the pattern after it fails. Redrawing the neckline to avoid admitting the pattern failed is a form of confirmation bias.

FAQ

What is the most reliable chart pattern?

No pattern is reliable on its own; failure rates are meaningful for all of them and vary by market and definition. Traders generally have more confidence in patterns that are confirmed by a close beyond the boundary, align with the higher-timeframe trend, and break on rising volume.

How do you calculate a chart pattern's price target?

Most patterns use a measured move: take the pattern's height, such as head to neckline or the pole of a flag, and project it from the breakout point. Treat the result as a rough guide and check it against nearby support and resistance.

Is a head and shoulders pattern bullish or bearish?

A standard head and shoulders at the end of an uptrend is traditionally read as bearish once the neckline breaks. The inverse head and shoulders, at the end of a downtrend, is read as bullish.

What is the difference between a flag and a pennant?

Both are short pauses after a sharp move. A flag consolidates in a small sloping rectangle, while a pennant consolidates in a small converging triangle.
