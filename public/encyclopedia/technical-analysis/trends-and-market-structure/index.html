Trends and Market Structure

Market structure is the pattern of swing highs and swing lows that price makes as it moves. An uptrend is a sequence of higher highs and higher lows, a downtrend is a sequence of lower highs and lower lows, and a range is price moving sideways between roughly stable boundaries. Identifying which of these a market is in is one of the first steps in most technical approaches, because many tools work well in one condition and poorly in the other. Structure describes the market's current state; it does not guarantee that the state will continue.

Key takeaways

  • Trends are defined by swing points: higher highs and higher lows in an uptrend, lower highs and lower lows in a downtrend.
  • Trendlines and channels are visual guides drawn through swing points, and they are more subjective than horizontal levels.
  • A break of structure, such as an uptrend making a lower low, is an early sign that the trend may be changing, not proof that it has.
  • Markets alternate between trending and ranging regimes, and strategies built for one usually struggle in the other.
  • ADX measures trend strength, not direction, and helps separate trending from choppy conditions.

Swing highs and swing lows

A swing high is a peak with lower prices on both sides. A swing low is a trough with higher prices on both sides. How many bars on each side are required is a choice: some traders use two bars, others use five or more, and some judge swings by eye. The choice changes which swings you see, which is one reason structure analysis can be subjective.

Once swings are marked, the trend definitions follow from Dow Theory, dating to Charles Dow's work in the late 19th century:

Regime Swing highs Swing lows Typical description
Uptrend Higher highs (HH) Higher lows (HL) Buyers step in at progressively higher prices
Downtrend Lower highs (LH) Lower lows (LL) Sellers step in at progressively lower prices
Range Roughly equal Roughly equal Neither side gains ground; price oscillates between support and resistance
Transition Mixed Mixed For example, lower highs with equal lows, which may form a triangle

Structure depends on timeframe. A daily uptrend can contain several short downtrends on the 15-minute chart. Traders usually decide which timeframe defines "the trend" for their style, then use a shorter one for timing. See multi-timeframe analysis.

Trendlines and channels

A trendline connects two or more swing lows in an uptrend, or two or more swing highs in a downtrend. It gives a rough guide to the trend's slope and a dynamic area where pullbacks have tended to find buyers (or sellers, in a downtrend).

Guidelines traders commonly use:

  • Two points draw a line; a third touch is often treated as confirmation that the line is meaningful.
  • Lines connecting wicks and lines connecting bodies give different results. Pick one convention and stay consistent.
  • Very steep trendlines tend to break quickly. Gentler ones tend to last longer but give later signals.
  • On long-term charts of assets that have risen many times over, logarithmic scale makes trendlines more meaningful than linear scale.

A channel adds a parallel line on the other side of price, through the swing highs in an uptrend or the swing lows in a downtrend. Some traders buy near the lower line and take profits near the upper line while the channel holds.

Trendlines are more subjective than horizontal levels. Two analysts can draw quite different lines on the same chart, and lines are often redrawn after they break. Treat a trendline break as information, not a signal on its own.

Break of structure and change of character

A break of structure happens when price breaks a swing point that defined the trend:

  • In an uptrend, the key level is the most recent higher low. A close below it means the sequence of higher lows has ended.
  • In a downtrend, the key level is the most recent lower high. A close above it ends the sequence of lower highs.

Some traders distinguish between a break of structure in the direction of the trend (a new higher high, which continues the trend) and a break against the trend, sometimes called a change of character. These terms are popular in Smart Money Concepts, but the underlying idea is classic Dow Theory.

A break of structure often leads to a range or a reversal, but the original trend frequently resumes. Many traders therefore wait for a lower high after the lower low before treating an uptrend as reversed.

Worked example: marking structure and a trendline

A stock makes these swing points over about a month (days are trading days):

Day Swing Price Label
0 Low $100 Start
5 High $112
10 Low $105 Higher low
15 High $118 Higher high
20 Low $110 Higher low
25 High $116 Lower high

Trendline. Connect the lows at day 0 ($100) and day 10 ($105). The slope is ($105 - $100) / 10 days = $0.50 per day. Projected to day 20, the line sits at $100 + 20 x $0.50 = $110. The day-20 low touched it exactly, giving a third touch.

Channel. Draw a parallel line through the day-5 high of $112. On day 5 the trendline was at $100 + 5 x $0.50 = $102.50, so the channel is $112 - $102.50 = $9.50 wide. On day 15 the upper line was at $100 + 15 x $0.50 + $9.50 = $117. The day-15 high of $118 slightly overshot it.

Structure change. On day 25 the rally stalls at $116, below the $118 high: a lower high. The trend is still technically up because the last higher low ($110) is intact. On day 28 the stock closes at $109, below $110. That is a break of structure. By day 28 the trendline is at $114, so the line had already broken a few days earlier.

A trader holding a long position from the trend might have exited on the trendline break, on the lower high, or on the break of structure, depending on their rules. Each choice trades earlier exits (and more false alarms) against later exits (and more profit given back). This is a stop-loss decision as much as an analysis decision.

Ranges vs trends

Markets spend a large share of their time moving sideways. Ranges and trends call for different tools:

Condition Tools that tend to fit Tools that tend to struggle
Trending Moving average filters, breakouts, pullback entries, trailing stops Oscillator "overbought / oversold" fades
Ranging Buying support and selling resistance, oscillators such as RSI Breakout entries, moving average crossovers (frequent whipsaws)

Many losing streaks come from using a trend tool in a range or a range tool in a trend. The hard part is that regimes are often only obvious in hindsight.

Identifying the regime

Traders use several methods, usually in combination:

  • Swing structure: are highs and lows progressing, or flat?
  • Moving average slope and order: in a strong uptrend, shorter moving averages sit above longer ones and all slope upward. In a range, they flatten and tangle.
  • Volatility: narrowing ranges often precede expansion, as shown by Bollinger Band width or ATR.
  • ADX: a dedicated measure of trend strength.

ADX: Average Directional Index

ADX was introduced by J. Welles Wilder Jr. in his 1978 book New Concepts in Technical Trading Systems, the same book that introduced RSI and ATR. It belongs to the Directional Movement System:

  • +DI (positive directional indicator) measures upward movement, based on how much each high exceeds the previous high.
  • -DI (negative directional indicator) measures downward movement, based on how much each low falls below the previous low.
  • ADX is a smoothed average of the absolute difference between +DI and -DI, scaled by their sum. It runs from 0 to 100. The default period is 14.

The key point is that ADX measures strength, not direction. A strong downtrend and a strong uptrend can both produce high ADX. Direction comes from comparing +DI and -DI.

Common rules of thumb:

ADX reading Common interpretation
Below about 20 Weak or no trend; range-type conditions
20 to 25 Trend may be developing
Above about 25 Trending conditions
Rising ADX Trend strengthening
Falling ADX from high levels Trend losing strength, or turning into a range

These thresholds are conventions, not tested laws, and ADX lags because of its double smoothing. It can stay low during the first part of a new trend and stay high for some time after a trend has ended.

What the evidence says

Trends are one of the better-supported ideas in technical analysis. Research on time-series momentum, notably Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen (2012), found that an asset's past-year return tended to predict its near-term return across many futures markets; this underpins systematic trend following. That evidence comes from mechanical rules across many markets, with long losing stretches, and does not validate any particular hand-drawn trendline.

Common mistakes

  • Forcing a trend onto a range. Not every chart is trending. Sideways is a valid answer.
  • Redrawing trendlines to fit. If a line has to be adjusted every few days, it is not telling you much.
  • Calling a reversal on one swing. A single lower high or break of a minor low often leads nowhere.
  • Mixing timeframes without a plan. Deciding the trend on one timeframe and then switching to another when a trade goes wrong.
  • Reading ADX as direction. High ADX in a falling market is a strong downtrend, not a buy signal.

FAQ

How do you know if a market is trending or ranging?

Look at the swing points: progressing highs and lows suggest a trend, while flat highs and lows suggest a range. Moving average slope and ADX readings above about 25 are common secondary checks.

What is a break of structure in trading?

It is when price breaks the swing point that defined the trend, such as an uptrend closing below its most recent higher low. It warns that the trend may be ending but is often followed by the trend resuming.

What is a good ADX value?

Readings above about 25 are commonly read as a trending market and below about 20 as weak or no trend. ADX shows strength only, so use +DI and -DI or price structure to judge direction.

Should trendlines connect wicks or candle bodies?

Both conventions are used, and neither has been shown to be better. What matters is applying one method consistently so that your lines are comparable across charts.
