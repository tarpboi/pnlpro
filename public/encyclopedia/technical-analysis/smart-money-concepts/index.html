Smart Money Concepts (SMC and ICT)

Smart money concepts (SMC) are a family of price action ideas built on the premise that large institutions ("smart money") move prices to trigger retail traders' stop orders and then reverse. The approach was popularized largely by Michael J. Huddleston, who teaches under the name Inner Circle Trader (ICT), and spread through YouTube, social media and paid mentorships, especially among forex, index futures and prop-firm traders. Most SMC ideas are new names for older technical concepts, and there is no independent body of evidence showing that the method as a whole produces an edge.

Key takeaways

  • SMC's core vocabulary is liquidity pools and sweeps, order blocks, fair value gaps, break of structure, premium and discount zones, and kill zones.
  • Nearly every concept maps to an established idea: stop runs, supply and demand zones, gaps, Dow-style trend structure, 50 percent retracements and session timing.
  • The part with real support is narrow: stop orders do cluster near obvious highs, lows and round numbers, and triggering them can accelerate moves.
  • The broader story of institutions deliberately engineering every move is not testable as stated and is not supported by independent research.
  • SMC is heavily marketed, often alongside prop-firm challenges and paid mentorships, so evaluate claims and track records skeptically.

The core concepts

Liquidity pools and sweeps

In SMC, liquidity means clusters of resting orders, especially stop-losses, that sit just beyond obvious swing highs, swing lows, equal highs or lows, and trendlines. A liquidity sweep (or "stop hunt," "liquidity grab") is a quick push beyond such a level that triggers those stops, followed by a reversal.

Example. A pair has made two highs at 1.2500 and 1.2502, a pattern SMC calls "equal highs," with likely buy-stop orders from short sellers above them. Price spikes to 1.2515, then closes the hour back at 1.2480. An SMC trader reads this as a sweep of buy-side liquidity and looks for short setups.

In classic terms this is a failed breakout or false break, the same idea as a Wyckoff "upthrust." See price action trading.

Break of structure and change of character

Break of structure (BOS) is when price breaks a prior swing high in an uptrend or a swing low in a downtrend, confirming the trend continues. Change of character (CHoCH) is the first break against the trend, such as the first lower low after a series of higher lows, treated as an early reversal signal. These are the higher-high, higher-low trend definitions of Dow theory and classic market structure analysis.

Order blocks

An order block is usually defined as the last opposite-colored candle before a strong, impulsive move: the last down candle before a sharp rally (bullish order block) or the last up candle before a sharp drop (bearish). The theory is that institutions placed large orders there and may defend the zone when price returns. Traders mark the candle's range as a zone for entries.

In classic terms, an order block is a supply or demand zone, closely related to support and resistance. Definitions vary between teachers, for instance whether to use the candle's body or full range, which makes order blocks subjective in practice.

Fair value gaps

A fair value gap (FVG) is a three-candle pattern where the middle candle moves so strongly that the first candle's high and the third candle's low (in a rally) do not overlap. The unfilled space is seen as an imbalance that price may return to "fill."

Example. Candle 1 has a high of 100.40. Candle 2 rallies strongly from 100.30 to 102.10. Candle 3 has a low of 101.20. The fair value gap is 100.40 to 101.20, a zone 0.80 wide that candles 1 and 3 never both traded through. SMC traders might look to buy if price pulls back into that zone.

In classic terms this resembles a gap or a "single print" or low-volume area in volume and market profile analysis: a price range traversed quickly with little two-sided trade.

Premium and discount

SMC divides a trading range into a premium half (above the 50 percent midpoint, or "equilibrium") and a discount half (below it). The guideline is to buy in discount and sell in premium. Many SMC teachers also mark an optimal trade entry (OTE) zone at roughly the 62 to 79 percent retracement.

Example. A swing runs from a low of 95.00 to a high of 105.00. Equilibrium is 100.00. Below 100.00 is discount. The OTE zone for a long is a 62 to 79 percent retracement: 105.00 − 6.20 = 98.80 down to 105.00 − 7.90 = 97.10.

In classic terms this is the 50 percent retracement and the 61.8 to 78.6 percent Fibonacci zone, which carry the same evidence problems.

Kill zones

Kill zones are time windows when SMC traders expect the most meaningful moves, typically around the London open and New York open, defined in New York time. Exact hours vary between teachers and versions. The underlying observation, that volume and volatility concentrate around major session opens and data releases, is well established. See forex trading sessions.

What the evidence says

What has support. Stop-loss orders do cluster. Research by economist Carol Osler, using order data from a large foreign exchange dealer, found that stop-loss and take-profit orders cluster near round numbers, and that triggered stop orders can propagate price moves. Session timing effects on volume and volatility are also well documented. So the idea that price sometimes spikes through an obvious level, triggers stops and then reverses is reasonable.

What lacks support. There is no body of independent, peer-reviewed research showing that trading SMC setups produces returns above what chance and costs would predict. The broader narrative, that institutions or "the algorithm" deliberately engineer each move to hunt retail stops, is usually presented as fact but cannot be tested in the form it is stated. Any outcome can be explained after the fact: if price reverses at an order block, the block "held"; if it doesn't, it was "mitigated," a sweep is still pending, or the trader picked the wrong block. A framework that can explain every result predicts none of them.

Large market participants do care about liquidity, because they need counterparties to fill big orders. That is a different claim from saying they steer price toward retail stops on a 5-minute chart. The market participants article describes who is actually on the other side of trades.

The marketing around SMC

SMC has a large commercial ecosystem: paid mentorships, signal groups, indicator packs and social media accounts showing large wins or funded-account payouts. Much of it is linked to prop firm challenges, where the business model depends heavily on evaluation fees, and to broker or prop-firm affiliate commissions. Profit screenshots are easy to select or fabricate, and verified, audited track records are rare across the trading-education market. See courses, gurus and signals for how to evaluate any educator.

None of this means every SMC trader loses. Some traders use its vocabulary as a structured way to read price action and manage risk. The value, if any, comes from consistent rules, risk control and execution, not from the terminology.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the narrative as fact. "Smart money hunted the stops" is an interpretation, not an observation.
  • Too many overlapping zones. With order blocks, FVGs, OTEs and liquidity levels on every timeframe, almost every price is near a "point of interest."
  • Moving definitions to fit. Relabeling a failed order block as a new one hides losses from your own review.
  • Tiny stops and huge stated reward-to-risk. Very tight stops can make hypothetical reward-to-risk look large while win rates fall sharply.
  • Buying expensive mentorship without verifying results.

FAQ

Does ICT or smart money trading actually work?

There is no independent evidence that SMC as a method produces an edge. Some of its building blocks, such as stop clustering near obvious levels and session-open volatility, are real, but they are long-known ideas, and results depend on the individual trader's rules and discipline.

What is an order block in trading?

In SMC, an order block is usually the last opposite-colored candle before a strong impulsive move, treated as a zone where institutions may have placed orders. In classic technical analysis it is essentially a supply or demand zone.

What is a fair value gap?

A fair value gap is a three-candle pattern where a strong middle candle leaves a price range that the first and third candles do not overlap. SMC traders expect price to revisit that range, similar to the classic idea of gaps being filled.

Is smart money concepts just price action?

Largely, yes. Most SMC concepts correspond to established price action ideas such as false breakouts, supply and demand zones, trend structure and retracement levels, described with new terminology and a narrative about institutional behavior.
