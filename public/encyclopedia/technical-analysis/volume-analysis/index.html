Volume Analysis

Volume analysis is the study of how many shares, contracts or coins change hands during a period, read alongside price. Price shows where the market traded; volume shows how much participation stood behind that move. Traders use it to judge whether a breakout, trend or reversal has broad support or is running on thin interest.

Key takeaways

  • Volume measures participation, not direction: every trade has a buyer and a seller, so high volume means many contracts changed hands, not that "buyers won."
  • A move on rising, above-average volume is usually treated as more credible than the same move on light volume.
  • Relative volume compares current activity to its own normal level and is more useful than raw volume numbers.
  • On-balance volume and the accumulation/distribution line turn volume into a running total that can confirm or diverge from price.
  • Crypto volume can be inflated by wash trading, and spot forex has no central volume figure at all, so the data source matters as much as the indicator.

What volume tells you, and what it doesn't

Each bar on a chart has a volume figure: the total quantity traded during that bar. It is usually drawn as a histogram under the price chart, often colored green or red depending on whether the bar closed up or down.

The most common misconception is that a big green volume bar means "more buyers than sellers." That cannot be true in a literal sense. Every share bought is a share sold. What volume does tell you is how much disagreement and turnover there was at those prices. When volume is high and price moves strongly in one direction, one side was willing to pay up (or sell down) aggressively, and enough counterparties met them. When volume is low, the move happened without much commitment and can reverse more easily.

If you need to know who was aggressive, buyers lifting the offer or sellers hitting the bid, that is a job for order flow tools, which classify each trade by side.

Volume confirming price

The classic principle, going back to Charles Dow's era, is that volume should expand in the direction of the trend. A few practical readings:

Price action Volume Common interpretation
Breakout above resistance Well above average Breakout more likely to hold
Breakout above resistance Below average Higher risk of a fakeout
Uptrend, pullback Volume shrinks on the pullback Pullback looks like profit-taking, trend intact
Uptrend, new high Volume falling on each new high Trend may be losing participation
Sharp drop to a new low Extreme spike Possible selling climax

These are tendencies, not rules. Breakouts from chart patterns and through support and resistance levels fail regularly even on strong volume. Volume improves the odds slightly in some contexts; it does not turn a coin flip into a sure thing.

Climax volume

A climax is an unusually large volume spike, often several times the recent average, that comes after an extended move. A selling climax appears after a long decline: panicked holders dump at any price and stronger hands absorb the supply. A buying climax appears after a long rally, when late buyers pile in and early holders sell into them. Climaxes often mark at least a short-term exhaustion point, but the reversal can take time, and sometimes a climax is followed by another one. The Wyckoff method builds a whole framework around these events; see Elliott Wave and Wyckoff.

Relative volume (RVOL)

Raw volume is hard to compare across assets. Five million shares is huge for a small cap and tiny for a mega cap. Relative volume solves this by dividing current volume by a typical level, usually the average over the past 10 to 30 sessions.

Example. A stock averages 2,000,000 shares a day over the last 20 sessions. Today it trades 6,000,000 shares. Its relative volume is 6,000,000 ÷ 2,000,000 = 3.0, or three times normal.

Intraday traders usually compare against the same time of day, because volume is heavily concentrated near the open and close in stock markets. If a stock normally trades 400,000 shares by 10:30 a.m. and today it has traded 1,500,000 by then, its time-adjusted RVOL is 1,500,000 ÷ 400,000 = 3.75.

High relative volume tends to accompany news, earnings and catalysts, which is why momentum traders scan for it. It tells you something unusual is happening; it does not tell you which way price will go next.

On-balance volume (OBV)

On-balance volume, popularized by Joseph Granville in the 1960s, is a running total. On an up-close day, that day's volume is added. On a down-close day, it is subtracted. On an unchanged day, nothing changes.

Worked example. Starting OBV at zero:

Day Close Change Volume OBV
1 50.00 (start) 1,000,000 0
2 50.80 Up 1,200,000 1,200,000
3 50.40 Down 800,000 400,000
4 51.10 Up 1,500,000 1,900,000
5 51.10 Unchanged 900,000 1,900,000
6 52.00 Up 2,000,000 3,900,000

The absolute OBV number is meaningless; only its direction matters. Traders watch for divergence: price makes a new high while OBV fails to, which suggests the rally is happening on lighter up-day volume. OBV is crude, though. A day that closes up by one cent counts all its volume as positive, the same as a day that rallies 5 percent.

Accumulation/distribution line

The accumulation/distribution (A/D) line, associated with Marc Chaikin, refines OBV by weighting each bar's volume by where it closed within its range. The close location value (CLV) is:

CLV = [(Close − Low) − (High − Close)] ÷ (High − Low)

CLV runs from +1 (close at the high) to −1 (close at the low). Each bar adds CLV × Volume to the running total.

Example. A day with high 22.00, low 20.00, close 21.50 and volume 100,000. CLV = [(21.50 − 20.00) − (22.00 − 21.50)] ÷ 2.00 = (1.50 − 0.50) ÷ 2.00 = 0.5. The day adds 0.5 × 100,000 = 50,000 to the A/D line.

Because the A/D line ignores gaps between bars, a stock that gaps up and then drifts down to close near its low will show distribution even though it finished the day higher. That is a known weakness, and a reason to read it with price, not instead of price.

Volume in crypto: the wash trading problem

Crypto exchanges report their own volume, and there is no consolidated tape. Wash trading, where the same party (or colluding parties) trades with itself to inflate activity, has been widely documented, especially on lightly regulated venues. A widely cited 2019 analysis by asset manager Bitwise argued that most of the bitcoin volume reported across exchanges at the time was likely fake or non-economic, and academic work has reached similar conclusions about unregulated exchanges.

Practical consequences:

  • Prefer volume from large, regulated or well-audited venues, or aggregated "real volume" data from reputable data providers.
  • Treat volume on small tokens and new listings with deep suspicion. See altcoin trading for more on this.
  • Volume across spot and perpetual futures markets can tell different stories; derivatives volume often dwarfs spot.

Volume in forex: no central number

Spot forex trades over the counter, across banks, ECNs and retail brokers, so there is no single volume figure. The "volume" on a retail forex chart is usually tick volume: the number of price updates in a bar from that broker's feed. Tick volume tends to correlate with real activity, since busier markets update more often, but it is one broker's view and is not a count of currency traded. Some traders use currency futures volume from a central exchange such as CME as a proxy. See forex basics for how the market is structured.

Common mistakes

  • Reading volume color as buyer-versus-seller control. A green volume bar only means the bar closed higher.
  • Comparing raw volume across assets or times of day. Use relative volume.
  • Trusting crypto volume at face value. Check the venue.
  • Treating one volume spike as a signal on its own. Spikes need context: where price is relative to trend, levels and news.
  • Ignoring structural shifts. Index rebalancing days, options expiry and holiday sessions distort volume for reasons unrelated to conviction.

FAQ

Is high volume bullish or bearish?

Neither by itself. High volume means heavy participation; whether it is bullish or bearish depends on the price action and context, such as a breakout above resistance versus a capitulation low.

What is a good relative volume for day trading?

Many day traders look for relative volume of 2 or higher as a sign that a stock is "in play," but there is no universal threshold. Higher relative volume usually means more liquidity and more volatility, which cuts both ways.

What is the difference between OBV and the accumulation/distribution line?

OBV adds or subtracts a bar's entire volume based only on whether the close was up or down. The accumulation/distribution line weights volume by where the close sits within the bar's high-low range, so a close near the middle contributes little.

Does volume work in forex?

Only approximately. Spot forex has no central exchange, so charts show tick volume from one broker's feed, which is a rough proxy for activity rather than a true count of currency traded.
