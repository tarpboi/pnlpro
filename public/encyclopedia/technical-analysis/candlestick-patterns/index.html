Candlestick Patterns

Candlestick patterns are named shapes formed by one, two or three consecutive candles on a price chart. Traders use them to describe moments when buying or selling pressure appears to shift, such as a long lower wick after a decline or a large candle that swallows the one before it. They are among the most widely taught tools in technical analysis, but on their own they are weak signals: their usefulness depends heavily on where they appear and what follows.

Key takeaways

  • Candlestick patterns describe short-term shifts in the balance between buyers and sellers over one to three periods.
  • The same pattern means little in the middle of a range and more at a level traders are already watching.
  • Most traders wait for confirmation, such as the next candle breaking the pattern's high or low, before acting.
  • Systematic tests of candlestick patterns have generally found weak or inconsistent predictive value, especially after trading costs.
  • A pattern's most practical use is often to define where a trade is wrong, which gives a logical stop location.

How to read this guide

Each pattern below is built from the parts of a candle described in reading candlestick charts: the body (open to close) and the wicks (extremes beyond the body). Definitions vary between books and software, so treat the proportions below as common conventions rather than fixed rules.

"Bullish" and "bearish" here describe what a pattern is traditionally said to suggest. They are not predictions.

Single-candle patterns

Doji

A doji has an open and close that are equal or very close, so the body is a thin line. It shows that price moved during the period but ended where it began.

  • Standard doji: short wicks on both sides.
  • Long-legged doji: long wicks on both sides; large range, no net progress.
  • Dragonfly doji: long lower wick, little or no upper wick.
  • Gravestone doji: long upper wick, little or no lower wick.

A doji is usually read as indecision. After a strong run it can hint that momentum is slowing, but dojis are common in quiet markets and often mean nothing.

Hammer and hanging man

Both have a small body near the top of the range and a lower wick at least about twice the body's length, with little or no upper wick.

  • A hammer appears after a decline. It is read as sellers pushing price down and buyers driving it back up before the close.
  • A hanging man is the same shape after a rally. It is read as a warning that selling appeared during the period, although the close held up.

Shooting star and inverted hammer

These are the mirror image: a small body near the bottom of the range and a long upper wick.

  • A shooting star appears after a rally and is read as buyers failing to hold higher prices.
  • An inverted hammer appears after a decline and is read as an early sign of buying interest.

Pin bars

"Pin bar" (from Pinocchio bar) is a price-action term for any candle with one long wick and a small body at the opposite end. Hammers and shooting stars are pin bars. Traders who use the term usually care less about the name and more about whether the long wick probed beyond a key level and was rejected. See price action trading.

Marubozu

A marubozu has a large body and little or no wick on either side: price opened at one extreme and closed at the other. It shows one-sided control for that period.

Two-candle patterns

Bullish and bearish engulfing

  • Bullish engulfing: a bearish candle followed by a bullish candle whose body completely covers the first candle's body.
  • Bearish engulfing: a bullish candle followed by a bearish candle whose body covers the first.

Example. Day 1 opens at $45.00 and closes at $44.00 (bearish). Day 2 opens at $43.80 and closes at $45.40. Day 2's body runs from $43.80 to $45.40, which fully contains Day 1's body from $44.00 to $45.00. That is a bullish engulfing pattern.

In 24-hour markets such as forex and crypto, each candle usually opens near the prior close, so a strict "engulfing" with a gap is rare. Many traders relax the definition there.

Harami

A harami is the reverse of engulfing: a large candle followed by a small candle whose body sits inside the first candle's body. "Harami" is Japanese for pregnant, describing the shape. It is read as momentum pausing. A harami whose second candle is a doji is called a harami cross.

Tweezer tops and bottoms

Two candles with matching or nearly matching highs (tweezer top) or lows (tweezer bottom). They are read as price testing the same level twice and failing to go beyond it.

Three-candle patterns

Morning star and evening star

  • Morning star: a large bearish candle, then a small-bodied candle (often gapping lower in stock markets), then a large bullish candle that closes well into the first candle's body. It is read as selling exhausting and buyers taking over.
  • Evening star: the bearish mirror, appearing after a rally.

Three white soldiers and three black crows

  • Three white soldiers: three consecutive large bullish candles, each opening within the previous body and closing near its high.
  • Three black crows: three consecutive large bearish candles, each closing near its low.

These describe sustained one-sided pressure. After an extended move, they can also mark short-term exhaustion, which is one reason they are unreliable on their own.

Context matters more than shape

A hammer in the middle of a sideways range carries little information. The same hammer at a level where price has turned several times before, on heavier-than-usual volume, after a long decline, is a different situation. Traders typically weigh:

  • Location: is the pattern at support or resistance, a moving average, or a prior swing point?
  • Trend: does it agree with the larger trend, or fight it?
  • Volume: did participation rise on the signal candle? See volume analysis.
  • Timeframe: a pattern on a daily chart reflects far more trading than one on a 1-minute chart.

Worked example: trading a hammer with a defined risk

A stock has fallen from $24 to around $19.50 and is approaching a level where it bounced twice last year. The next daily candle has open $20.10, high $20.25, low $19.20 and close $20.20.

  • Body = $20.20 - $20.10 = $0.10
  • Lower wick = $20.10 - $19.20 = $0.90, nine times the body
  • Upper wick = $20.25 - $20.20 = $0.05

This qualifies as a hammer at a support area. A trader might plan:

  • Entry: buy only if the next day trades above the hammer's high, at $20.26 (confirmation).
  • Stop: just below the hammer's low, at $19.19.
  • Risk per share: $20.26 - $19.19 = $1.07.
  • Target: prior resistance at $22.40, a reward of $22.40 - $20.26 = $2.14.
  • Reward-to-risk: $2.14 / $1.07 = 2.0.

If the next day never trades above $20.26, there is no trade. If it does and then falls to $19.19, the trade loses $1.07 per share, an expected outcome a meaningful share of the time. The pattern did not predict the bounce; it gave a specific, testable plan with a known loss. Sizing the position from that $1.07 is covered in position sizing.

What the evidence says

Candlestick patterns have been tested many times in academic and practitioner studies across stock, futures and currency markets. The broad picture is that most patterns show little or no consistent predictive power once trading costs and multiple testing are accounted for, and results that look strong in one market or period often fail in another. A few studies report modest effects for some patterns in specific settings, but there is no consensus list of patterns that reliably work.

Part of the difficulty is definitional: "small body" and "long wick" must be turned into exact numbers to test, and slightly different thresholds give different results. If you want to rely on a pattern, define it precisely and backtest it on the market and timeframe you trade, with realistic costs.

Common mistakes

  • Trading the pattern without the context. Location and trend matter more than the candle's shape.
  • Acting before the candle closes. A hammer halfway through the day can close as an ordinary bearish candle.
  • Skipping confirmation. Entering on the pattern alone means taking many trades that never follow through.
  • Memorizing dozens of patterns. Many named patterns are variations of the same idea: rejection of a price area, or one side taking control.
  • Placing stops too tight. A stop just a few cents beyond a long wick can be hit by normal noise. See stop-loss strategies.

FAQ

What is the most reliable candlestick pattern?

No candlestick pattern has been shown to be reliably predictive across markets and time periods. Traders who use them tend to focus on a few simple patterns, such as engulfing candles and pin bars, at important price levels, and to confirm them before acting.

What does a hammer candlestick mean?

A hammer has a small body near the top and a long lower wick, and it appears after a decline. It shows that sellers pushed price down during the period but buyers drove it back up before the close.

What is the difference between a doji and a spinning top?

Both show indecision. A doji has an open and close that are virtually equal, while a spinning top has a small but visible body with wicks on both sides.

Do candlestick patterns work in crypto and forex?

The same shapes appear in every market, but 24-hour trading means candles rarely gap, which changes how patterns like engulfing and morning star form. Their evidence base is no stronger in crypto or forex than elsewhere, so test any pattern on the market you trade.
