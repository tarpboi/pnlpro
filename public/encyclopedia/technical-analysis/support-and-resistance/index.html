Support and Resistance

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Support is a price area where a falling market has tended to stop and turn up, and resistance is an area where a rising market has tended to stop and turn down. These levels form at prior highs and lows, round numbers and other prices that traders remember and place orders around. Support and resistance are the foundation of most chart-based trading because they give traders reference points for entries, stops and targets. They describe where price has reacted before, not where it must react again.

Key takeaways

  • Support and resistance are best treated as zones, not exact lines.
  • Common sources are prior swing highs and lows, round numbers, gap edges and areas of heavy trading.
  • A broken level often switches roles, so old resistance can act as new support, and vice versa.
  • Levels tend to matter because orders cluster there and because many traders watch the same prices.
  • Breakouts frequently fail, so traders look for confirmation before treating a level as broken.

What support and resistance are

Imagine a stock that falls to about $48, bounces, falls back to $48 a few weeks later and bounces again. Traders start to call $48 support: an area where buying interest has, so far, outweighed selling. If the same stock repeatedly stalls near $55, that area becomes resistance.

These levels are horizontal. Diagonal lines drawn along rising or falling prices are trendlines, covered in trends and market structure. Moving averages can also act as moving levels, which is discussed in moving averages.

Why levels work (when they do)

There is no single accepted explanation, but several mechanisms are plausible and work together:

  • Order clustering. Traders place limit orders, stop-loss orders and take-profit orders at obvious prices. A cluster of buy limit orders below the market can absorb selling and slow a decline. A cluster of stop orders can do the opposite: when hit, they add fuel to the move. The order book is where this happens.
  • Memory and regret. Traders who bought at $55 and watched the price fall may sell when it returns to $55 to "get out even." Traders who missed buying at $48 may buy if it gets back there. This anchoring on past prices is well documented in behavioral finance. See cognitive biases.
  • Self-fulfilling attention. If many people watch the same level, their actions near it can create a reaction, at least briefly.
  • Institutional activity. Large buyers and sellers often work orders over days around a price they consider fair, which can create repeated turns near that area.

None of these makes a level reliable on its own. Levels break all the time, and the more obvious a level is, the more likely it is that other traders are positioned around it, including those who aim to trigger the stops beneath it.

Zones, not lines

Price rarely turns at exactly the same number twice. A level that "held at $48" may have actually seen lows of $48.20, $48.55, $48.35 and $48.70. Drawing a single line at $48.00 would be misleading; drawing a zone from $48.20 to $48.70 better represents where buyers showed up.

Practical ways to draw zones:

  • Use the bodies and wicks of the turning candles to set the top and bottom of the zone.
  • Make zones wider on volatile assets and higher timeframes. A useful sanity check is the asset's typical daily range, measured by ATR.
  • Prefer levels visible on higher timeframes. A level on the weekly chart reflects more trading than one on a 5-minute chart.

Round numbers

Round numbers matter partly because people choose them for orders. Research on order placement has found that limit orders cluster at round prices. A level just above or below a round number (for example, stops placed at $49.90 rather than $50.00) can be where the actual reaction occurs.

Role reversal

When a level breaks decisively, it often changes roles. Old resistance becomes new support, and old support becomes new resistance. The logic is that traders who sold at resistance and watched price break higher may buy back if price returns to that level, and traders who missed the breakout may buy the retest.

Role reversal is common enough that many breakout traders wait for a "retest" of the broken level before entering. But retests do not always happen, and when they do, the level can fail again.

Breakout vs fakeout

A breakout is a move through a support or resistance level that continues. A fakeout (also called a false breakout or failed breakout) is a move through the level that quickly reverses back inside.

Fakeouts are frequent because obvious levels attract stop orders just beyond them. When price pushes through and triggers those stops, the burst of orders can exhaust itself, and price returns to the range. Traders try to separate the two with:

  • Closing basis. Requiring a candle to close beyond the level, not just trade through it.
  • Distance filter. Requiring the close to be beyond the zone by a set amount, such as a fraction of ATR.
  • Volume. Breakouts on rising volume are commonly treated as more credible than those on thin volume.
  • Retest. Waiting for price to come back to the broken level and hold.
  • Time. Seeing whether price can stay beyond the level for several bars.

Every filter trades off speed for confirmation. Waiting for a close and a retest reduces fakeouts but means a worse entry price and missing some breakouts entirely.

Worked example: a breakout with defined risk

A stock has met resistance three times in the zone $61.80 to $62.30 over two months. Today it closes at $62.90 on volume about twice its recent average.

A swing trader with a $20,000 account who risks 1 percent per trade plans:

  • Entry: $62.90.
  • Stop: below the resistance zone, which should now act as support, at $61.50.
  • Risk per share: $62.90 - $61.50 = $1.40.
  • Dollar risk: 1% of $20,000 = $200.
  • Position size: $200 / $1.40 = 142.86, rounded down to 142 shares.
  • Position value: 142 x $62.90 = $8,931.80.

Over the next week, the stock rises to $64.50, then pulls back to $62.10, inside the old resistance zone, and holds. That is a role-reversal retest. If instead it had closed back below $61.80 within a day or two, the breakout would have been a fakeout, and the stop at $61.50 would have limited the loss to about $200 plus costs. See position sizing and stop-loss strategies.

How strong is a level?

Traders commonly judge strength by:

  • Number of touches. More reactions suggest more interest. However, each test may also absorb the orders resting there, so some traders view repeated tests as a sign a level is weakening.
  • Timeframe. Weekly levels generally outweigh intraday ones.
  • Size of the reaction. A sharp move away from a level shows stronger participation than a small drift.
  • Recency. Recent levels are more likely to be remembered by active participants.

There is no objective formula that combines these, which is one reason two traders often draw different levels on the same chart.

Common mistakes

  • Drawing too many lines. A chart covered in levels guarantees that some will "work" in hindsight and gives no clear plan.
  • Treating levels as exact. Stops placed exactly at a level, or a few ticks beyond it, are vulnerable to normal noise and stop runs.
  • Buying every touch of support. Support in a downtrend breaks more often than it holds. Check the larger trend first.
  • Ignoring the timeframe. An intraday level may be irrelevant to a swing trade, and vice versa.
  • Chasing every breakout. Without filters, many breakouts are fakeouts, and the losses add up.

FAQ

How do you identify support and resistance levels?

Start with obvious swing highs and lows on a higher timeframe, add round numbers and areas where price turned several times, and draw each as a zone covering the cluster of reactions. Fewer, clearer levels are more useful than many faint ones.

Why does broken resistance become support?

Traders who sold at the old resistance, or missed the breakout, may buy when price returns to that level, creating demand where there used to be supply. This tendency is common but not guaranteed.

How can you tell a real breakout from a fake one?

No method is certain, but traders often require a candle to close beyond the level, look for rising volume, and wait to see whether price holds on a retest. Each filter reduces false signals at the cost of a later entry.

Do support and resistance work in crypto?

The same ideas are widely used in crypto, and round numbers such as major Bitcoin price thresholds draw a lot of attention. Crypto's 24/7 trading, thin weekend liquidity and frequent leverage-driven liquidation cascades mean that wicks through levels are common.
