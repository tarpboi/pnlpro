Technical Analysis

Technical analysis (TA) is the study of past prices and trading volume to make decisions about future trades. Instead of asking what an asset is worth, a technical analyst asks how buyers and sellers have behaved and what that behavior might mean for the next move. TA is used in stocks, forex, futures, crypto and even prediction markets, at every horizon from seconds to years. It is popular because charts are free and visual, but the evidence that it produces profits after costs is mixed, so it pays to understand both what it can show and where it fails.

Key takeaways

  • Technical analysis treats price and volume as a record of supply, demand and crowd behavior, and looks for repeating structure in that record.
  • The same market can look completely different on a line, bar, candlestick, Heikin-Ashi or renko chart, and on a 5-minute versus a weekly timeframe.
  • Academic research has documented momentum and trend effects across many markets, but many classic chart patterns and indicator signals have not held up once costs and data snooping are accounted for.
  • TA is most useful as a framework for planning trades and managing risk, not as a way to predict prices with certainty.
  • Every TA rule can be tested on historical data, and untested rules deserve skepticism.

The premises of technical analysis

Technical analysis rests on a few working assumptions. None of them is a law of nature, and each has critics.

  1. Price reflects available information. Earnings, interest rates, news and rumors all reach the chart through trades. A technical analyst therefore watches price rather than trying to model every input. This overlaps with, but is not the same as, the efficient market idea, which says that if price reflects all information, past prices should not help predict future ones.
  2. Prices move in trends. Markets can drift in one direction for long stretches because information spreads slowly, positions are built gradually, and traders herd. The study of trends and market structure builds on this premise.
  3. Behavior repeats. Fear, greed, anchoring on past prices and reluctance to sell at a loss are persistent human traits. Technical analysts argue that these traits leave recurring footprints, such as support and resistance levels where traders remember earlier turning points.

The roots of the field go back a long way. Japanese rice traders used early forms of candlestick charts centuries ago, and in the United States, Charles Dow's newspaper writing in the late 19th century became the basis of what is now called Dow Theory, with its focus on trends confirmed by higher highs and higher lows.

Technical vs fundamental analysis

Fundamental analysis estimates value from earnings, cash flows, economic data or token supply. Technical analysis studies the market's behavior directly. Many traders combine the two: fundamentals to choose what to trade and in which direction, technicals to choose when to enter and where to place a stop.

Chart types

A chart is a way of compressing many trades into a picture. The choice of chart changes what you see.

Chart type What each unit shows Strengths Weaknesses
Line One price per period, usually the close Clean view of the overall path Hides intraperiod range and volatility
Bar (OHLC) Open, high, low and close per period Full range information Harder to read at a glance
Candlestick Same data as a bar, with a colored body Easy to see direction and range Invites over-reading of single candles
Heikin-Ashi Smoothed candles built from averages Makes trends easier to see Prices shown are not real traded prices
Renko Fixed-size bricks, time ignored Filters out small moves Depends heavily on brick size; lags

Line, bar and candlestick charts

Line charts connect closing prices. They are useful for long-term views and for comparing several assets. Bar charts and candlestick charts both show the open, high, low and close (OHLC) for each period. The data is identical; candles simply color the area between open and close, which most traders find faster to read. Specific shapes and sequences of candles are covered in candlestick patterns.

Heikin-Ashi

Heikin-Ashi ("average bar" in Japanese) candles are calculated rather than plotted directly from trades:

  • HA close = (open + high + low + close) / 4
  • HA open = (previous HA open + previous HA close) / 2
  • HA high = the highest of the high, HA open and HA close
  • HA low = the lowest of the low, HA open and HA close

Worked example. Suppose the previous Heikin-Ashi candle had an HA open of 100 and an HA close of 102. The current real candle has open 102, high 105, low 101 and close 104.

  • HA close = (102 + 105 + 101 + 104) / 4 = 412 / 4 = 103
  • HA open = (100 + 102) / 2 = 101
  • HA high = max(105, 101, 103) = 105
  • HA low = min(101, 101, 103) = 101

The real candle closed at 104, but the Heikin-Ashi candle shows 103. That difference matters: you cannot place orders at Heikin-Ashi prices, and a backtest that assumes fills at them will look better than reality. Heikin-Ashi is a viewing aid for trend direction, not a price record.

Renko and other time-independent charts

Renko charts draw a new brick only when price moves a fixed amount, such as $1 or 10 pips, from the previous brick. Time is ignored, so a quiet day may produce no bricks and a volatile hour may produce ten. Renko filters noise, but the picture depends entirely on the brick size, and bricks form only after the move has happened.

Timeframes

Every chart has a timeframe: the period each bar or candle represents. Common choices are 1-minute, 5-minute, 15-minute, 1-hour, 4-hour, daily and weekly. The choice usually follows your trading style:

Style Typical holding period Common chart timeframes
Scalping Seconds to minutes Tick, 1-minute
Day trading Minutes to hours 1- to 15-minute
Swing trading Days to weeks 1-hour, 4-hour, daily
Position trading Weeks to months Daily, weekly

The same asset can be in an uptrend on the daily chart and a downtrend on the 15-minute chart. Neither view is wrong; they answer different questions. Traders who look at several timeframes in sequence use what is called multi-timeframe analysis.

Shorter timeframes have more noise relative to the size of the move, and trading costs take a larger share of each trade. That is one reason short-horizon retail trading has particularly poor outcomes in the research on retail day traders.

The main toolkits

Technical analysis is a broad family of methods. Most fall into a few groups:

  • Price structure: trends, swing highs and lows, support and resistance, chart patterns such as triangles and head and shoulders. Traders who work mainly from bare charts call this price action trading.
  • Indicators: formulas applied to price or volume. Examples include moving averages, the RSI, MACD, Bollinger Bands and ATR. Indicators do not add new information; they summarize price in a different form.
  • Volume and order flow: volume analysis, VWAP, volume profile and order flow look at how much traded and where.
  • Structural theories: frameworks such as Elliott Wave and Wyckoff, Fibonacci tools and Smart Money Concepts. These tend to be more interpretive and harder to test objectively.

What the evidence says

The honest answer to "does technical analysis work?" is: some parts appear to have had value in some markets and periods, many parts have not, and edges that once existed tend to shrink after they are published.

Evidence that supports some technical ideas:

  • Momentum. Jegadeesh and Titman (1993) documented that US stocks with strong returns over the past 3 to 12 months tended to keep outperforming over the following months. This cross-sectional momentum effect has been found in many countries and asset classes.
  • Time-series momentum and trend following. Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen (2012) found that an asset's own past 12-month return tended to predict its next month's return across dozens of futures markets. This is the academic backbone of trend following and the managed-futures industry. The strategy has historically had long flat or losing periods.
  • Simple trading rules in older data. Brock, Lakonishok and LeBaron (1992) found that moving-average and trading-range-break rules had predictive power on the Dow Jones index over a long historical sample. Later work by Sullivan, Timmermann and White (1999), which adjusted for data snooping, found that the best rules did not keep working in later data.
  • Patterns with some information. Lo, Mamaysky and Wang (2000) used automated pattern recognition on US stocks and concluded that some classic patterns carried a small amount of incremental information. They did not show that trading them was profitable after costs.

Evidence that cuts against it:

  • Many popular patterns and indicator signals look convincing on hand-picked charts but fail in systematic tests, especially after commissions, spreads and slippage.
  • Results are fragile. Change the parameters slightly, or test on a different period or market, and the edge often disappears. This is the problem of overfitting and data snooping.
  • Markets adapt. When a rule becomes widely known and easy to automate, faster and better-funded traders tend to compete its profits away.
  • Retail outcomes are poor. Studies of retail day traders, including Barber, Lee, Liu and Odean on Taiwan and Chague, De-Losso and Giovannetti on Brazil, found that the large majority lost money, and very few were profitable year after year. Most of those traders used some form of chart-based trading.

There is also a self-fulfilling element. If many traders watch the same 200-day moving average or the same round-number level, orders cluster there and price can react, at least briefly. That reaction is real, but it is not the same as a reliable, exploitable edge.

Worked example: why a "good" pattern can still lose

Suppose a chart setup is said to win 55 percent of the time. Each winner makes $100 and each loser loses $100. Commissions, spread and slippage cost $8 per round trip.

  • Expectancy per trade = (0.55 x $100) - (0.45 x $100) - $8 = $55 - $45 - $8 = +$2

Over 100 trades that is +$200, a thin edge. Now suppose the real win rate, measured honestly on fresh data, is 52 percent rather than 55:

  • Expectancy per trade = (0.52 x $100) - (0.48 x $100) - $8 = $52 - $48 - $8 = -$4

Over 100 trades that is -$400. A 3-point error in the estimated win rate turns a small winner into a steady loser. This is why costs and honest measurement matter more than the pattern itself. See risk/reward and expectancy for the full framework.

How to use technical analysis responsibly

  • Use TA to define risk, not just entries. A support level or swing low gives a logical place for a stop. Knowing where you are wrong is often more valuable than the entry signal. See stop-loss strategies.
  • Write rules down. "Buy when it looks strong" cannot be tested. "Buy when the daily close is above the 50-day moving average and RSI is above 50" can.
  • Test before trading. Backtesting on historical data, with realistic costs, then paper trading, will reject many ideas cheaply.
  • Prefer simple rules with few parameters. The more knobs a system has, the easier it is to fit it to past noise.
  • Look for independent confirmation. Two indicators built from the same closing prices are not independent evidence.
  • Keep a record. A trading journal shows whether your chart reading actually adds value, rather than relying on memory, which tends to favor winners.
  • Remember the base rate. Most retail traders lose money, especially at short horizons and with leverage. TA does not exempt anyone from that.

Common mistakes

  • Seeing patterns everywhere. Humans are good at finding shapes in random data. A random-walk price series will produce head and shoulders, triangles and double tops by chance.
  • Hindsight charting. Patterns are easy to label after the fact. The question is whether you could have identified them in real time, before the outcome.
  • Indicator stacking. Adding more indicators derived from the same price data adds complexity, not information.
  • Ignoring costs. A rule that makes a small gross profit per trade can lose money after spread, commission and slippage, especially on short timeframes.
  • Treating TA as prediction. Charts describe probabilities and context. A setup failing is a normal outcome, not proof the market was manipulated.
  • Buying courses that promise accuracy. Claims of 80 or 90 percent win rates with no verified record are a common marketing tactic. See courses, gurus and signals.

FAQ

Does technical analysis actually work?

Parts of it have academic support, notably momentum and trend-following effects across many markets, while many chart patterns and indicator signals have not held up in rigorous tests after costs. It works best as a framework for planning and risk control rather than as a prediction tool.

What is the best chart type for beginners?

Candlestick charts are the most common starting point because they show the open, high, low and close clearly and are the default on most platforms. Line charts are useful for seeing the big picture without distraction.

Which timeframe should I use for technical analysis?

Match the timeframe to how long you plan to hold a trade: minutes-long trades use 1- to 15-minute charts, while trades held for days or weeks use 4-hour, daily or weekly charts. Shorter timeframes carry more noise and higher costs relative to the expected move.

Is technical analysis better than fundamental analysis?

Neither is universally better; they answer different questions. Fundamental analysis estimates what an asset may be worth, technical analysis studies how it is trading, and many traders use both together.
