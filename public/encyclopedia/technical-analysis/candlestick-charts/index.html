How to Read Candlestick Charts

A candlestick chart shows, for each time period, the price at which trading opened, the highest and lowest prices reached, and the price at which it closed. Each period is drawn as a "candle" with a thick body between the open and close and thin wicks reaching to the high and low. Candlesticks are the default chart on most trading platforms, so reading them is a basic skill for anyone who looks at price charts. A candle is a summary of what happened, not a forecast of what will happen next.

Key takeaways

  • Every candle encodes four prices: open, high, low and close (OHLC).
  • The body shows the distance between open and close; the wicks show how far price traveled beyond that range during the period.
  • Where the close sits within the candle's range is a simple description of which side controlled the end of the period.
  • The same price action looks very different on different timeframes, because a candle is just an aggregation of smaller ones.
  • Candles and OHLC bars contain identical data; the difference is only visual.

Anatomy of a candle

Each candle is built from four numbers for its period:

  • Open: the first traded price of the period.
  • High: the highest traded price.
  • Low: the lowest traded price.
  • Close: the last traded price.

These are drawn as two parts:

  • Body. The rectangle between open and close. If the close is above the open, the candle is bullish and is usually drawn green or hollow. If the close is below the open, it is bearish and usually drawn red or filled. Colors are a platform setting, and some regions use the reverse convention (for example, red for up is common in China and some other East Asian markets), so check your chart's settings.
  • Wicks (also called shadows or tails). Thin lines above and below the body. The upper wick runs from the top of the body to the high; the lower wick runs from the bottom of the body to the low.
Element Measured as What it describes
Body size Absolute value of close minus open Net progress over the period
Upper wick High minus the higher of open and close How far price rose before being pushed back
Lower wick Lower of open and close minus low How far price fell before being pushed back
Range High minus low Total volatility within the period

A candle with no body (open and close equal or nearly equal) is called a doji. Named shapes like this are covered in candlestick patterns.

What candles say about buying and selling pressure

A candle does not show who bought or sold, or how much. It shows the path's endpoints and extremes. Traders still read it as a rough description of the balance between buyers and sellers:

  • Large body, small wicks. Price moved steadily in one direction and closed near the extreme. One side was in control for the whole period.
  • Small body, long wicks. Price moved a lot but ended close to where it started. Neither side kept control, which traders often describe as indecision.
  • Long lower wick. Sellers pushed price down during the period, but it recovered before the close. Traders call this rejection of lower prices.
  • Long upper wick. Buyers pushed price up, but it fell back. Traders call this rejection of higher prices.

These descriptions are useful shorthand, but they are interpretations. A long lower wick in a thin market may reflect one large sell order hitting an empty order book, not a broad shift in sentiment. Wicks also tend to be longer around news releases and at session opens, when liquidity is thin.

Context matters more than the candle itself. A long lower wick that forms at a well-watched support level tells a different story from the same shape in the middle of a range. Volume adds another dimension: a large-bodied candle on unusually heavy volume is generally treated as more meaningful than one on light volume. See volume analysis.

Worked example: measuring one candle

A stock's daily candle has open $50.00, high $52.40, low $49.20 and close $51.80.

  • The close is above the open, so it is a bullish candle.
  • Body = $51.80 - $50.00 = $1.80
  • Upper wick = $52.40 - $51.80 = $0.60
  • Lower wick = $50.00 - $49.20 = $0.80
  • Range = $52.40 - $49.20 = $3.20
  • Check: body + upper wick + lower wick = $1.80 + $0.60 + $0.80 = $3.20.

Two ratios are often used to describe a candle objectively:

  • Body as a share of range = $1.80 / $3.20 = 56.25%. More than half the day's range was net progress.
  • Close location = (close - low) / (high - low) = ($51.80 - $49.20) / $3.20 = $2.60 / $3.20 = 81.25%. The stock closed in the top fifth of its range.

A reasonable description: buyers made solid progress and held most of it into the close, with a modest dip below the open early on. That describes the day. It does not say whether tomorrow will be up or down.

Timeframe dependence

A candle can represent one minute, one hour, one day or one week. Longer candles are simply combinations of shorter ones:

  • The open is the first sub-candle's open.
  • The high is the highest high among the sub-candles.
  • The low is the lowest low.
  • The close is the last sub-candle's close.

Worked example. Four 1-hour candles:

Hour Open High Low Close
1 100.0 102.0 99.0 101.0
2 101.0 103.0 100.0 102.5
3 102.5 102.8 98.5 99.0
4 99.0 100.5 98.8 100.2

Combined into one 4-hour candle: open 100.0, high 103.0, low 98.5, close 100.2. That is a small-bodied candle (body 0.2) with a long range (4.5). On the 4-hour chart it looks like indecision. On the 1-hour chart, it was a rally followed by a sharp selloff and a partial recovery.

Neither picture is wrong. They describe the same trades at different resolutions. This is why a "bullish" candle on a 5-minute chart can sit inside a bearish daily candle, and why traders often check more than one timeframe. See multi-timeframe analysis.

When does a candle "close"?

Daily candles depend on where the day starts. For stocks, the daily candle usually covers the regular session, and pre-market or after-hours trades may or may not be included depending on the platform. For forex, many platforms close the daily candle at 5 p.m. New York time. Crypto trades around the clock, so daily candles commonly close at 00:00 UTC, but some platforms use local time. Two traders looking at the "same" daily chart may therefore see different candles. See market hours and sessions.

Also note that the most recent candle is not finished until its period ends. A candle that looks like a strong rejection halfway through the hour may close as something quite different.

Bar charts vs candlestick charts

An OHLC bar chart uses the same four prices. Each bar is a vertical line from low to high, with a small tick on the left for the open and a tick on the right for the close.

Feature OHLC bar Candlestick
Data shown Open, high, low, close Open, high, low, close
Direction cue Position of the left and right ticks Body color
Readability at a glance Lower Higher
Visual weight Lighter; good for dense charts Heavier; bodies draw the eye
Traditional users Western traders, many futures traders Originated in Japan; now dominant globally

Because the data is identical, any analysis done on candles can be done on bars. Some experienced traders prefer bars precisely because candle bodies make single periods look more significant than they are.

Candlestick charting is traditionally associated with Japanese rice traders and was popularized among Western traders in the early 1990s.

Common mistakes

  • Reading the unfinished candle as final. The current candle can change completely before it closes.
  • Ignoring the timeframe. A strong candle on a 1-minute chart may be invisible on the daily chart.
  • Assuming platform candles match. Different session times and data feeds produce different OHLC values, especially for daily candles in forex and crypto.
  • Over-reading wicks in thin markets. A long wick during illiquid hours may reflect one order, not market sentiment.
  • Treating color as signal. A green candle only means the close was above the open. A green candle can still close well below the previous candle's close if the market gapped down.

FAQ

What do the wicks on a candlestick mean?

Wicks show the highest and lowest prices traded during the period beyond the open and close. A long wick means price traveled far in that direction but was pushed back before the period ended.

What does a green or red candle mean?

A green (or hollow) candle closed above its open, and a red (or filled) candle closed below its open. Colors are a platform setting, and some markets use the opposite convention, so check your chart settings.

Are candlestick charts better than bar charts?

They show exactly the same open, high, low and close data. Candlesticks are easier to read quickly, which is why most platforms use them by default, while some traders prefer bars for a less cluttered view.

Why do daily candles look different on different platforms?

Platforms define the start and end of the trading day differently, especially for forex and crypto, which trade nearly or fully around the clock. Different session times and data sources produce different open, high, low and close values.
