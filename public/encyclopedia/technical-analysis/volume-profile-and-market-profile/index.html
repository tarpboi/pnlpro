Volume Profile and Market Profile

Volume profile and market profile are ways of showing where trading took place by price rather than by time. Instead of a volume bar under each candle, a volume profile draws a horizontal histogram showing how much traded at each price level. Market profile, developed by J. Peter Steidlmayer at the Chicago Board of Trade in the 1980s, shows how much time price spent at each level. Both are widely used by futures traders to find the prices the market has accepted as fair and the prices it has rejected.

Key takeaways

  • A volume profile shows volume at each price; its peak is the point of control (POC), the price with the most volume.
  • The value area is the range around the POC containing about 70 percent of the period's volume.
  • High-volume nodes tend to act as areas of acceptance where price slows down; low-volume nodes tend to be areas price moves through quickly.
  • Market profile uses time (TPO letters) instead of volume and frames the market as moving between balance and imbalance.
  • These tools describe where business was done; they do not predict which way price will go next.

Volume at price

A conventional volume histogram tells you how much traded during each time bar. A volume profile groups the same trades by price. Every trade in the chosen period is added to the row for its price, and the result is drawn as horizontal bars, usually on the right or left side of the chart.

The period can be one session, a week, a custom range (for example, from a major low to today), or a "visible range" covering whatever is on screen. Each choice can show a different picture, so traders specify which profile they are using.

Key terms

Term Meaning
Point of control (POC) The price level with the highest volume in the profile
Value area (VA) The range of prices around the POC containing about 70 percent of volume
Value area high (VAH) / low (VAL) The upper and lower edges of the value area
High-volume node (HVN) A price area with heavy volume, a peak in the histogram
Low-volume node (LVN) A price area with little volume, a trough in the histogram
Naked or virgin POC A prior session's POC that price has not traded back to since

The 70 percent figure is a convention borrowed from statistics: about 68 percent of a normal distribution lies within one standard deviation of the mean. Market data is not normally distributed, so the value area is a practical rule of thumb, not a precise statistical band.

Worked example: POC and value area

A futures contract's session profile, with one-point rows:

Price Volume (contracts)
4510 2,000
4509 4,000
4508 9,000
4507 15,000
4506 22,000
4505 18,000
4504 12,000
4503 6,000
4502 3,000
Total 91,000

The POC is 4506, with 22,000 contracts. Seventy percent of 91,000 is 63,700.

A common simplified method builds the value area outward from the POC, at each step adding whichever adjacent row (above or below) has more volume:

  1. Start at 4506: 22,000.
  2. Compare 4507 (15,000) with 4505 (18,000). Add 4505: total 40,000.
  3. Compare 4507 (15,000) with 4504 (12,000). Add 4507: total 55,000.
  4. Compare 4508 (9,000) with 4504 (12,000). Add 4504: total 67,000, which exceeds 63,700. Stop.

The value area runs from 4504 (VAL) to 4507 (VAH) and holds 67,000 ÷ 91,000 ≈ 73.6 percent of volume. Some platforms compare two rows at a time instead of one, so their value area can differ slightly.

How traders read a profile

Acceptance and rejection. High-volume nodes are prices where many participants were willing to trade. Price often slows down and rotates around them. Low-volume nodes are prices the market moved through quickly, often during a sharp move; when price returns, it may move through them quickly again or reverse at their edge.

Value area as a reference. Many traders compare where a session opens to the prior session's value area:

  • Opening inside the prior value area suggests the market is still in balance and may rotate within it.
  • Opening outside it suggests the market may be seeking a new value, especially if it holds outside.
  • A widely repeated rule of thumb, often called the "80 percent rule," says that if price opens outside the prior value area and then trades back inside it for a sustained period, it is likely to travel across to the other side. The 80 percent figure is folklore rather than a verified statistic, and results vary by market and period.

POC as a magnet or level. The POC is often watched as an area price may return to, and naked POCs from earlier sessions are tracked as potential targets. These are observations traders find useful, not proven effects.

Volume profile levels overlap heavily with traditional support and resistance: heavy trading at a price is one reason that price becomes a reference point. The difference is that the profile shows the volume behind the level rather than inferring it from swing highs and lows.

Market profile and TPO charts

Market profile uses time price opportunities (TPOs) instead of volume. The session is split into periods, traditionally 30 minutes each, and each period gets a letter. Every price traded during a period receives that period's letter. Stacking the letters at each price produces a profile shaped by time rather than volume.

Common market profile concepts:

Concept Meaning
Initial balance The range of the first hour of the session (traditionally the first two periods)
Range extension Trading beyond the initial balance later in the session
Single prints Prices with only one TPO letter, left by fast moves; similar to low-volume nodes
Poor high or low A session extreme without a clean tail, sometimes seen as unfinished business
Profile shapes A rounded "D" profile suggests balance; elongated profiles suggest trend days; "P" and "b" shapes suggest short covering or long liquidation

The TPO POC (the price with the most letters) and the volume POC are often close, but not always. A level where price spent a long time on thin volume will look important on a TPO chart and less so on a volume profile.

Balance and imbalance

Market profile frames the market as alternating between two states:

  • Balance: Buyers and sellers agree roughly on value. Price rotates within a range, and profiles take a bell-like shape.
  • Imbalance: One side is more aggressive, value is being re-priced, and price trends toward a new area where trade can be facilitated, after which a new balance forms.

Traders who use this framework typically fade the edges of a balance area (sell near the top, buy near the bottom) and switch to trading with the move when price breaks out of balance and is accepted outside it. This is a conceptual cousin of range versus trend regimes discussed in trends and market structure.

How futures traders use it

Profiles are most common among traders of index futures, Treasury futures, crude oil and other central-exchange futures, because those markets publish complete, centralized volume data. Typical uses:

  • Separate profiles for the regular session and the overnight session.
  • Composite profiles over weeks or months to find longer-term value areas and nodes.
  • Combining profile levels with VWAP and order flow to judge whether a level is being defended.

In US stocks, volume is spread across many exchanges and off-exchange venues, but consolidated data makes profiles workable. In crypto, profiles depend on which exchange's data you use, and wash trading can distort them. In spot forex, profiles rely on tick volume from a single broker and are rough at best. See volume analysis.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the POC as a guaranteed turning point. It is a reference area, not a wall.
  • Mixing profile periods. A session profile and a monthly composite answer different questions; label which one you are using.
  • Using poor data. Profiles are only as good as the volume behind them.
  • Ignoring context. A value area means little during a major news repricing, when the old value is no longer relevant.
  • Overlaying too many profiles. With enough of them, every price is near a node.

FAQ

What is the point of control in volume profile?

The point of control is the price level where the most volume traded during the period the profile covers. Traders watch it as a reference for fair value and as an area price may revisit.

What is the difference between volume profile and market profile?

Volume profile measures how much volume traded at each price. Market profile measures how much time price spent at each level using TPO letters. They often highlight similar areas but can differ when price lingers on thin volume.

Why is the value area 70 percent?

The 70 percent convention comes from the normal distribution, in which about 68 percent of observations fall within one standard deviation of the mean. Markets are not normally distributed, so it is a practical rule of thumb rather than a precise statistic.

Does volume profile work for forex?

Only approximately. Spot forex has no central volume, so profiles use tick volume from one broker's feed. Some traders use currency futures data from a central exchange instead.
