Gold and Precious Metals

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Commodities, Bonds & More

Precious metals, mainly gold, silver, platinum and palladium, are traded as safe-haven assets, inflation hedges, industrial inputs and speculative vehicles. Gold is by far the largest market: it is held by central banks as a reserve asset, bought as jewelry and investment worldwide, and traded around the clock as XAU/USD, futures and exchange-traded funds. Its price responds to interest rates, the US dollar, central bank buying and fear, while silver and platinum add a strong industrial component that makes them more volatile.

Key takeaways

  • Gold pays no interest, so its price has historically been sensitive to real (inflation-adjusted) interest rates and to the US dollar.
  • Central bank purchases became a major driver from 2022 onward, weakening gold's traditional link to real yields.
  • Gold can be traded as spot XAU/USD through brokers, as futures, through physically backed ETFs or as physical bars and coins, each with different costs.
  • Silver, platinum and palladium are smaller, more volatile markets driven partly by industrial demand.
  • The gold/silver ratio is a popular relative-value gauge, but it can stay at extremes for years.

How gold is quoted and traded

Gold is priced in US dollars per troy ounce, which is about 31.1 grams. In forex-style trading it appears as XAU/USD, quoted like a currency pair, where XAU is the code for one troy ounce of gold. Most of the world's wholesale trading happens in London's over-the-counter market, with the LBMA Gold Price, set by auction twice each London business day, used as a benchmark. Futures trade on COMEX (part of CME Group), the Shanghai Futures Exchange and other venues, and the Shanghai Gold Exchange is a major physical hub. Gold trades nearly 24 hours a day on weekdays as the market passes from Asia to London to New York.

Worked example: a micro gold futures trade

The COMEX Micro Gold contract represents 10 troy ounces, so each $1.00 move in the gold price is worth $10 per contract. A trader buys one contract at $3,000.00 and sells at $3,045.00.

  • Gain: (3,045 − 3,000) × 10 = 45 × 10 = $450.
  • Had gold fallen to $2,970.00 instead: (2,970 − 3,000) × 10 = −$300.

The contract's value is $30,000 (3,000 × 10), while the margin required is a small fraction of that, so moves of 1% to 2%, common for gold in a day of major news, can be a large share of the margin. Prices here are illustrative; see commodity futures for contract details and leverage and margin for how margin works. On the 100-ounce standard contract, the same moves would be ten times larger: $4,500 and −$3,000.

What drives the gold price

Real interest rates

Because gold yields nothing, the cost of holding it is the interest an investor gives up. When real yields, nominal bond yields minus expected inflation, rise, that cost rises and gold has historically tended to fall. When real yields fall, especially when they turn negative, gold has tended to do well. The yield on US Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) is the most common gauge. See central banks and interest rates.

The US dollar

Gold is priced in dollars, so a stronger dollar makes it more expensive in other currencies and tends to weigh on demand. The relationship is a tendency, not a rule: in severe crises, gold and the dollar can rise together. See intermarket analysis.

Central bank buying

Central banks hold gold as a reserve asset that carries no credit risk and cannot be frozen in the way foreign-currency reserves can. According to the World Gold Council, net central bank purchases exceeded 1,000 tonnes a year in 2022, 2023 and 2024, roughly double the pace of the previous decade, led by emerging-market central banks such as those of China, Poland, Turkey and India. This official demand is widely seen as the main reason gold rose sharply after 2022 even as real yields climbed. Gold set a long series of record highs in 2024 and 2025.

Fear and safe-haven demand

War, financial crises, banking stress and political uncertainty tend to bring buying into gold. These spikes can fade once the immediate fear passes.

Jewelry, technology and investment demand

Jewelry demand, concentrated in China and India, tends to rise when prices fall and weaken when prices spike. ETF flows, bar and coin buying, and positioning in futures by funds can amplify moves in either direction. The CFTC's Commitments of Traders report shows how speculators are positioned in COMEX gold; see market sentiment indicators.

Silver

Silver is both a precious and an industrial metal. Roughly half or more of its demand comes from industrial uses, including electronics and solar panels, so it is more sensitive to economic growth than gold. It is also a much smaller market, which makes it more volatile: daily moves of several percent are common. Silver tends to follow gold's direction but with larger swings. COMEX silver futures represent 5,000 troy ounces in the standard contract, with smaller versions available.

Platinum and palladium

Platinum and palladium are used mainly in catalytic converters that clean vehicle exhaust, along with jewelry, chemicals and, increasingly for platinum, hydrogen-related technology. Supply is highly concentrated: South Africa produces most of the world's platinum, and Russia and South Africa together dominate palladium. That concentration makes both metals vulnerable to mining disruptions, power shortages and sanctions. Demand depends heavily on the car industry, so a shift toward battery electric vehicles, which do not need catalytic converters, is a long-term headwind. Both trade as futures on NYMEX and as spot contracts and ETFs, with thinner liquidity than gold or silver.

The gold/silver ratio

The gold/silver ratio is the number of ounces of silver needed to buy one ounce of gold: gold price divided by silver price. It has spent much of the past few decades between roughly 50 and 90, and spiked above 100 during the March 2020 market panic. Some traders treat a high ratio as a sign that silver is cheap relative to gold and a low ratio as the reverse.

Worked example: the ratio in action

Suppose gold is at $3,000 and silver at $37.50.

  • Ratio = 3,000 / 37.50 = 80.
  • If the ratio falls to 70 while gold stays at $3,000, silver must rise to 3,000 / 70 = about $42.86, a gain of about 14.3%.
  • If instead the ratio rises to 90 with gold unchanged, silver falls to 3,000 / 90 = about $33.33, a loss of about 11.1%.

A ratio trade usually means buying one metal and selling the other in matching dollar amounts, so the trader profits or loses on the relative move rather than on the direction of metals overall. There is no fixed "normal" level, and the ratio can stay stretched for years.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming gold always rises in crises. In sharp sell-offs, gold can fall at first as investors sell what they can to raise cash.
  • Relying on a single driver, such as real yields, when central bank demand has changed the picture.
  • Ignoring financing and roll costs on leveraged positions held for months.
  • Treating silver like gold. Its larger swings require smaller position sizes.
  • Overpaying for physical metal through high dealer premiums, or ignoring storage and resale costs.

FAQ

What makes the price of gold go up?

Falling real interest rates, a weaker US dollar, central bank purchases, safe-haven demand during crises and strong investment flows have historically been the main drivers. Their relative importance changes over time.

Is gold a good hedge against inflation?

Over very long periods gold has broadly kept its purchasing power, but over shorter periods it has often failed to track inflation. It tends to do best when inflation is high and real interest rates are low or falling.

What is XAU/USD?

XAU/USD is the price of one troy ounce of gold in US dollars, quoted like a currency pair on forex and CFD platforms. A rise in XAU/USD means gold is getting more expensive in dollars.

Why is silver more volatile than gold?

Silver's market is much smaller and a large share of its demand is industrial, so it reacts to both economic growth and safe-haven flows. Smaller markets move more on the same flow of money.
