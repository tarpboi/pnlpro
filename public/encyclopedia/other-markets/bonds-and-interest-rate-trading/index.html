Bonds and Interest Rate Trading

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Commodities, Bonds & More

  • Gold & Precious Metals

  • Bonds & Rates
  • Stock Index Trading
  • Volatility & VIX

A bond is a loan to a government or company that pays interest (the coupon) and returns the face value at maturity. Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions, and government bond yields form the base interest rates against which almost every other asset is priced. Traders trade bonds and interest rate futures to express views on central bank policy, inflation and growth, and every trader benefits from understanding them, because a large move in yields can reprice stocks, currencies, gold and crypto at the same time.

Key takeaways

  • When yields rise, existing bond prices fall, and the longer the bond's maturity, the larger the price change.
  • Duration measures a bond's sensitivity to yield changes: a modified duration of 8 means about an 8% price move for a 1 percentage point change in yield.
  • The yield curve shows yields across maturities; an inverted curve, with short rates above long rates, has historically preceded many US recessions, but not with reliable timing.
  • Government bond futures (Treasuries, Bunds, Gilts, JGBs) and short-term rate futures (SOFR, fed funds, €STR, SONIA) are the main trading instruments.
  • Bond yields are the discount rate for other assets, so sharp bond moves spill over into stocks, currencies and commodities.

Prices and yields

A bond's yield to maturity is the annual return an investor earns if they buy at today's price and hold to maturity, receiving all coupons. If market interest rates rise after a bond is issued, its fixed coupon becomes less attractive, so its price must fall until its yield matches new bonds. If rates fall, the price rises.

Bond prices are usually quoted as a percentage of face value. A price of 98 means $980 for a bond with $1,000 face value. Yields are quoted in percent, and changes are often measured in basis points (bp), where 1 bp = 0.01 percentage point.

Duration and convexity

Duration summarizes how sensitive a bond's price is to changes in yield. Modified duration gives the approximate percentage price change for a 1 percentage point change in yield. Longer maturities and lower coupons mean higher duration.

Worked example: duration in action

Take a 10-year bond with a 4% annual coupon, priced at 100 (yield 4%). Its modified duration is about 8.1.

  • Estimated price change if the yield rises 0.50 point to 4.50%: −8.11 × 0.50% = about −4.06%, so a price near 95.94.
  • Actual price at a 4.50% yield, discounting each cash flow: about 96.04, a fall of 3.96%.

The small gap between the estimate and the actual price is convexity: the price-yield relationship is curved, so prices fall slightly less than duration predicts when yields rise, and rise slightly more when yields fall.

A 2-year bond with the same coupon has a modified duration of only about 1.9, so the same 0.50 point rise would cost about 0.95%. This is why long-dated bonds can be surprisingly volatile. In 2022, as yields rose rapidly, many long-dated government bonds fell by more than many stock indices.

The yield curve

The yield curve plots yields of the same issuer's bonds against maturity, usually from 3 months to 30 years.

Shape Description Usual interpretation
Normal (upward-sloping) Long yields above short yields Investors demand extra yield for lending longer; growth expected
Flat Similar yields across maturities Transition; uncertainty about the outlook
Inverted Short yields above long yields Markets expect rate cuts, often because they expect a slowdown

Common measures are the 2s10s spread (10-year yield minus 2-year yield) and the 3-month to 10-year spread. In the US, inverted curves have preceded most recessions since the 1960s, but the lag has varied from several months to about two years. The US 2s10s curve inverted in mid-2022 and stayed inverted for about two years, one of the longest inversions on record, and it was not followed by a recession in the usual time frame. The signal is useful context, not a timer.

Traders also describe curve moves: steepening (long yields rise relative to short, or short yields fall faster) and flattening (the reverse). "Bull steepening" typically happens when markets expect rate cuts; "bear flattening" when they expect hikes.

Trading instruments

Government bond futures

Bond futures let traders take leveraged positions on government bond prices without buying the bonds themselves.

Market Main contracts Exchange
US Treasuries 2-year, 5-year, 10-year, Ultra 10-year, Treasury bond, Ultra bond CBOT (CME Group)
German Bunds Schatz (2-year), Bobl (5-year), Bund (10-year), Buxl (30-year) Eurex
Other euro area Italian BTP and French OAT futures Eurex
UK Gilts Long Gilt ICE Futures Europe
Japanese government bonds 10-year JGB Osaka Exchange
Australian bonds 3-year and 10-year ASX

The US 10-year Treasury note future, for example, has a face value of $100,000 and is quoted in points and fractions of 32nds. Bond futures are physically settled against a basket of eligible bonds, and pricing involves the "cheapest-to-deliver" bond, so they do not track any single yield perfectly. Most traders close or roll positions before the delivery month. See futures basics.

Short-term interest rate futures

These contracts trade expectations for central bank policy rates. Their prices are quoted as 100 minus the expected rate.

  • SOFR futures (CME): based on the Secured Overnight Financing Rate, the main US short-term benchmark. They replaced Eurodollar futures, which stopped trading after June 2023. The three-month contract is worth $25 per basis point.
  • 30-day fed funds futures (CBOT): based on the average effective federal funds rate in a month, worth about $41.67 per basis point. Widely used to compute meeting-by-meeting probabilities, as shown in central banks and interest rates.
  • Europe and UK: three-month Euribor and €STR futures (mainly on Eurex and ICE), and SONIA futures in the UK.

Worked example: a SOFR futures trade

A three-month SOFR future trades at 96.25, implying an average SOFR of 100 − 96.25 = 3.75% over its reference quarter. A trader expects the Fed to cut more than markets assume and buys one contract.

  • Weak data arrive, and the implied rate falls to 3.50%. The futures price rises to 96.50.
  • Gain: 25 basis points × $25 = $625 per contract.
  • If instead the implied rate rose to 4.00% (price 96.00), the loss would be 25 × $25 = $625.

Other ways in

Retail traders can also use bond ETFs, which hold portfolios of bonds by maturity or credit type, buy government bonds directly through brokers or government programs in some countries, or use CFDs on bond futures where those are available. Bond ETFs never mature, so their duration stays roughly constant, unlike an individual bond whose duration shrinks as it ages.

Spreads and credit

Not all bonds carry the same risk. The extra yield over a benchmark government bond is a spread.

  • Credit spreads measure the extra yield on corporate bonds. They widen in recessions and crises as default risk rises, and are watched as a stress gauge.
  • Sovereign spreads compare governments, for example Italian versus German 10-year yields in the euro area, a closely watched measure of regional stress.
  • Breakeven inflation is the gap between nominal government bond yields and inflation-linked bond yields (such as US TIPS or UK index-linked gilts), a market estimate of future inflation.

How bond moves affect other markets

  • Stocks: rising yields raise the discount rate on future earnings and tend to pressure valuations, especially for growth stocks. See stock indices trading.
  • Currencies: rising yields relative to other countries tend to support a currency.
  • Gold: rising real yields have historically weighed on gold, though that link weakened after 2022.
  • Financial stability: sudden bond sell-offs can stress leveraged investors. In September 2022, after the UK government's "mini-budget," long-dated gilt yields jumped sharply in a few days, forcing pension funds using leveraged liability-driven investment strategies to sell assets, and the Bank of England stepped in with temporary bond purchases.

The intermarket analysis article covers these links in more depth.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking bonds are always safe. Government bonds carry little default risk in major economies, but long-dated bonds can lose a lot when yields rise.
  • Ignoring duration when comparing positions; one long-bond future can carry much more rate risk than one 2-year future.
  • Treating the yield curve as a precise recession timer.
  • Confusing price and yield direction when reading headlines ("yields up" means prices down).
  • Forgetting delivery and roll dates on bond futures.

FAQ

Why do bond prices fall when interest rates rise?

Existing bonds pay fixed coupons. When new bonds offer higher rates, older bonds must fall in price until their yield matches the market, so that buyers are indifferent between them.

What does an inverted yield curve mean?

It means short-term yields are higher than long-term yields, usually because markets expect central banks to cut rates in the future. In the US it has preceded most recessions since the 1960s, but with highly variable timing and some false signals.

What is duration in bonds?

Duration measures how sensitive a bond's price is to changes in interest rates. A modified duration of 6 means the price will move about 6% for each 1 percentage point change in yield.

How do you trade interest rates?

The most common instruments are government bond futures, short-term rate futures such as SOFR or SONIA futures, bond ETFs and, in some countries, CFDs. Each lets traders express views on the direction of yields or central bank policy.
