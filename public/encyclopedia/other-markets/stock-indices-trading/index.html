Stock Index Trading

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Commodities, Bonds & More

  • Gold & Precious Metals

A stock index tracks the combined value of a group of shares, such as the 500 large US companies in the S&P 500 or the 40 largest German companies in the DAX. Indices summarize how a market or sector is doing, serve as benchmarks for funds, and are among the most heavily traded instruments in the world through futures, exchange-traded funds, CFDs and options. Trading an index rather than a single stock removes company-specific surprises but leaves full exposure to the market's swings, and the way an index is weighted changes what it really measures.

Key takeaways

  • Most major indices are weighted by market capitalization, but the Dow Jones Industrial Average and the Nikkei 225 are price-weighted, which makes high-priced stocks dominate.
  • Market-cap weighting means a handful of very large companies can drive much of an index's movement.
  • Index futures, ETFs, CFDs and options give different combinations of leverage, cost, trading hours and access.
  • Index rebalancing and reconstitution create predictable trading flows, especially at the close on rebalance days.
  • Indices remove single-company risk but not market risk; leveraged index trading still carries large drawdown risk.

Other widely traded indices include Australia's S&P/ASX 200, South Korea's KOSPI, India's Nifty 50 and Sensex, and mainland China's CSI 300. Index rules, constituent counts and weight caps are set by the index providers and change from time to time, so check the provider's methodology for current details. The stock market basics article covers exchanges and how share prices are set.

Why weighting matters

Worked example: price-weighted versus cap-weighted

Imagine a two-stock index:

  • Stock A: price $500, 1 billion shares, market cap $500 billion.
  • Stock B: price $50, 20 billion shares, market cap $1,000 billion.

Price-weighted (like the Dow or Nikkei): index = (500 + 50) / 2 = 275.

  • If A rises 10% to $550: (550 + 50) / 2 = 300, a gain of 9.09%.
  • If B rises 10% to $55: (500 + 55) / 2 = 277.5, a gain of 0.91%.

Cap-weighted (like the S&P 500 or DAX): total value = $1,500 billion.

  • If A rises 10%: +$50 billion, so the index rises 50 / 1,500 = 3.33%.
  • If B rises 10%: +$100 billion, so the index rises 100 / 1,500 = 6.67%.

In the price-weighted index, the smaller company dominates simply because its share price is higher. In the cap-weighted index, the larger company dominates. Neither is "correct," but traders need to know which stocks actually move the index they trade.

Concentration

Market-cap weighting means index performance can depend on a few giants. In 2024 and 2025, the ten largest companies in the S&P 500, mostly large technology firms, made up more than a third of the index's weight. A trader buying the S&P 500 is therefore making a large bet on those companies. Equal-weight versions of major indices exist for those who want broader exposure, and comparing the two shows how narrow or broad a rally is.

Price return versus total return

Most US, UK and Japanese headline indices are price indices, which exclude dividends. The DAX is a total return (performance) index that assumes dividends are reinvested, which is one reason it can look stronger over long periods than price indices. When comparing returns, check which version you are looking at.

Index futures

US index futures trade on CME Group: the E-mini S&P 500 is worth $50 per index point and the Micro E-mini $5 per point; the E-mini Nasdaq-100 is $20 per point and the Micro $2. Eurex lists DAX futures in standard (€25 per point), mini (€5) and micro (€1) sizes, as well as Euro Stoxx 50 futures. Nikkei 225 futures trade on the Osaka Exchange, SGX and CME. The index futures article covers contract specifications, fair value and the overnight session.

Worked example: a Micro E-mini S&P 500 trade

A trader buys one Micro E-mini S&P 500 contract at 6,000.00 and sells at 6,030.00.

  • Gain: 30 points × $5 = $150.
  • Contract value at entry: 6,000 × $5 = $30,000.
  • If the index had instead fallen 2% to 5,880, the loss would be 120 × $5 = $600, which could be a large share of the margin posted.

ETFs and options

Index ETFs such as those tracking the S&P 500 or Nasdaq-100 are the simplest route for most investors. See ETFs and index trading for how they work and why leveraged ETFs lose value in choppy markets. Index options on the S&P 500 (SPX), its smaller version (XSP), the Nasdaq-100 (NDX) and others are widely used for hedging and volatility trading, and very short-dated contracts have become popular; see 0DTE options.

Trading hours and sessions

Cash indices are calculated only while their home exchange is open, but futures and many CFDs trade almost around the clock. This means a trader can react to a US data release with DAX futures, or to Asian news with S&P 500 futures, outside the cash session. Liquidity is thinner overnight, and moves can be sharp. See market hours and sessions.

Index rebalancing

Index providers periodically update constituents and weights:

  • Scheduled reviews: the S&P 500 rebalances quarterly, effective after the third Friday of March, June, September and December. The FTSE 100, DAX and many others review quarterly. The Nasdaq-100 reconstitutes annually in December. FTSE Russell announced a move from annual to semiannual reconstitution of its US indices starting in 2026; check the provider's current calendar.
  • Additions and deletions: when a company joins a major index, index funds must buy it. Prices have historically tended to rise between the announcement and the effective date, though this "index effect" has become smaller as traders anticipate it.
  • Closing auction flows: on rebalance days, index funds trade at the close, so closing auction volumes can be many times normal.
  • Special rebalances: providers can rebalance outside the normal schedule when concentration rules are breached, as the Nasdaq-100 did in July 2023.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming an index represents "the market" when a few stocks dominate it.
  • Comparing price and total return indices as if they were the same.
  • Ignoring weighting differences, such as how one high-priced stock can move the Nikkei or the Dow.
  • Over-leveraging index CFDs or futures because indices feel safer than single stocks.
  • Holding leveraged ETFs long-term without understanding volatility decay.

FAQ

What is the best index to trade?

There is no single best index. The S&P 500 and Nasdaq-100 have the deepest futures and options markets, while the DAX, FTSE 100, Nikkei 225 and Hang Seng are popular for trading during European and Asian hours.

What is the difference between a price-weighted and a market-cap weighted index?

A price-weighted index gives more influence to stocks with higher share prices, regardless of company size. A market-cap weighted index gives more influence to companies with larger total market value.

Can you trade indices 24 hours a day?

Index futures and many CFDs trade nearly 24 hours on weekdays, with short daily breaks, even though the underlying stock exchanges are open only during local hours. Liquidity is lower outside main sessions.

How do index rebalances affect stock prices?

When a company is added to a major index, index-tracking funds must buy its shares, which has historically pushed prices up around the change. Rebalance days also bring very high trading volume at the close.
