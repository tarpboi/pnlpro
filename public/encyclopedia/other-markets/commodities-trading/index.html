Commodities Trading

8 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Commodities, Bonds & More

  • Gold & Precious Metals

  • Volatility & VIX

Commodities are raw materials, such as crude oil, natural gas, gold, copper, wheat, coffee and cattle, traded in standardized form on exchanges around the world. Their prices are set by physical supply and demand, which makes them sensitive to weather, wars, inventories, shipping and economic growth in ways that stocks and currencies are not. Traders reach commodities through futures, exchange-traded products, CFDs, options and shares of producing companies, and each route behaves differently, especially because of the cost of storing and rolling physical goods over time.

Key takeaways

  • Most commodity prices are discovered in futures markets, so even ETFs and CFDs are usually linked to futures and inherit their quirks.
  • Prices are driven by physical supply and demand: production, inventories, weather, growth, the US dollar and geopolitics.
  • Scheduled reports, such as weekly US oil inventory data and monthly crop estimates, are major volatility events.
  • The shape of the futures curve, contango or backwardation, can add or subtract a large return over time, independent of the spot price.
  • Commodities are volatile and often leveraged; physical delivery rules, limit moves and sudden supply shocks add risks unknown in many other markets.

The main commodity groups

Group Examples Main exchanges (examples) Key drivers
Energy WTI and Brent crude, natural gas, gasoline, heating oil, diesel CME Group (NYMEX), ICE OPEC+ policy, inventories, refinery demand, weather, geopolitics
Precious metals Gold, silver, platinum, palladium CME Group (COMEX), Shanghai Futures Exchange, Osaka Exchange Real interest rates, the dollar, central bank and investment demand, industrial use
Base (industrial) metals Copper, aluminum, nickel, zinc London Metal Exchange (LME), CME Group, Shanghai Futures Exchange Chinese and global industrial demand, mine supply, energy costs
Grains and oilseeds Corn, wheat, soybeans, soybean oil and meal, palm oil CME Group (CBOT), Euronext, Dalian Commodity Exchange, Bursa Malaysia Weather, planting and harvest, government policy, biofuel demand

Other large markets include India's Multi Commodity Exchange, the Zhengzhou Commodity Exchange in China and Japan's Osaka Exchange, which took over the former Tokyo Commodity Exchange's main contracts in 2020. Chinese exchanges trade very large volumes, but foreign access is limited to certain contracts.

Ways to trade commodities

Route How it works Advantages Drawbacks
Futures Standardized contracts to buy or sell a set quantity at a future date, traded on exchanges Direct exposure, deep liquidity in major contracts, central clearing, leverage Large contract sizes, expiry and rolling, margin calls, delivery risk if held too long
Micro and mini futures Smaller versions of major contracts, such as micro crude or micro gold Lower capital needed Somewhat wider relative costs; same roll issues
ETFs and ETCs Exchange-traded funds or commodities that hold physical metal or futures Trade like shares, no margin calls, no expiry for the holder Futures-based funds suffer roll costs; fees; tracking error
CFDs and spread bets Broker contracts that track the price, available in many countries but not to US retail Small sizes, easy access Financing or embedded roll costs, counterparty risk, broker spreads; most retail CFD accounts lose money
Options on futures or ETFs Rights to buy or sell at a set price Defined risk for buyers, ability to trade volatility Complexity, time decay
Producer and miner stocks Shares of oil companies, gold miners, agricultural firms No expiry; may pay dividends Company-specific risk; equity market moves; operating leverage to price
Physical Bars, coins, sometimes stored by a dealer No counterparty if held directly (precious metals only, in practice) Dealer premiums, storage and insurance, wide buy-sell spreads

The commodity futures article covers contract specifications in detail, and futures basics explains margin and daily settlement. CFDs and spread betting covers the broker-based route.

Worked example: a micro crude oil futures trade

A trader buys one Micro WTI crude oil contract, which represents 100 barrels, at $70.00 a barrel. Each $0.01 move is worth $1.00, and each $1.00 move is worth $100.

  • If oil rises to $73.50, the gain is 3.50 × 100 = $350.
  • If oil falls to $67.00, the loss is 3.00 × 100 = $300.

The exchange requires initial margin that is only a fraction of the $7,000 contract value (70 × 100), so a move of a few percent can be a large share of the margin posted. Margin levels change with volatility; check the exchange's current figures. The standard contract is ten times larger, 1,000 barrels, where a $1.00 move is worth $1,000.

What drives commodity prices

Supply

Supply depends on production capacity, which often takes years to change. A new copper mine can take a decade or more to develop, and a new oil field several years. In the short term, supply responds to strikes, accidents, weather, war, sanctions, export bans and decisions by producer groups such as OPEC+. Because supply is slow to adjust, small changes in demand can cause large price moves.

Demand

Demand tracks economic growth, especially industrial activity. China's rapid growth in the 2000s drove what is often called a commodity supercycle, and Chinese demand data remains a key driver for metals and energy. Policy matters too: biofuel mandates affect corn, soybean oil and palm oil, and the energy transition affects copper, lithium, nickel and silver.

Inventories

Inventories act as a buffer between supply and demand. Low stocks make prices jumpy and tend to lift near-term prices relative to later ones. High stocks dampen price moves. Many key reports are essentially inventory updates.

The US dollar and interest rates

Most commodities are priced in US dollars, so a stronger dollar makes them more expensive for buyers elsewhere and tends to weigh on prices, as described in intermarket analysis. Interest rates matter through the cost of holding inventory and, for gold, the opportunity cost of holding an asset with no yield.

Speculative positioning

Funds and other speculators can amplify trends. The CFTC's weekly Commitments of Traders report shows how they are positioned in US futures; see market sentiment indicators.

Seasonality

Many commodities follow seasonal patterns linked to weather and usage:

  • Natural gas: demand peaks in winter for heating, and in some regions in summer for air-conditioning; storage builds in spring and summer.
  • Gasoline: demand tends to rise into the Northern Hemisphere summer driving season.
  • Grains: prices are sensitive to weather during planting and the growing season, and harvest pressure often weighs on prices in the autumn for Northern Hemisphere crops. South American crops run on the opposite calendar.
  • Heating oil: tends to see stronger demand ahead of winter.

Seasonal tendencies are averages, not schedules. A single drought, mild winter or supply shock can overwhelm them, and widely known seasonal patterns are usually already reflected in futures prices for later months.

Report schedules shift around holidays, and US government shutdowns have delayed some releases in the past, so check the publisher's calendar. As with economic data, prices move on the difference from expectations, not on the raw number. The oil and energy trading article covers the inventory reports in more detail.

Storage, term structure and roll costs

Unlike a share, a physical commodity costs money to hold: storage, insurance and financing. This shapes the futures curve:

  • Contango: later contracts are more expensive than nearby ones, typical when supply is ample and storage costs dominate.
  • Backwardation: nearby contracts are more expensive, typical when supply is tight and buyers pay up for immediate delivery.

Anyone holding futures exposure over time must roll, selling the expiring contract and buying a later one. In contango, each roll means selling low and buying high, which drags on returns even if the spot price is flat. In backwardation, rolling can add return. See contango, backwardation and rollover for the mechanics.

Worked example: roll cost in a futures-based ETF

A fund rolls oil futures every month. Suppose each month the next contract trades 1.5% above the expiring one, and the spot price ends the year exactly where it started.

  • After each roll, the fund holds 1 / 1.015 as many barrels' worth of exposure as before, for the same money.
  • After 12 rolls: (1 / 1.015)^12 = 1 / 1.1956 = about 0.836.
  • The fund loses roughly 16% over the year from roll costs alone, before fees, while the spot price is unchanged.

This is why long-term holders of futures-based commodity funds sometimes see returns far below the headline price change, and why physically backed funds, practical mainly for precious metals, behave differently.

Risks specific to commodities

  • Supply shocks: a war, sanctions or a weather event can move prices by double digits in days.
  • Limit moves and trading halts: some contracts have daily price limits. In March 2022, the LME suspended nickel trading and canceled trades after prices more than doubled within about a day.
  • Physical delivery: holding certain futures into the delivery period can create an obligation to take or make delivery. Retail brokers usually force-close positions beforehand, but traders must know the dates.
  • Negative prices: in April 2020, the expiring WTI crude oil contract settled below zero, at about −$37 a barrel, as storage filled up. See leverage risk and black swans.
  • Concentrated production: softs such as cocoa depend on a few countries. Cocoa futures more than tripled across 2023 and 2024 after poor West African harvests.
  • Leverage: small contract margins mean small price moves have large effects on account equity.

Common mistakes

  • Buying a futures-based ETF for the long term without understanding roll costs.
  • Forgetting expiry and delivery dates on futures.
  • Treating seasonality as a guarantee.
  • Trading inventory reports without knowing the consensus, or ignoring the earlier industry estimate.
  • Oversizing positions because the margin required looks small compared with the contract's value.

FAQ

What is the easiest way to trade commodities?

For many beginners, exchange-traded funds or ETCs are the most accessible route because they trade like shares and have no margin calls. Futures-based funds can lag the spot price because of roll costs, so it is important to check how a fund gets its exposure.

Do commodity prices follow the stock market?

Sometimes. Industrial commodities such as oil and copper often rise with growth expectations, as stocks do, but supply shocks can push commodities up while stocks fall. Gold often behaves differently from both.

What is contango in commodities?

Contango is when futures for later delivery trade above nearer ones, usually because of storage and financing costs. It makes rolling long positions costly over time.

Can you lose more than you invest trading commodities?

Yes, with futures and some leveraged products, because losses are based on the full contract value, not the margin posted. Some regions require retail CFD brokers to offer negative balance protection, but futures accounts generally do not have it.
