Volatility Trading and the VIX

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Commodities, Bonds & More
  • Commodities Trading
  • Gold & Precious Metals
  • Oil & Energy
  • Bonds & Rates
  • Stock Index Trading
  • Volatility & VIX
  • Betting Exchange Trading

The VIX is an index calculated by Cboe Global Markets that measures the stock market's expected volatility over the next 30 days, derived from S&P 500 option prices. It usually rises when stocks fall, which is why it is called the "fear gauge." Traders cannot buy the VIX directly, so they use VIX futures, options and exchange-traded products, and these instruments behave very differently from the index itself. Many traders have lost money by assuming a VIX product will follow the VIX, or by selling volatility without respecting its rare but violent spikes.

Key takeaways

  • The VIX reflects the market's implied volatility for the S&P 500 over the next 30 days, expressed as an annualized percentage.
  • VIX futures usually trade above the spot VIX in calm markets (contango), which makes long volatility products lose value steadily over time.
  • Short volatility strategies tend to earn small, steady gains punctuated by occasional very large losses.
  • In February 2018, a one-day volatility spike wiped out almost all the value of a popular inverse VIX product, an episode known as Volmageddon.
  • Volatility is mean-reverting: spikes have historically faded, but timing the fade has destroyed many accounts.

What the VIX measures

The VIX is calculated from the prices of a wide range of S&P 500 index options with roughly 30 days to expiry. Higher option prices mean traders are paying more for protection and expect larger moves, which raises the VIX. It is a measure of implied volatility, the market's forecast, not of how much prices have actually moved. See implied volatility for the concept.

A VIX reading is an annualized standard deviation in percent. Two rules of thumb convert it into expected moves:

  • Expected one-month move ≈ VIX / √12. With the VIX at 16: 16 / 3.46 ≈ 4.6%.
  • Expected one-day move ≈ VIX / √252 (about VIX / 16). With the VIX at 16: ≈ 1.0%.

These are one-standard-deviation estimates, so the market is expected to move less than that about two-thirds of the time, under simplifying assumptions that real markets often break.

The VIX has a long-run average near 20. It tends to sit in the low teens in calm bull markets and spike in crises: its record close, above 80, came in March 2020, and its intraday record, near 90, in October 2008. On 5 August 2024, during the yen carry trade unwind, it briefly jumped above 60 in early trading before closing far lower, a reminder that spikes can be extreme and short-lived.

Other markets have similar indices: the VSTOXX for the Euro Stoxx 50, the VXN for the Nasdaq-100, the Nikkei Volatility Index for Japan and the ICE BofA MOVE index for US Treasury volatility. The VVIX measures the volatility of the VIX itself.

VIX futures and the term structure

VIX futures, listed on the Cboe Futures Exchange since 2004, settle to a special calculation of the VIX on their expiry date, usually a Wednesday each month. Each contract reflects the market's expectation of where the VIX will be at its expiry, not where it is today.

Term structure Shape When it appears Effect on long futures holders
Contango Later months above earlier months, all above spot Calm markets; most of the time historically Futures drift down toward spot as expiry approaches, creating losses
Backwardation Near months above later months Stress and sell-offs Futures can drift up toward a high spot level, creating gains

Contango is normal because volatility is mean-reverting and option sellers demand a premium for bearing crash risk. The shape of the curve is itself watched as a stress indicator: a flip into backwardation signals that near-term fear dominates. The market sentiment indicators article covers the VIX as a sentiment tool.

Worked example: the cost of contango

Suppose the spot VIX is 14 and the one-month VIX future is 16. A trader buys the future, expecting volatility to rise.

  • If nothing happens and the VIX is still 14 at expiry, the future converges to 14.
  • Loss: (16 − 14) / 16 = 12.5% in one month, even though the VIX never moved.
  • VIX futures are worth $1,000 per index point, so on one standard contract the loss is 2 × $1,000 = $2,000.

Long VIX exchange-traded products face the same problem continuously, because they roll from the front month into the second month every day. In a typical calm year, this roll cost has erased a large share of their value, and long-term charts of such products tend to slope down toward zero, punctuated by brief spikes. Many have undergone repeated reverse splits.

Volatility exchange-traded products

Product type What it does Typical behavior
Long short-term VIX futures ETPs Hold a rolling mix of the first two VIX futures Lose value in calm markets from roll costs; spike in sell-offs
Leveraged long VIX ETPs Target a multiple of the daily move of the futures index Larger spikes and faster decay
Inverse (short) VIX ETPs Target the opposite of the daily move Gain steadily in calm markets; can lose most of their value in a single day
Mid-term VIX futures ETPs Hold futures four to seven months out Less decay and less responsive to spikes

These products track VIX futures indices, not the spot VIX, and leveraged and inverse versions reset daily, which causes further divergence over longer periods. Product designs and leverage levels have changed over time, often after large losses, so read each prospectus before trading.

February 2018: Volmageddon

By early 2018, after a long stretch of very low volatility, inverse VIX products had attracted large inflows from traders who had earned steady gains by betting volatility would stay low. On 5 February 2018, US stocks fell sharply, and the VIX more than doubled in a single session, from around 17 to around 37, its largest one-day percentage rise on record at that time.

The short-term VIX futures index nearly doubled that day. The largest inverse product, an exchange-traded note called XIV issued by Credit Suisse, which aimed to return the inverse of that index's daily move, lost nearly all of its value. The issuer then exercised its right to terminate the note. Another inverse fund lost most of its value and later reduced its target from −1x to −0.5x. Rebalancing by these products, which had to buy VIX futures into the spike, is widely believed to have amplified the move.

The lesson is structural: a product that earns small returns most days by selling insurance can lose everything in one day, and an inverse daily-reset product cannot survive a 100% rise in its underlying index. The leverage risk and black swans article covers similar tail events in other markets.

Long volatility versus short volatility

Long volatility Short volatility
Ways to do it Buy VIX calls, VIX futures, long VIX ETPs, buy S&P 500 puts or straddles Sell options, short VIX futures, inverse VIX ETPs, iron condors
Typical pattern Frequent small losses, rare large gains Frequent small gains, rare large losses
Return skew Positive Negative
Main use Hedging portfolios, crisis bets Collecting premium
Main danger Slow bleed from decay and contango Sudden wipeout in a spike

Short volatility strategies have historically earned a premium over time because investors pay for protection, but the pattern of returns looks like "picking up pennies in front of a steamroller." Long volatility is costly to hold but can offset losses elsewhere in a crash. Sizing is decisive: short volatility positions must be small enough to survive a VIX doubling or tripling in a day. Option sensitivity to volatility is measured by vega; see the options Greeks.

Common mistakes

  • Buying a long VIX ETP and holding it in expectation of a crash, while contango steadily erodes its value.
  • Assuming a VIX product tracks the spot VIX. Products track futures, which often move much less.
  • Selling volatility with too much size because it has "worked every month."
  • Shorting a VIX spike too early. Volatility tends to revert, but it can rise further first.
  • Ignoring daily reset effects on leveraged and inverse products held for weeks.

FAQ

What is a high VIX level?

Readings above about 30 have historically signaled significant stress, and readings above 40 have been rare and associated with crises. The long-run average is near 20, and calm markets often see the VIX in the low to mid teens.

Can you buy the VIX directly?

No. The VIX is a calculated index, not a tradable asset. Traders use VIX futures, VIX options or exchange-traded products that hold VIX futures, all of which behave differently from the index.

Why do VIX ETFs lose value over time?

Most hold short-term VIX futures, which usually trade above the spot VIX. As those futures approach expiry they tend to fall toward the spot level, and the fund repeatedly sells cheaper expiring contracts to buy more expensive later ones, which erodes value.

What happened to XIV in 2018?

On 5 February 2018 the VIX futures index that XIV tracked inversely nearly doubled in one day, wiping out almost all of the note's value. Its issuer, Credit Suisse, then terminated the product.
