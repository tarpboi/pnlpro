Betting Exchange Trading

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Commodities, Bonds & More

A betting exchange is a marketplace where people bet against each other rather than against a bookmaker. Users can back an outcome (bet that it happens) or lay it (bet that it does not), and the exchange matches opposite orders and takes a commission on net winnings. Because odds move continuously before and during events, some users trade them like prices, backing at one price and laying at another to lock in a profit or cap a loss. The mechanics resemble a financial order book, but it remains gambling, legality varies widely by country, and most participants lose money after commission.

Key takeaways

  • Backing means betting for an outcome; laying means betting against it and acting as the bookmaker, with a liability that can be several times the stake.
  • Decimal odds convert to implied probability as 1 divided by the odds, so odds of 2.50 imply 40%.
  • "Greening up" means placing an opposite bet at new odds so the trader makes the same profit, or the same smaller loss, whatever the result.
  • Commission, delays on in-play bets and faster participants make consistent profit difficult.
  • Betting exchanges are licensed in some countries, such as the UK, and unavailable in many others; check local law before using one.

How a betting exchange works

The best-known example is the Betfair Exchange; others include Smarkets, Matchbook and Betdaq. Each market, such as "Match Odds" for a football game, lists every outcome with a ladder of prices on both sides:

  • Back prices: odds at which you can back an outcome, offered by users who want to lay it.
  • Lay prices: odds at which you can lay an outcome, offered by users who want to back it.

This is a two-sided order book with bids and asks, much like the order book of a stock exchange. You can take the best available price immediately or post your own odds and wait to be matched. Popular markets, such as top football leagues, major horse races and tennis Grand Slams, can have deep liquidity; minor events can be thin, with wide gaps between back and lay prices.

Odds and implied probability

Exchanges usually display decimal odds, which include the returned stake. A £10 back bet at 3.00 returns £30 if it wins: £20 profit plus the £10 stake.

Implied probability = 1 / decimal odds.

Decimal odds Implied probability Profit on a £10 back bet
1.50 66.7% £5
2.00 50.0% £10
2.50 40.0% £15
3.00 33.3% £20
5.00 20.0% £40

On a liquid exchange market, the implied probabilities of the best back prices across all outcomes add up to close to 100%, while bookmaker odds typically add up to noticeably more, the extra being the bookmaker's margin. This is similar to how prediction market prices work as probabilities.

Laying and liability

Laying means you take the bookmaker's side. If you lay £10 at 3.00:

  • If the outcome loses, you win the backer's £10 stake.
  • If it wins, you pay the backer's profit: £10 × (3.00 − 1) = £20. This is your liability.

Laying at high odds can carry large liabilities. Laying £10 at 21.0 risks £200 to win £10.

Trading odds movements

Odds change as money flows in and as information arrives: team news, weather, a goal, a red card, a break of serve. Traders try to profit from these moves without caring who wins.

  • Pre-match trading: positioning on expected moves before the event starts, such as odds shortening as money arrives in the final hour before a horse race, or reacting to team announcements.
  • In-play trading: trading during the event, when prices move fastest. Exchanges apply a short bet delay to in-play orders, a few seconds, to reduce the advantage of participants watching faster feeds than the television broadcast.
  • Scalping: trying to capture one or two price ticks repeatedly in liquid markets, a strategy that competes directly with automated traders.

Worked example: greening up a winning trade

A trader backs a team for £100 at 3.00 before a match. The team scores early and its odds shorten to 2.50. The trader wants to lock in a profit whatever happens.

The lay stake that equalizes the outcome is:

Lay stake = back stake × back odds / lay odds = 100 × 3.00 / 2.50 = £120.

Liability on the lay = 120 × (2.50 − 1) = £180.

Result Back bet Lay bet Net
Team wins +£200 (100 × 2.00 profit) −£180 +£20
Team does not win −£100 +£120 +£20

The trader has a guaranteed £20 before commission, shown in green on many exchange interfaces, hence "greening up." If the exchange charges 5% commission on net market winnings, the trader keeps £20 − £1 = £19.

Worked example: cutting a loss

Now suppose the team concedes instead and its odds drift from 3.00 to 3.60.

Lay stake = 100 × 3.00 / 3.60 = £83.33. Liability = 83.33 × 2.60 = £216.67.

  • Team wins: +£200 − £216.67 = −£16.67.
  • Team does not win: −£100 + £83.33 = −£16.67.

The trader accepts a certain £16.67 loss instead of risking the full £100. This is the betting equivalent of a stop loss. No commission is charged, since the market is a net loss.

Costs and practical frictions

  • Commission: exchanges charge a percentage of net winnings on each market, typically a few percent, with rates varying by exchange, country, market and account. Some exchanges add extra charges for consistently profitable customers. Check the current terms.
  • Spread between back and lay: in thin markets, crossing the gap costs money on every trade.
  • Bet delays and suspensions: markets suspend at key moments, such as goals or penalties, so you cannot exit during the biggest moves.
  • Faster competition: automated traders, professional syndicates and people with low-latency data have an edge in the busiest markets.
  • Ambiguous rules: as with any event contract, settlement rules (for example, whether extra time counts) decide who is paid. Read them.

Comparison with prediction markets

Settlement Exchange settles from official results Resolution rules and sometimes oracles; see prediction markets explained

In the US, sports-related event contracts on CFTC-regulated venues have become a contested alternative to traditional sports betting, with legal challenges from several states as of 2026. See prediction market regulation for the current picture.

Legality and taxes

Betting exchanges operate under gambling licenses in jurisdictions such as the UK, Ireland, Australia and several other countries, and are restricted or unavailable in many others, including most of the US. Rules change, so check the law where you live and the exchange's terms. Tax treatment also varies: in the UK, betting winnings are generally not taxed for individuals as of 2026, while many other countries tax them as income. See a qualified adviser for your situation.

Gambling risk

Exchange trading can feel like financial trading, but it is gambling, and fast in-play markets can encourage chasing losses. Set a fixed budget, use deposit limits and time-outs where the exchange offers them, and stop if betting stops being affordable or enjoyable. Help is available from problem gambling services in many countries, such as GamCare in the UK and the National Council on Problem Gambling in the US. The handling losses and tilt article covers warning signs.

Common mistakes

  • Laying at long odds without realizing the liability can be many times the potential gain.
  • Ignoring commission when calculating whether a small trade is worth it.
  • Trading illiquid markets where back and lay prices are far apart.
  • Letting a losing position run instead of cutting it, hoping the odds will come back.
  • Assuming in-play edges that faster, automated participants have already taken.

FAQ

What is the difference between backing and laying?

Backing is betting that an outcome will happen. Laying is betting that it will not, which means you act as the bookmaker and pay the backer's winnings if the outcome does happen.

What does greening up mean?

Greening up means placing an opposite bet at new odds so that you make the same profit whatever the result. It is usually done after the odds have moved in your favor, by laying an outcome you previously backed at higher odds.

Is betting exchange trading profitable?

A small number of skilled or well-resourced traders profit, but most participants lose money after commission, spreads and competition from faster traders. It should be treated as gambling, not as a reliable income source.

Are betting exchanges legal?

It depends on the country. They are licensed in places such as the UK, Ireland and Australia, but restricted or unavailable in many others, including most of the US, so check local law before using one.
