Forex Trading Basics

Forex, short for foreign exchange and often written FX, is the global market for exchanging one currency for another. It is the largest financial market in the world by turnover, and it runs almost around the clock from Monday morning in Asia to Friday evening in New York. For a retail trader, forex usually means speculating on exchange rates through a broker with borrowed money, which makes it accessible but also one of the easiest places to lose money quickly.

Key takeaways

  • Forex is an over-the-counter market made up of banks, funds, companies and brokers, with no single central exchange.
  • According to the BIS Triennial Survey, global FX turnover was about $7.5 trillion a day in April 2022 and roughly $9.6 trillion a day in April 2025.
  • Currencies always trade in pairs, so every trade is a bet that one currency will strengthen against another.
  • Retail traders reach the market through brokers, usually with high leverage, and most retail leveraged FX and CFD accounts lose money.
  • The main costs are the spread, any commission and the overnight financing charge, called swap or rollover.

What forex is

Every time a currency changes hands, someone is trading foreign exchange. A Japanese carmaker converting dollar sales into yen, a pension fund buying European bonds, a tourist buying euros at an airport and a hedge fund betting on the pound all take part in the same market.

Exchange rates between the major currencies have floated freely since the early 1970s, when the Bretton Woods system of fixed rates broke down. Since then, the price of one currency in terms of another has been set by supply and demand, with occasional intervention by governments and central banks. Some countries still peg or tightly manage their currencies. For example, the Hong Kong dollar has traded in a band of 7.75 to 7.85 per US dollar since 2005.

A price in forex is always a ratio between two currencies, written as a currency pair. If EUR/USD is 1.1000, one euro costs 1.10 US dollars. If the rate rises to 1.1100, the euro has strengthened and the dollar has weakened against it.

How big the market is

The best source on market size is the Triennial Central Bank Survey run by the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) every three years. Central banks collect data from thousands of dealers for the month of April and the BIS publishes the totals.

Survey Average daily FX turnover
April 2022 About $7.5 trillion
April 2025 (preliminary) About $9.6 trillion

A few other patterns from recent surveys are worth knowing:

  • The US dollar dominates. It is on one side of close to 90 percent of all trades. The euro, yen and pound follow at a distance.
  • Most volume is not spot trading. FX swaps, used by banks and companies to manage funding and hedge, make up the largest share. Spot trading, the simple exchange of one currency for another, is a smaller slice.
  • Trading is concentrated in a few cities. The United Kingdom is the largest trading center, followed by the United States, with Singapore and Hong Kong also large.

The headline number is often used to sell forex to beginners as "the most liquid market in the world". That is true for the major pairs at busy times, but the figure includes huge interbank swap volumes that have nothing to do with retail speculation. Retail traders make up a small share of the total.

How the market is structured

Forex is an over-the-counter (OTC) market. There is no central exchange and no single official price. Instead, a network of dealers quotes prices to each other and to clients. This is different from a stock exchange, where every order goes through one central order book.

The market works in tiers:

  1. The interbank and dealer tier. Large global banks quote prices to each other and to big clients through electronic platforms and direct links. Specialist non-bank market makers, including high-frequency trading firms, now provide a large share of this liquidity.
  2. The institutional client tier. Asset managers, hedge funds, corporations and smaller banks trade with dealers, often through multi-dealer platforms.
  3. The retail tier. Individual traders trade with retail brokers, who either take the other side of the trade themselves or pass it on to larger liquidity providers.

Because there is no central exchange, the price you see at one broker can differ slightly from the price at another. There is also no single public record of total volume, which is why technical traders note that volume analysis works differently in forex.

Who trades currencies and why

Participant Main reason for trading
Commercial banks and dealers Making markets, serving clients, managing their own funding
Central banks Managing reserves, setting policy, occasionally intervening
Corporations Paying for imports, converting export revenue, hedging
Asset managers and pension funds Buying foreign assets and hedging currency exposure
Hedge funds and CTAs Speculating on macro trends, carry and momentum
Retail traders Short-term speculation, usually with leverage

Many of these participants are not trying to profit from the trade itself. A company hedging next year's euro revenue cares about certainty, not about beating the market. This mix of motives is part of what keeps the market active. It also means a retail speculator is competing against well-informed dealers and funds with better data and lower costs. The market participants article covers who sits on the other side of a trade in more detail.

Spot, forwards, swaps and retail products

Professional FX uses several instruments:

  • Spot: exchange at today's rate, settled in two business days for most pairs (one day for USD/CAD).
  • Forwards: exchange at a rate agreed today for a later date.
  • FX swaps: a spot trade and a reverse forward trade done together, mainly used for funding.
  • Futures and options: exchange-traded or OTC contracts on currencies, such as the currency futures listed on CME.

Retail traders rarely take delivery of currency. Instead they trade rolling spot contracts or CFDs with a broker. These positions are rolled forward every night so they never settle, and the broker charges or pays a financing amount, called swap or rollover, based on the interest rates of the two currencies. That financing is the basis of the carry trade.

How a retail forex trade works, end to end

Here is a complete example of one trade, using round numbers.

1. The quote. A trader's platform shows EUR/USD at 1.1000 bid and 1.1001 ask. The bid is the price at which you can sell the euro; the ask is the price at which you can buy it. The difference, 0.0001, is one pip, which is the spread.

2. The order. The trader thinks the euro will rise and buys one mini lot, which is 10,000 euros. Buying at the ask means the entry price is 1.1001. The position is worth 10,000 × 1.1001 = $11,001.

3. The margin. The account is in US dollars and the broker allows 30:1 leverage on this pair. Required margin is $11,001 ÷ 30 = $366.70. The rest of the position is effectively borrowed. The pips, lots and leverage article explains these calculations step by step.

4. Holding overnight. The trader keeps the position past the daily rollover time, 5 p.m. New York time. The broker applies a swap, which could be a small credit or debit depending on interest rates and the broker's markup. Assume a charge of $0.80 for the night.

5. Closing. The next day, EUR/USD is quoted at 1.1050 bid and 1.1051 ask. To close a long position, the trader sells at the bid, 1.1050.

6. The result.

Item Amount
Price change 1.1050 − 1.1001 = 0.0049, or 49 pips
Value per pip for 10,000 EUR $1.00
Gross profit 49 × $1.00 = $49.00
Overnight swap −$0.80
Net profit $48.20
Return on margin used $48.20 ÷ $366.70 ≈ 13.1%

The spread was already counted, because the trade was bought at the ask and sold at the bid. If the rate had fallen 49 pips instead, the trader would have lost about the same amount. A move of about 367 pips against the position, small by the standards of a volatile month, would wipe out the whole margin deposit if nothing else was in the account. That is how leverage scales both gains and losses, as covered in leverage and margin.

7. The other side. The broker either matched the trade internally, kept the risk itself, or hedged it with a larger liquidity provider. These models, known as B-book and A-book, are explained in the forex brokers article.

When the market is open

The forex week runs from Sunday evening to Friday evening, New York time. Trading activity passes through the Sydney, Tokyo, London and New York sessions. Liquidity is usually deepest when London and New York are both open and thinnest in the gap after New York closes. Prices can also jump between Friday's close and Sunday's open. See forex trading sessions for times and overlaps.

What moves exchange rates

Over months and years, currencies respond to interest rate differences, inflation, growth and the balance of trade and capital flows. Over minutes and days, they respond to economic data releases, central bank decisions, risk sentiment and positioning. What moves currencies covers each driver, and central banks and interest rates covers the policy side.

What the evidence says about retail outcomes

Since 2018, brokers offering CFDs to retail clients in the European Union have had to publish the percentage of their retail accounts that lose money, and the UK has a similar rule. These disclosures typically show that 70 to 80 percent or more of retail accounts lose money. Leverage, frequent trading, costs and the fact that the other side of many retail trades is a professional all contribute.

That does not mean every trader loses. It means a beginner should treat forex as a skill that takes time, risk small amounts and focus on risk management before strategy.

Common mistakes

  • Using maximum leverage. High leverage makes small, normal price moves large enough to wipe out an account.
  • Ignoring the cost of swaps. Holding positions for weeks against an unfavorable interest rate difference can cost more than the spread.
  • Trading exotic pairs as a beginner. Wide spreads and sudden gaps make them expensive and unpredictable.
  • Choosing a broker on bonus offers. Regulation and the ability to withdraw money matter far more. See forex risks and scams.
  • Trading every session. Spreads widen and moves become erratic in thin hours, especially around the daily rollover.

FAQ

How much money do you need to start trading forex?

Many brokers let you open an account with a small deposit and trade micro lots worth about 1,000 units of currency, where one pip is worth around 10 cents. The real question is how much you can afford to lose, because a small account combined with high leverage tends to be wiped out quickly.

Is forex trading legal?

Retail forex trading is legal in most countries, but it is regulated differently in each. Some countries cap leverage, some restrict products such as CFDs, and some limit trading with foreign brokers, so check the rules where you live.

Is forex a zero-sum game?

Before costs, currency speculation is close to zero-sum, since one side's gain on an exchange rate move is the other side's loss. After spreads, commissions and financing, it is negative-sum for traders as a group.

Why is forex traded in pairs?

A currency only has a price in terms of another currency. Buying one currency always means selling another, so every forex trade is a view on the relative strength of two currencies.
