Forex Risks and Scams

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Forex attracts more than its share of fraud. The market is global and over the counter, leverage makes small deposits look like big opportunities, and online marketing can make an unlicensed firm look as polished as a bank. Beyond the ordinary risk of losing money in a legitimate account, retail traders face fake brokers, clone firms, signal and robot sellers, managed-account schemes and withdrawal traps. Knowing how these work, and how to verify a firm, is the best defense.

Key takeaways

  • Legitimate forex trading is risky in itself; most retail leveraged accounts lose money even with honest, regulated brokers.
  • Fake and clone brokers often look professional, so verify every firm on the regulator's own register, not through links they provide.
  • Promises of guaranteed or steady high returns are the clearest warning sign of fraud.
  • Signal sellers, managed-account offers and trading robots are common routes for scams and for misleading performance claims.
  • If you have been scammed, stop paying, contact your bank and report it, and be very wary of anyone offering to recover your money for a fee.

Legitimate risks first

Even with a well-regulated broker, forex carries real risks:

  • Leverage risk: small price moves create large gains and losses. See leverage and margin.
  • Gap and slippage risk: prices can jump past stops on news or over weekends.
  • Counterparty risk: you trade with the broker, so its solvency matters.
  • Cost drag: spreads, commissions and swaps add up for active traders.

These risks are why EU and UK providers must disclose loss rates that typically show 70 to 80 percent or more of retail CFD accounts losing money. A scam adds the risk of losing everything regardless of how you trade.

Unregulated and offshore brokers

Some brokers have no license at all. Others hold a license from a jurisdiction with minimal supervision, and some offshore authorities state publicly that they do not regulate forex trading even though firms registered there advertise themselves as regulated. Common features:

  • leverage of 500:1 or more and large deposit bonuses;
  • pressure from an "account manager" to deposit more;
  • bonus terms that block withdrawals until an unrealistic trading volume is reached;
  • trading platforms where prices, fills or account balances may not reflect a real market.

The line between a weakly regulated broker and a fraud is not always clear until a client tries to withdraw. The forex brokers article explains how legitimate brokers operate and what strong regulation provides.

Clone firms

A clone firm copies the name, license number, address and website design of a real, authorized firm, then uses a slightly different web address, email or phone number. Victims check the register, see a genuine entry and send money to the fake.

How to spot one: on the regulator's register, use only the contact details listed there. Contact the real firm through those details and confirm the account and payment information. Regulators such as the UK's FCA publish warnings about clone firms.

Signal sellers

Signal services send trade ideas, usually through messaging apps, for a subscription fee. Some are honest attempts to share analysis. Many are not:

  • Cherry-picked results: showing winning trades and quietly deleting losers.
  • Demo-account screenshots presented as real money.
  • Multiple channels: running several groups with opposite calls, then promoting the one that happened to win.
  • Broker kickbacks: the provider earns rebates on every trade members place through a recommended broker, which rewards frequent trading rather than profitable trading.

Real track records are verified, long, and show drawdowns. The trading education and gurus article covers how to evaluate claims, and copy trading and social trading explains why even genuine records can mislead.

Managed-account and "guaranteed return" scams

A common pattern is an offer to trade on your behalf for a share of the profits, promising returns such as 5 or 10 percent a month with little or no risk.

Worked example: why guaranteed monthly returns are implausible

A scheme promises 8 percent per month, compounded.

  • After 12 months: 1.08^12 = 2.518. A $10,000 deposit would become about $25,180.
  • After 5 years: 1.08^60 = about 101.3. The same $10,000 would become about $1,013,000.

No legitimate fund delivers anything close to this consistently. Returns that are both high and steady, with no losing months, are a hallmark of Ponzi schemes, which pay early investors with money from new ones until inflows stop. In the US, managing money in forex for others generally requires registration with the CFTC and membership of the NFA. In other countries, it requires an investment management license.

Variations include requests for your trading account password so a "professional" can trade for you, or "investment plans" with fixed tiers based on how much you deposit.

Trading robots and Expert Advisors

Expert Advisors (EAs) are automated strategies for platforms such as MetaTrader. Legitimate automated trading exists, and the principles are covered in building trading bots. Scams in this area include:

  • Backtests tuned to past data that look perfect and fail live. See overfitting and data snooping.
  • Martingale and grid systems, which double down on losing positions. They produce smooth equity curves for months, then lose most of the account in one adverse move.
  • "AI trading bots" that are really Ponzi schemes, where no trading happens and "profits" are paid from new deposits. In one well-known case, the CFTC charged a South African-based scheme called Mirror Trading International, describing it in 2022 as the largest fraudulent bitcoin scheme it had charged.

A robot sold for a one-time fee that supposedly makes large returns raises an obvious question: why would the seller not just run it themselves?

Withdrawal problems

Many scams only reveal themselves at withdrawal. Typical tactics:

  • demands for a "tax", "fee" or "insurance deposit" before funds can be released;
  • claims that bonus terms have not been met;
  • account freezes for vague "compliance" reasons;
  • staff who stop responding.

Any request to pay money in order to withdraw money is a major warning sign.

How to check a license

  1. Find the full legal name and license number on the broker's website, usually in the footer.
  2. Go directly to the regulator's website by typing the address yourself. Examples include the FCA's Financial Services Register in the UK, the NFA's BASIC database in the US, ASIC's professional registers in Australia and CySEC's register in Cyprus.
  3. Check that the details match: legal name, website address, phone numbers and permitted activities.
  4. Check which entity your account would be with. A group may have a strictly regulated entity and an offshore one.
  5. Search warning lists. Many regulators publish lists of unauthorized firms, and the International Organization of Securities Commissions (IOSCO) runs a portal that collects investor alerts from regulators around the world.

The trading regulation overview lists the main regulators and what they protect.

If you think you have been scammed

  • Stop sending money, including any "fee" to unlock a withdrawal.
  • Contact your bank or card issuer immediately. Card payments and some transfers can sometimes be disputed if you act quickly.
  • Report it to the police and to your financial regulator. In the US, the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) collects reports.
  • Keep records: messages, transaction details, website addresses and screenshots.
  • Beware recovery scams. Victims are often contacted by people claiming they can recover lost funds for an upfront fee. These are usually the same criminals, or others working from lists of past victims. See trading scams.

Common mistakes

  • Trusting a regulator logo. Logos are easy to copy. Verify independently.
  • Being persuaded by early withdrawals. Scams often let you withdraw a small amount to build trust before a larger deposit.
  • Sharing account passwords or remote access with anyone offering to trade for you.
  • Believing that a slick website means a real firm. Professional design costs little.
  • Paying to get money back. Legitimate firms do not charge release fees.

FAQ

How can I tell if a forex broker is a scam?

Check its license directly on the regulator's own register, compare the contact details, and look for warning signs such as guaranteed returns, pressure to deposit more and large bonuses. Test a small withdrawal before depositing more.

Are forex signal groups legit?

Some are honest, but many show cherry-picked or demo results and earn money from subscriptions or broker rebates rather than trading. Look for a long, independently verified track record that includes losing periods.

Do forex robots work?

Some automated strategies work for a time, but robots sold with promises of large, steady profits are usually overfitted, high-risk grid or martingale systems, or outright scams.

Can I get my money back from a forex scam?

Sometimes, if you contact your bank or card provider quickly and report the fraud. Be very cautious of recovery services that ask for upfront fees, as they are often a second scam.
