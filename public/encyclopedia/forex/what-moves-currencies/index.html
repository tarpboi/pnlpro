What Moves Currencies

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Exchange rates move because the supply of and demand for each currency change. Behind those shifts are interest rates, inflation, economic growth, central bank decisions, global risk appetite, trade and capital flows, and occasional government intervention. No single factor explains every move, and the importance of each driver changes over time, but understanding them helps a trader make sense of price action and avoid being surprised by scheduled events.

Key takeaways

  • Interest rate differentials, and especially changes in expected future rates, are among the strongest drivers of major exchange rates.
  • Markets react to surprises relative to expectations, not to whether data is good or bad in absolute terms.
  • In periods of stress, money tends to flow toward perceived safe havens such as the US dollar, Japanese yen and Swiss franc.
  • Commodity prices strongly influence the currencies of major commodity exporters such as Australia, Canada, New Zealand and Norway.
  • Governments and central banks sometimes intervene directly, which can cause sudden, large moves.

Interest rates and rate expectations

Money tends to flow toward currencies that offer higher returns. If US interest rates rise relative to euro-area rates, holding dollars becomes more attractive, and EUR/USD tends to fall.

What matters most is not today's rate but where markets expect rates to go. Short-term government bond yields, such as two-year yields, reflect those expectations, which is why traders often chart the gap between two countries' two-year yields alongside their exchange rate. The tools central banks use, from policy rates to quantitative easing and forward guidance, are covered in central banks and interest rates.

Worked example: how interest rates set forward prices

Interest rates also explain the gap between spot and forward exchange rates, through a relationship called covered interest parity.

  • Spot EUR/USD: 1.1000
  • One-year US dollar interest rate: 4 percent
  • One-year euro interest rate: 2 percent

The one-year forward rate is approximately: 1.1000 × (1.04 ÷ 1.02) = 1.1000 × 1.019608 = 1.1216.

The forward price is higher than spot, so a euro buys more dollars for delivery in one year than it does today. In market terms, the higher-yielding dollar trades at a forward discount. This is not a forecast that the euro will rise. It simply prevents a risk-free profit from borrowing in one currency and lending in the other. A speculator who bets that the higher-yielding currency will not fall by as much as the forward implies is running a carry trade.

Inflation

High inflation erodes a currency's purchasing power. Over long periods, countries with persistently higher inflation have tended to see their currencies weaken, an idea known as purchasing power parity. The Economist's Big Mac index is a well-known, lighthearted illustration of the concept.

In the short term, the effect can run the other way. A higher-than-expected inflation reading often strengthens a currency, because traders expect the central bank to raise rates or keep them high. The market response depends on whether the central bank is seen as willing and able to respond.

Growth and economic data

Strong growth tends to attract investment and supports expectations of higher rates. Key releases include GDP, employment reports, purchasing managers' indexes (PMIs), retail sales and inflation figures. The economic indicators article explains each.

Surprise matters more than direction

Markets price in the consensus forecast before a release. The move comes from the gap between the actual figure and that forecast.

Scenario Consensus Actual Typical first reaction in the currency
Jobs growth beats +150,000 +250,000 Stronger
Jobs growth misses +150,000 +50,000 Weaker
In line +150,000 +155,000 Little lasting change

Revisions to past data, details within the report and the central bank's likely reaction can reverse the first move within minutes. The news trading and economic calendar article covers how traders handle these releases.

Central bank surprises

Central bank meetings are scheduled, and markets usually price in the expected decision. Large moves follow when a bank does something unexpected: a bigger or smaller rate change than forecast, a shift in its guidance, or a change in its asset purchases. Tone matters too. A statement that sounds more willing to raise rates is called hawkish, and one that leans toward cuts is called dovish.

Some of the largest currency moves in modern history came from policy shocks, such as the Swiss National Bank removing its EUR/CHF floor in January 2015, which sent the franc up sharply in minutes.

Risk sentiment and safe havens

When investors are confident, they tend to buy riskier assets, including higher-yielding and emerging-market currencies. This is called a risk-on environment. When fear rises, they cut risk and move money toward assets seen as safe. This is risk-off.

Currency type Examples Typical behavior in risk-off
Safe havens USD, JPY, CHF Tend to strengthen
Commodity and higher-yielding currencies AUD, NZD, CAD, NOK Tend to weaken
Emerging-market currencies MXN, ZAR, TRY, BRL Often weaken sharply

Why these havens? The US dollar is the world's main reserve and funding currency, so demand for dollars rises when markets need cash. Japan and Switzerland have large net foreign assets, and in a crisis their investors tend to bring money home. Carry trades funded in yen are also often unwound in risk-off episodes, which pushes the yen up. These patterns are tendencies and can break down. The intermarket analysis article covers how currencies relate to stocks, bonds and commodities.

Trade balances and capital flows

A country that exports more than it imports receives foreign currency that must be converted back, which supports its own currency. A country with a large current account deficit must attract foreign capital to fund it, which can leave its currency exposed if that funding dries up.

In practice, capital flows, meaning investment in bonds, stocks and companies, are far larger than trade flows for major currencies. That is why trade data rarely moves major pairs much on release day, though persistent imbalances shape long-term trends.

Commodity currencies

Some currencies are closely tied to the prices of their country's main exports:

  • Australian dollar: iron ore, coal and other resources, plus Chinese demand.
  • Canadian dollar: crude oil.
  • Norwegian krone: oil and gas.
  • New Zealand dollar: dairy and other agricultural exports.
  • Chilean peso: copper.

A rise in oil prices tends to support the Canadian dollar, so USD/CAD often falls when crude rises. The link varies in strength and can reverse when other drivers, such as interest rates, dominate. See oil and energy trading.

Intervention and managed currencies

Governments sometimes act directly in the currency market:

  • Direct intervention: buying or selling currency to push the rate. Japan's authorities bought yen in 2022 for the first time since 1998 and intervened again in 2024 when the yen was weak.
  • Coordinated action: major economies acting together, such as the 1985 Plaza Accord to weaken the dollar.
  • Pegs and bands: fixing a currency to another, such as the Hong Kong dollar's band against the US dollar.
  • Managed rates: China's central bank sets a daily reference rate for the onshore yuan and limits how far it can trade from it.

Intervention can cause moves of several percent within hours. Warnings from officials, sometimes called verbal intervention, can move markets on their own.

Politics and positioning

Elections, referendums, fiscal crises and geopolitical conflicts affect currencies through their expected impact on growth, rates and risk. Positioning matters too. When many traders hold the same trade, even modest news can trigger a sharp reversal as they exit together. Data such as the CFTC's Commitments of Traders report, discussed in market sentiment indicators, gives a rough picture of positioning in currency futures.

Common mistakes

  • Trading the headline number. The surprise relative to consensus, and the details, drive the move.
  • Assuming good news always lifts a currency. If the market already priced it in, the currency can fall.
  • Ignoring the other currency in the pair. A strong US report means little if euro-area news is even stronger.
  • Treating relationships as fixed. Correlations between currencies, commodities and yields shift over time.
  • Fighting a central bank. Betting against an active intervention or peg can be costly, though pegs do occasionally break.

FAQ

What is the biggest factor affecting exchange rates?

For major currencies, interest rates and expectations about future rates are usually the most important single driver. Risk sentiment and capital flows can dominate during periods of market stress.

Why does the yen strengthen when markets fall?

Japan is a large net creditor, so Japanese investors tend to bring money home in a crisis. The yen is also a common funding currency for carry trades, and unwinding those trades involves buying yen.

Why do currencies move on inflation data?

Higher inflation can lead a central bank to raise or hold interest rates, which makes the currency more attractive to hold. Markets react to how the figure compares with forecasts.

What are commodity currencies?

They are currencies of countries whose economies depend heavily on commodity exports, such as the Australian dollar, Canadian dollar, Norwegian krone and New Zealand dollar. Their values tend to move with the prices of those commodities.
