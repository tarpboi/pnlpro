CFDs and Spread Betting

A contract for difference (CFD) is an agreement between a trader and a provider to exchange the difference in an asset's price between the time a position is opened and the time it is closed. Spread betting, available mainly in the UK and Ireland, works in a similar way but is structured as a bet of a set amount per point of movement. Both let retail traders take leveraged long or short positions on currencies, indices, commodities and shares without owning the underlying asset, and both come with high loss rates for retail clients.

Key takeaways

  • A CFD pays the price difference on an asset; the trader never owns the asset itself.
  • Costs include the spread, sometimes a commission, and an overnight financing charge on leveraged positions.
  • CFDs are traded over the counter with the provider, so the provider's solvency and conduct matter.
  • In the EU and UK, retail CFD leverage is capped (30:1 on major currency pairs) and providers must disclose the share of retail accounts that lose money, typically 70 to 80 percent or more.
  • CFDs are not available to retail traders in the United States; UK spread betting profits are generally free of capital gains tax for individuals, but rules depend on personal circumstances.

How a CFD works

When you buy a CFD, you agree to receive any rise in the asset's price and pay any fall, multiplied by your position size. Selling a CFD reverses this. You put up margin, a fraction of the position's value, and the provider tracks your profit or loss in real time.

Most retail forex trading outside the United States is done through CFDs or very similar rolling spot contracts. The pip and margin arithmetic is the same, as shown in pips, lots and leverage.

Worked example: a share CFD with financing

A trader buys CFDs equivalent to 1,000 shares of a UK company at £10.00, a position worth £10,000.

  • Margin: under UK retail rules, individual shares are capped at 5:1 leverage, so margin is 20 percent: £2,000.
  • Financing: the provider charges a benchmark interest rate plus a markup. Assume a 4 percent benchmark plus a 3 percent markup, 7 percent a year in total. Daily charge: £10,000 × 7% ÷ 365 = £1.92.
  • Holding for 30 days: financing totals about 30 × £1.92 = £57.53 (using the unrounded daily figure of £1.9178).
  • Closing: the share price rises to £10.50. Gross profit: 1,000 × £0.50 = £500.
  • Net profit before spread and commission: £500 − £57.53 = £442.47.

If the price had fallen to £9.50, the loss would be £500 plus the same £57.53 financing. Financing is charged on the full £10,000 position, not on the £2,000 margin, so it adds up quickly on longer holds. Short positions may receive a small credit or pay a charge, depending on the benchmark rate and the markup. See trading costs and fees.

Costs of CFD trading

Cost What it is
Spread The gap between buy and sell prices; the main cost on forex and index CFDs
Commission Charged on share CFDs and on some raw-spread forex accounts
Overnight financing Interest on the full position value for each night held
Currency conversion When the asset is priced in a different currency from the account
Guaranteed stop premium An optional fee for a stop that cannot be slipped

Counterparty risk

A CFD is an over-the-counter contract with the provider, not a share in a company or a contract on an exchange. That creates risks:

  • Provider insolvency. If the provider fails, your money depends on client money segregation rules and any compensation scheme.
  • Pricing and execution. The provider sets or relays prices and decides how orders are filled. Many providers take the other side of client trades, a model discussed in forex brokers.
  • Extreme events. When the Swiss National Bank removed its euro floor in January 2015, some CFD and forex providers suffered heavy losses as client accounts went deeply negative, and at least one UK broker entered insolvency.

The EU and UK retail rules

In 2018, the European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA) introduced product intervention measures for CFDs sold to retail clients. National regulators later made them permanent, and the UK's Financial Conduct Authority applied similar rules to both CFDs and spread bets from 2019. As of 2026, the core measures are:

Underlying Maximum retail leverage Minimum margin
Major currency pairs 30:1 3.33%
Non-major currency pairs, gold, major stock indices 20:1 5%
Commodities other than gold, non-major indices 10:1 10%
Individual shares and other assets 5:1 20%
Cryptocurrencies 2:1 50%

Major currency pairs are combinations of the US dollar, euro, Japanese yen, pound, Canadian dollar and Swiss franc. Other parts of the package include:

  • Margin close-out: the provider must close positions when account equity falls to 50 percent of the margin required.
  • Negative balance protection: retail clients cannot lose more than the funds in their account.
  • Limits on incentives: bonuses and trading incentives for retail clients are restricted.
  • A standard risk warning, which must state the percentage of the provider's retail accounts that lost money over a recent period.

The UK also banned the sale of crypto derivatives, including crypto CFDs, to retail clients from January 2021.

What the loss disclosures show

The mandatory disclosures typically show that 70 to 80 percent or more of retail CFD accounts at each provider lose money. These figures cover all products and all clients, so they do not mean every strategy fails. They do show that leveraged short-term trading is, for most people, a losing activity.

Professional clients

Traders who meet certain tests, such as trading frequently in significant size, holding a large portfolio, or having relevant professional experience, can ask to be treated as professional clients. That unlocks higher leverage but removes protections such as negative balance protection and some complaint rights. Offers to "go pro" mainly to get more leverage deserve caution.

CFDs outside Europe

  • United States: CFDs are not offered to retail traders. Since the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010, off-exchange swaps, a category that includes CFDs, generally cannot be sold to individuals who are not eligible contract participants. US retail traders use regulated retail forex dealers, futures and options instead. See futures vs other instruments.
  • Australia: ASIC imposed leverage caps and negative balance protection broadly similar to the European rules in 2021.
  • Elsewhere: rules range from strict regimes to outright restrictions to almost no oversight. Check the rules where you live.

Spread betting

Spread betting is a UK and Irish product in which you bet a chosen amount per point of price movement, such as £2 per point on an index or £1 per pip on a currency pair.

Worked example: CFD vs spread bet on the same move

A UK trader expects the FTSE 100 index to rise from 8,000.

  • Spread bet: £5 per point. Exposure: £5 × 8,000 = £40,000. With a 20:1 limit on major indices, margin is 5 percent, £2,000. The index rises to 8,060, a 60-point gain: 60 × £5 = £300 profit.
  • CFD: 5 index CFDs at £1 per point each. Same exposure, same margin, same £300 profit.

The economic result is identical. Financing on overnight positions also works the same way. The main differences are tax and how the position is expressed.

UK tax treatment in general terms

For most UK individuals, as of 2026:

  • Spread betting profits are generally free of capital gains tax and stamp duty, because they are treated as betting. Losses, in turn, cannot be offset against other gains.
  • CFD profits are generally subject to capital gains tax, and losses can usually be offset against other capital gains.

HM Revenue and Customs can treat things differently, for example if trading is someone's main source of income. Tax rules change and depend on personal circumstances. Readers should check current HMRC guidance or ask a qualified tax adviser. The broader principles are covered in trading taxes overview.

Common mistakes

  • Ignoring financing on long holds. It is charged on the full position value every night.
  • Trading at the maximum leverage allowed. Even the regulated caps allow positions large enough to wipe out an account on an ordinary move.
  • Opting into professional status for leverage alone. You lose key protections.
  • Assuming CFDs are like owning shares. You have no voting rights, and you rely on the provider.
  • Choosing spread betting purely for tax. A tax-free loss is still a loss.

FAQ

What is a CFD in simple terms?

A CFD is a contract that pays you the rise in an asset's price, or charges you the fall, without you owning the asset. It is usually traded with leverage through a broker.

Why are CFDs banned in the US?

US law since the Dodd-Frank Act generally prohibits selling off-exchange swaps, including CFDs, to retail investors who are not eligible contract participants. US residents can trade regulated retail forex, futures and options instead.

What is the difference between CFDs and spread betting?

The economics are almost identical. Spread bets are sized in money per point and in the UK are generally free of capital gains tax for individuals, while CFDs are sized in contracts or units and their gains are generally taxable.

What percentage of CFD traders lose money?

Mandatory disclosures by EU and UK providers typically show that 70 to 80 percent or more of retail CFD accounts lose money. The figure varies by provider and period.
