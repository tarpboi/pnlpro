Forex Brokers

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

A forex broker is the firm that gives a retail trader access to the currency market, quotes prices, holds the trader's money and either takes the other side of each trade or passes it on to a larger liquidity provider. The broker's business model, regulator and account type shape what a trader pays, how orders are filled and what happens to client money if the broker fails. Choosing a broker is mostly a question of safety and cost, not of bonuses or marketing claims.

Key takeaways

  • Retail forex brokers either act as the counterparty to client trades (market maker or B-book) or pass trades to liquidity providers (STP, ECN or A-book), and many do both.
  • Brokers earn money from spreads, commissions, swap markups and, for B-book flow, from client losses.
  • Regulation varies widely; strong regulators enforce client money segregation, leverage caps and negative balance protection, while some offshore jurisdictions offer little oversight.
  • The total cost of trading is spread plus commission plus swaps, so compare all three rather than headline spreads.
  • Always verify a broker's license directly on the regulator's own register before depositing.

How retail forex brokers work

Forex is an over-the-counter market, so a retail trader does not send orders to an exchange. The broker quotes a bid and an ask, and the trade is with the broker or with a liquidity provider the broker connects to. The general differences between brokers, dealers and exchanges are covered in brokers and exchanges.

Market makers (dealing desk)

A market maker, or dealing desk broker, sets its own prices based on interbank feeds and takes the other side of client trades. If a client buys EUR/USD, the broker is short EUR/USD. The broker may offset that risk against other clients' opposite trades and hedge any net exposure.

  • Typical features: fixed or variable spreads, no separate commission, small minimum trade sizes.
  • The conflict of interest: when the broker keeps client risk on its own book, it profits when clients lose.

STP and ECN brokers (no dealing desk)

Straight-through processing (STP) brokers route client orders to one or more liquidity providers, such as banks or non-bank market makers, usually with a markup added to the spread. ECN-style brokers give access to a pool of prices from several providers, often with raw spreads plus a separate commission.

  • Typical features: variable spreads that can be near zero on majors in busy hours, a commission per lot, and prices that reflect the wider market.
  • Limitations: the term "ECN" is used loosely in marketing, and a broker can call itself one without truly offering a multi-party venue.

A-book and B-book

These industry terms describe what a broker does with each client's risk:

Term What happens to the trade How the broker earns
A-book Passed on and hedged with liquidity providers Spread markup and commission
B-book Kept on the broker's own book Spread, plus client losses minus client gains
Hybrid Some clients or trades A-booked, others B-booked Both

Most large retail brokers run a hybrid model. They often B-book small or short-term accounts, where the client base as a whole tends to lose, and A-book large or consistently profitable accounts to avoid paying them out of their own pocket. A hybrid model is legal in most jurisdictions when disclosed and managed under conflict-of-interest rules. In 2017, the US Commodity Futures Trading Commission found that a large US retail FX broker had misled customers about having an interest in the market maker that took the other side of their trades. The case ended with a penalty and the firm leaving the US market, and it is often cited as a reason to read execution disclosures closely.

How brokers make money

  • Spread: the gap between bid and ask, or a markup on top of the raw interbank spread.
  • Commission: a fixed charge per lot on raw-spread accounts.
  • Swap markups: overnight financing charged at a rate less favorable than the pure interest rate difference. See carry trade.
  • Client losses: on B-book flow, losing client trades are broker revenue.
  • Other fees: inactivity fees, currency conversion on deposits, and withdrawal charges at some firms.

Spread accounts vs commission accounts

Many brokers offer two account types. The cheaper one depends on trade size and style.

Worked example: comparing costs on one round trip

A trader opens and closes 1 standard lot of EUR/USD, where each pip is worth $10.

Account Spread Commission per round trip Total cost
Standard (spread only) 1.2 pips = $12.00 $0 $12.00
Raw (commission) 0.2 pips = $2.00 $7.00 $9.00

On these figures the raw account is $3.00 cheaper per lot. A trader doing 100 round-trip lots a month would save $300 a month. For someone trading a few micro lots, the difference is a few cents and other factors matter more. The numbers above are illustrative, since spreads and commissions vary by broker, pair and time of day. The trading costs and fees article shows how such costs add up for active traders.

Fixed vs variable spreads

Fixed spreads look predictable but are usually wider on average, and brokers may still widen them or refuse orders during extreme news. Variable spreads are tighter in normal conditions and widen at news, rollover and illiquid hours.

Regulation tiers

Regulators differ in what they require and how strictly they enforce it. This table describes the general picture as of 2026; rules change, so check the current requirements.

Regulator Country or region Notes
CFTC and NFA United States Retail forex dealers must register; high capital requirements; leverage capped at 50:1 on majors; CFDs not offered to US retail clients
FCA United Kingdom Leverage caps, negative balance protection, client money rules, compensation scheme for eligible claims
ESMA rules via national regulators (for example CySEC in Cyprus, BaFin in Germany) European Union Leverage caps, negative balance protection, margin close-out rule, loss-rate disclosure; firms can passport across the EU
ASIC Australia Leverage caps and negative balance protection under a product intervention order since 2021
FSA Japan Leverage capped at 25:1 for retail FX
Others such as FINMA (Switzerland), MAS (Singapore), SFC (Hong Kong) Various Generally strict regimes
Offshore regulators in some small jurisdictions Various Lighter rules, often high leverage and weak recourse; some authorities state that they do not supervise forex trading at all

Being registered as a company in a country is not the same as being licensed by its financial regulator. The trading regulation overview explains what each regulator does and how compensation schemes work in general terms.

Negative balance protection

Negative balance protection means a retail client cannot lose more than the money in their account. It became mandatory for retail CFD clients in the EU and UK in 2018 and 2019, and in Australia in 2021. Its value showed in January 2015, when the Swiss franc's sudden jump after the Swiss National Bank removed its euro floor left many retail accounts with negative balances. Several brokers suffered heavy losses and at least one UK broker entered insolvency. See leverage risk and black swans.

Offshore broker risks

Brokers licensed only in lightly regulated jurisdictions often offer leverage of 500:1 or more, large deposit bonuses and fast sign-up. The risks include:

  • client money that may not be segregated from the firm's own funds;
  • withdrawal delays or refusals, sometimes justified by bonus terms;
  • price manipulation or unexplained trade cancellations;
  • no meaningful complaints process or compensation scheme;
  • little recourse if the firm disappears.

Some offshore brokers are legitimate, but a trader using one should assume their money is less protected. The forex risks and scams article covers warning signs and how to check a license.

Trading platforms

Most retail forex brokers offer MetaTrader 4 or MetaTrader 5 (developed by MetaQuotes), cTrader (developed by Spotware), their own web and mobile apps, or links to charting services. The platform is only a front end: it does not change who holds your money or how orders are executed. See trading platforms and tools.

A short checklist

  1. Find the license number and verify it on the regulator's own website, not through a link from the broker.
  2. Check which legal entity your account would be opened with, since many groups have both a strictly regulated entity and an offshore one.
  3. Read the order execution policy and conflicts-of-interest disclosure.
  4. Compare total costs: spread, commission and swap on the pairs you trade.
  5. Test deposits and withdrawals with a small amount first.
  6. Be wary of large bonuses, account managers who push you to deposit more, and guaranteed-return claims.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing on maximum leverage. High leverage is a risk, not a feature.
  • Trusting a regulator logo on a website. Clone firms copy real license numbers. Verify independently.
  • Comparing only headline spreads. Commissions and swap markups can matter more.
  • Accepting bonuses without reading the terms. Bonus conditions often restrict withdrawals.
  • Ignoring which entity holds your account. The same brand can carry very different protections in different countries.

FAQ

What is the difference between an ECN and a market maker broker?

A market maker takes the other side of client trades and sets its own quotes, while an ECN-style broker routes orders to a pool of outside liquidity providers and usually charges a commission. Many brokers combine both approaches.

Do forex brokers trade against you?

Brokers that B-book client flow are the counterparty to those trades, so they gain when clients lose. This is common and legal in many countries if disclosed and managed under conflict-of-interest rules.

How do I know if a forex broker is regulated?

Look up the firm and its license number on the regulator's official register, such as the FCA register in the UK or the NFA's BASIC database in the US. Check that the website address and contact details match the register entry.

Is my money safe with a forex broker?

It depends on the regulator and the legal entity. Strict regimes require client money to be held separately from the firm's own money and may offer a compensation scheme, while offshore brokers often offer little protection.
