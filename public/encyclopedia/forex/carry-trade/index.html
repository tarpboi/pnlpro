Carry Trade

6 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

A carry trade is a position that borrows, or sells, a currency with a low interest rate and buys a currency with a higher interest rate, aiming to earn the difference. In retail forex, that difference is paid or charged every night through the broker's swap, also called rollover. Carry trades can produce steady income for long stretches, but they are exposed to sudden, sharp exchange rate moves that can erase months or years of interest in days.

Key takeaways

  • The carry trade earns the interest rate differential between two currencies, minus the broker's markup.
  • Retail brokers credit or debit this differential nightly at the 5 p.m. New York rollover, usually with a triple charge on Wednesday.
  • The yen has historically been the classic funding currency because Japanese interest rates stayed near zero for decades.
  • Carry returns have historically come with crash risk: slow gains punctuated by fast, large losses when many traders unwind at once.
  • The August 2024 yen surge is a recent example of a carry unwind.

How the carry trade works

Each currency has an interest rate set mainly by its central bank. Holding a currency effectively earns that rate; borrowing it costs that rate. A carry trader goes long a high-yielding currency against a low-yielding one.

In theory, the higher-yielding currency should depreciate over time by about the interest difference, which would cancel the gain. This idea is called uncovered interest parity. In practice, academic research has found that higher-yielding currencies have often not fallen as much as the theory predicts, at least on average and over long periods. That gap is the source of carry returns. Economists call it the forward premium puzzle. The forward pricing relationship behind it is shown in what moves currencies.

Swap and rollover at retail brokers

Retail forex positions are rolled forward every night so they never settle. When a position is held past the daily cutoff, usually 5 p.m. New York time, the broker applies a swap:

  • If you are long the higher-yielding currency, you may receive a credit.
  • If you are long the lower-yielding currency, you pay a debit.
  • The broker adds a markup on both sides, so the credit is smaller than the true differential and the debit is larger. On some pairs, both sides can be negative.

Swaps are usually shown in the platform's contract specifications, either in points or in money per lot. Because spot FX settles two business days after the trade, the swap applied on Wednesday night normally covers the weekend, so it is charged or credited three times.

Some brokers offer swap-free accounts, often marketed as Islamic accounts for clients who cannot pay or receive interest for religious reasons. These usually replace swaps with other fees or wider spreads.

Worked example: a USD/JPY carry trade

The rates below are hypothetical, chosen to show the arithmetic.

Setup

  • US dollar interest rate: 4.5 percent. Japanese yen interest rate: 0.5 percent. Differential: 4.0 percent.
  • A trader buys 1 standard lot of USD/JPY, meaning long $100,000 and short the equivalent in yen, at 150.00.
  • Account equity: $20,000. Effective leverage: $100,000 ÷ $20,000 = 5 times.

Gross carry

  • Annual interest differential: $100,000 × 4.0% = $4,000.
  • Per night: $4,000 ÷ 365 = $10.96.

After the broker's markup

  • Suppose the broker pays a swap of $9.00 per night on the long side.
  • Annual credit: $9.00 × 365 = $3,285. (The Wednesday triple swap covers weekends, so the credit applies to all 365 nights.)
  • Return on equity from carry alone: $3,285 ÷ $20,000 = 16.4 percent a year.

The risk

  • Suppose USD/JPY falls from 150.00 to 142.00, a move of 800 pips.
  • Loss in yen: $100,000 × (150.00 − 142.00) = ¥800,000.
  • Loss in dollars at the new rate: ¥800,000 ÷ 142.00 = $5,633.80.
  • That loss equals about 1.7 years of swap income ($5,633.80 ÷ $3,285) and 28.2 percent of the account.

An 800-pip fall in USD/JPY is large, but it has happened within weeks several times. With higher leverage, the same move could wipe out the account. The pip and margin arithmetic is explained in pips, lots and leverage.

The classic yen-funded carry trade

Japan kept short-term interest rates near zero, and at times below zero, for most of the period from the late 1990s until 2024. That made the yen a cheap currency to borrow. Investors and traders, including Japanese households, borrowed in yen or sold it to buy higher-yielding currencies such as the Australian dollar, New Zealand dollar, US dollar and emerging-market currencies. AUD/JPY and NZD/JPY became popular carry pairs.

The Swiss franc and, at times, the euro have also been used as funding currencies when their rates were very low.

Carry unwinds

Carry trades often attract many participants at once. When something triggers losses, such as a shock to risk appetite, a surprise rate change or a sharp move in the funding currency, traders close positions together. Closing a yen-funded carry trade means buying back yen, which pushes the yen higher, which causes more losses and more closing. Researchers have described carry returns as "going up the stairs and down the elevator", and a well-known study by Brunnermeier, Nagel and Pedersen linked carry trades to currency crash risk.

2008

During the global financial crisis, yen carry trades unwound violently. AUD/JPY roughly halved between mid-2008 and late October 2008 as investors fled risk and bought back yen.

August 2024

In July 2024, USD/JPY traded near 162, its highest level in decades, after years of wide rate differentials. Then several things happened close together:

  • The Bank of Japan raised its policy rate at the end of July 2024 and signaled more tightening.
  • A weaker-than-expected US jobs report in early August raised expectations of US rate cuts.
  • Carry positions began to unwind, and the yen rose sharply.

By August 5, 2024, USD/JPY had fallen to around 142, a drop of more than 10 percent in about a month. Japan's Nikkei 225 stock index fell more than 12 percent that day, its biggest one-day point drop on record, and volatility spread to global markets. Prices partly recovered in the following days, but traders caught with leveraged carry positions had already taken large losses or been liquidated. See leverage risk and black swans for other tail events.

Managing carry risk

  • Size for the move, not the swap. Plan for an adverse move several times larger than normal daily ranges.
  • Watch the central banks of both currencies. A rate change by the funding currency's central bank can end a carry trade. See central banks and interest rates.
  • Watch positioning. Crowded carry trades unwind fastest.
  • Diversify carefully. Several carry pairs funded in the same currency are really one trade. See correlation and portfolio risk.
  • Check swaps regularly. Brokers change swap rates as interest rates move, and a positive carry can turn negative.

Common mistakes

  • Using high leverage to boost carry. Leverage multiplies the swap, but it also multiplies the price risk that dominates in a crash.
  • Ignoring the broker's markup. The net swap can be far smaller than the headline rate gap, and sometimes negative on both sides.
  • Treating carry as risk-free income. It is compensation for crash risk.
  • Forgetting about the Wednesday triple swap. It triples a negative charge as well as a positive one.
  • Holding exotic carry pairs without checking liquidity. High rates often come with wide spreads and sudden devaluations.

FAQ

What is a carry trade in simple terms?

It is borrowing in a low-interest currency and holding a high-interest currency to earn the difference. The trade loses money if the exchange rate moves against it by more than the interest earned.

How do forex swaps work?

When you hold a forex position past the daily rollover, usually 5 p.m. New York time, the broker credits or debits the interest difference between the two currencies, minus its markup. Most brokers apply three days' worth on Wednesday to cover the weekend.

Why did the yen carry trade unwind in August 2024?

The Bank of Japan raised rates at the end of July 2024 while weak US data raised expectations of US rate cuts, narrowing the expected rate gap. As the yen rose, traders closed positions together, which pushed the yen up even faster.

Is the carry trade still profitable?

It depends on current interest rate differentials, costs and market conditions. Historically, carry strategies have earned positive average returns over long periods but with occasional severe losses, so past results do not guarantee future ones.
