Currency Pairs

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

A currency pair is the price of one currency expressed in another, such as EUR/USD or USD/JPY. Every forex trade buys one currency and sells the other at the same time. Knowing how pairs are quoted, how they are grouped and how each one tends to behave is the first practical step in forex trading, because the pair you choose shapes your costs, your risk and the hours you need to watch.

Key takeaways

  • The first currency in a pair is the base currency and the second is the quote currency; the price shows how much of the quote currency buys one unit of the base.
  • Majors are the most traded pairs and all include the US dollar; crosses pair two non-dollar currencies; exotics pair a major currency with an emerging-market currency.
  • Spreads and volatility generally rise as you move from majors to crosses to exotics.
  • Each pair has its own drivers, active hours and typical behavior, which traders sometimes call its personality.

Base currency and quote currency

In the quote EUR/USD = 1.0850:

  • EUR is the base currency. It is the thing being priced.
  • USD is the quote currency, also called the counter currency. It is the unit of the price.
  • 1.0850 means one euro costs 1.0850 US dollars.

If you buy EUR/USD, you buy euros and sell dollars. You profit if the euro rises against the dollar. If you sell EUR/USD, you sell euros and buy dollars, and you profit if the euro falls. Selling a pair is as easy as buying it, which is one reason forex appeals to traders who want to trade in both directions. See long and short positions for the general idea.

Bid, ask and spread

Brokers show two prices for every pair:

Quote Meaning
Bid 1.0850 The price at which you can sell the base currency
Ask 1.0851 The price at which you can buy the base currency
Spread 0.0001 The difference, here one pip

You buy at the higher price and sell at the lower one, so a trade starts slightly in the red by the amount of the spread. The bid-ask spread and liquidity article explains why spreads widen and narrow.

Decimal places and pips

Most pairs are quoted to four decimal places, plus a fifth "pipette" digit at many brokers. A pip is the fourth decimal place, 0.0001. Pairs involving the Japanese yen are quoted to two decimal places, plus a third pipette, and a pip is 0.01. Calculating what a pip is worth in money is covered in pips, lots and leverage.

Which currency comes first

Market convention decides which currency is the base, and it is not alphabetical. The usual order of priority is EUR, then GBP, then AUD, then NZD, then USD, then CAD, then CHF, then JPY. So the euro is quoted as EUR/USD, never USD/EUR, and the dollar is quoted as USD/JPY, never JPY/USD. A pair can be inverted by dividing 1 by the price: if USD/JPY is 150.00, JPY/USD is 1 ÷ 150 = 0.006667.

The three groups of pairs

Majors

The majors are the most heavily traded pairs. Each includes the US dollar and another major currency:

Pair Nickname Notes
EUR/USD Fiber (rarely used) The single most traded pair, roughly a fifth of global turnover in BIS surveys
USD/JPY None in wide use Highly sensitive to US yields and Japanese policy
GBP/USD Cable Named after the transatlantic telegraph cable once used for quotes
USD/CHF Swissie The franc is treated as a safe haven
AUD/USD Aussie Linked to commodity prices and China's economy
USD/CAD Loonie Linked to oil prices and the US economy
NZD/USD Kiwi Linked to dairy prices and risk sentiment

Minors and crosses

A cross is any pair that does not include the US dollar, such as EUR/GBP, EUR/JPY, GBP/JPY, AUD/JPY or EUR/CHF. The term "minors" is often used for the crosses between major currencies. Crosses are priced from the two dollar pairs behind them. For example, if EUR/USD is 1.0850 and USD/JPY is 150.00, EUR/JPY should be about 1.0850 × 150.00 = 162.75. In practice, the main crosses are traded directly, but if the cross drifts too far from this implied price, arbitrage traders pull it back.

Exotics

An exotic pair combines a major currency with a currency from a smaller or emerging economy, such as USD/TRY (Turkish lira), USD/ZAR (South African rand), USD/MXN (Mexican peso), EUR/PLN (Polish zloty) or USD/SGD (Singapore dollar). Exotics can offer large moves and, often, high interest rates, but they come with:

  • much wider spreads and higher swap charges;
  • sudden gaps on political news or central bank surprises;
  • thin liquidity outside local trading hours;
  • capital controls or intervention risk in some countries.

Some exotic currencies are pegged or tightly managed. The Hong Kong dollar trades in a 7.75 to 7.85 band against the US dollar, and the Danish krone is held close to the euro. A peg can make a pair look calm for years, then break suddenly, as happened when the Swiss National Bank dropped its EUR/CHF floor in January 2015. See leverage risk and black swans.

Spreads and volatility by group

The ranges below are typical for retail accounts during liquid hours and are only a rough guide. Actual spreads vary by broker, account type, time of day and market conditions, and they can widen sharply around news and at the daily rollover.

Group Example Typical retail spread Typical behavior
Majors EUR/USD Often under 1 to about 1.5 pips Deep liquidity, steady moves, tightest costs
Major crosses EUR/GBP, EUR/JPY About 1 to 3 pips Liquid, but costs a little more
Volatile crosses GBP/JPY Several pips Larger daily ranges and sharper swings
Exotics USD/TRY, USD/ZAR Tens of pips or more Wide spreads, gaps, high swap costs

A useful way to compare cost is to divide the spread by the pair's average daily range. A 1-pip spread on a pair that moves 70 pips a day is a much smaller hurdle than a 40-pip spread on a pair that moves 300 pips a day. The ATR and volatility indicators article explains how to measure that range.

Worked example: comparing cost

Suppose a trader wants to scalp for about 10 pips of profit per trade.

  • On EUR/USD with a 0.8-pip spread, the spread costs 0.8 ÷ 10 = 8 percent of the target.
  • On GBP/JPY with a 3-pip spread, the spread costs 3 ÷ 10 = 30 percent of the target.
  • On an exotic with a 40-pip spread, the target is impossible: the trade starts 40 pips behind.

This is why short-term strategies are usually confined to majors, and why exotics are mostly traded on longer timeframes, if at all.

Pair personalities

These are broad tendencies, not rules, and they change as economic conditions change.

  • EUR/USD reflects the gap between US and euro-area interest rates and growth. It is usually the cheapest pair to trade and moves most during London and New York hours.
  • USD/JPY tends to track the difference between US and Japanese bond yields. The yen often strengthens in risk-off episodes, and Japan's authorities have intervened to support it in the past, including in 2022 and 2024.
  • GBP/USD reacts strongly to UK data and Bank of England decisions and can move sharply on political news.
  • USD/CHF and EUR/CHF reflect the franc's safe-haven status and the Swiss National Bank's willingness to intervene.
  • AUD/USD and NZD/USD are tied to commodity prices, Chinese demand and global risk appetite.
  • USD/CAD often moves with crude oil prices, since oil is a major Canadian export.
  • AUD/JPY has historically been watched as a barometer of risk appetite and of the carry trade, because it pairs a higher-yielding currency with a low-yielding one.

The forces behind these tendencies are covered in what moves currencies.

Correlated pairs

Because so many pairs share the US dollar, they often move together. EUR/USD and GBP/USD tend to rise and fall together, while EUR/USD and USD/CHF tend to move in opposite directions. A trader who buys EUR/USD, buys GBP/USD and sells USD/CHF may think they have three trades, but they may really have one large bet against the dollar. See correlation and portfolio risk.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up base and quote. Selling USD/JPY is a bet on the yen rising, not on the dollar rising.
  • Comparing spreads in pips alone. A cheap-looking spread can be expensive relative to a pair's normal movement.
  • Holding exotics without checking swaps. Overnight charges on some exotic pairs can be large.
  • Treating a peg as permanent. Pegs can hold for years, then break overnight.
  • Stacking correlated pairs. Several positions against the same currency multiply risk.

FAQ

What are the seven major currency pairs?

They are EUR/USD, USD/JPY, GBP/USD, USD/CHF, AUD/USD, USD/CAD and NZD/USD. All include the US dollar and are the most heavily traded pairs.

Which currency pair is best for beginners?

Many beginners start with a major pair such as EUR/USD because its spreads are usually the tightest and its liquidity the deepest. That makes costs lower, but it does not make the pair easier to predict.

What is a cross currency pair?

A cross is a pair that does not include the US dollar, such as EUR/GBP or AUD/JPY. Its price is linked to the two dollar pairs behind it.

Why are exotic pairs so expensive to trade?

Exotic currencies trade in much smaller volumes, so dealers quote wider spreads to cover the risk of holding them. Many also have high interest rates, which makes overnight swap charges larger.
