Pips, Lots and Leverage

8 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

A pip is the standard unit for measuring a change in an exchange rate, a lot is the standard unit for measuring the size of a forex trade, and leverage is how much exposure a broker lets you control for each unit of your own money. Together they decide how much money each price move makes or loses and how much of your account a trade ties up. Getting these numbers right for each currency pair is the basis of position sizing and of risk management in forex.

Key takeaways

  • A pip is 0.0001 for most pairs and 0.01 for pairs quoted in Japanese yen; a pipette is one tenth of a pip.
  • A standard lot is 100,000 units of the base currency, a mini lot is 10,000 and a micro lot is 1,000.
  • Pip value equals the pip size times the number of units, expressed in the quote currency, then converted into your account currency.
  • Required margin equals the trade's notional value in your account currency divided by the leverage.
  • Leverage caps differ by country and change over time; as of 2026, retail limits in the EU, UK and Australia are 30:1 on major pairs.

Pips and pipettes

For most currency pairs, a pip is the fourth decimal place:

  • EUR/USD moving from 1.0850 to 1.0851 is a 1-pip move.
  • EUR/USD moving from 1.0850 to 1.0900 is a 50-pip move.

For pairs where the yen is the quote currency, a pip is the second decimal place:

  • USD/JPY moving from 150.00 to 150.01 is a 1-pip move.
  • USD/JPY moving from 150.00 to 151.00 is a 100-pip move.

Most brokers quote one extra digit, called a pipette or fractional pip. A EUR/USD price of 1.08503 has a pipette of 3, meaning 0.3 pips beyond 1.0850. Spreads are often shown in pips with one decimal place, such as 0.6 pips.

Some instruments on forex platforms, such as gold or stock indices, have their own conventions. Always check the contract specification in your platform.

Lots

A lot is a standard trade size, measured in units of the base currency.

Lot type Units of base currency Platform size
Standard 100,000 1.00
Mini 10,000 0.10
Micro 1,000 0.01
Nano (some brokers only) 100 Varies

Buying 1 standard lot of EUR/USD means buying 100,000 euros. Buying 1 standard lot of USD/JPY means buying 100,000 US dollars. Many platforms let you trade any size in steps of 0.01 lots.

How to calculate pip value

The formula has two steps.

  1. Pip value in the quote currency = pip size × number of units.
  2. Convert to your account currency if the quote currency is different.

Example 1: EUR/USD, US dollar account

  • Pip size: 0.0001. Units: 100,000.
  • Pip value: 0.0001 × 100,000 = $10.00 per pip for a standard lot.
  • The quote currency is already USD, so no conversion is needed.
  • A mini lot is worth $1.00 per pip and a micro lot $0.10 per pip.

This is the same for every pair where USD is the quote currency, such as GBP/USD, AUD/USD and NZD/USD.

Example 2: USD/JPY at 150.00, US dollar account

  • Pip size: 0.01. Units: 100,000.
  • Pip value in yen: 0.01 × 100,000 = ¥1,000 per pip.
  • Convert to dollars: ¥1,000 ÷ 150.00 = $6.67 per pip (6.6667 before rounding).

Because the conversion uses the current rate, the dollar value of a USD/JPY pip changes as the rate moves. At 140.00 it would be ¥1,000 ÷ 140 = $7.14.

Example 3: USD/CAD at 1.3500, US dollar account

  • Pip value in Canadian dollars: 0.0001 × 100,000 = CA$10.00.
  • Convert: CA$10.00 ÷ 1.3500 = $7.41 per pip (7.4074 before rounding).

Example 4: EUR/GBP, US dollar account, with GBP/USD at 1.2700

  • Pip value in pounds: 0.0001 × 100,000 = £10.00.
  • Convert to dollars by multiplying by GBP/USD: £10.00 × 1.2700 = $12.70 per pip.

Example 5: EUR/USD at 1.0850, euro account

  • Pip value in dollars: $10.00.
  • Convert to euros: $10.00 ÷ 1.0850 = €9.22 per pip (9.2166 before rounding).

Quick reference

Pair (standard lot) Rates used Pip value in USD
EUR/USD, GBP/USD, AUD/USD None needed $10.00
USD/JPY USD/JPY 150.00 $6.67
USD/CAD USD/CAD 1.3500 $7.41
USD/CHF USD/CHF 0.9000 $11.11
EUR/GBP GBP/USD 1.2700 $12.70

Your platform calculates this for you, but knowing the method lets you check it and size trades on pairs you don't usually trade.

Leverage and margin

Leverage lets you control a position larger than your deposit. Margin is the amount of your own money the broker sets aside to hold the position. The general mechanics, including margin calls, are covered in leverage and margin.

Required margin = notional value in account currency ÷ leverage.

Leverage of 30:1 means a margin requirement of 1 ÷ 30 = 3.33 percent. Leverage of 50:1 means 2 percent, and 500:1 means 0.2 percent.

Example 6: 1 standard lot of EUR/USD at 1.0850, US dollar account

  • Notional value: 100,000 × 1.0850 = $108,500.
Leverage Required margin
30:1 $108,500 ÷ 30 = $3,616.67
50:1 $108,500 ÷ 50 = $2,170.00
500:1 $108,500 ÷ 500 = $217.00

Example 7: 1 standard lot of USD/JPY, US dollar account

The base currency is USD, so the notional value is simply $100,000.

Leverage Required margin
25:1 $100,000 ÷ 25 = $4,000.00
30:1 $100,000 ÷ 30 = $3,333.33

Example 8: 1 mini lot of EUR/GBP, US dollar account, EUR/USD at 1.0850

  • Notional value in euros: 10,000.
  • In dollars: 10,000 × 1.0850 = $10,850.
  • At 30:1: $10,850 ÷ 30 = $361.67.

Margin level and how an account gets closed out

Brokers track equity (balance plus or minus open profit and loss), used margin, free margin (equity minus used margin) and margin level (equity ÷ used margin × 100).

Example 9: how far can price move?

A trader has a $5,000 US dollar account and buys 1 standard lot of EUR/USD at 1.0850 with 30:1 leverage.

  • Used margin: $3,616.67.
  • Free margin: $5,000 − $3,616.67 = $1,383.33.
  • Margin level: $5,000 ÷ $3,616.67 × 100 = 138.2 percent.
  • Effective leverage on the account: $108,500 ÷ $5,000 = 21.7 times.

Under the EU and UK retail rules, brokers must start closing positions when margin level falls to 50 percent. That happens when equity falls to 50 percent of $3,616.67, which is $1,808.33. The loss needed is $5,000 − $1,808.33 = $3,191.67. At $10 per pip, that is a move of about 319 pips against the trade, ignoring the small change in required margin as the price moves. EUR/USD has moved more than that in a single week many times.

Note that effective leverage, meaning total position size divided by account equity, is what drives risk, not the maximum leverage the broker offers. A trader with 500:1 available who only opens positions worth twice their equity is using 2:1.

Putting it together: sizing a trade

A trader with a $5,000 account wants to risk 1 percent, which is $50, on a EUR/USD trade with a 25-pip stop.

  • Required pip value: $50 ÷ 25 pips = $2.00 per pip.
  • Since a mini lot is worth $1.00 per pip, the size is 2 mini lots, or 0.20 lots (20,000 units).
  • Notional: 20,000 × 1.0850 = $21,700. Margin at 30:1: $723.33.

The leverage cap barely matters here. The stop and the risk limit set the size. This is the approach explained in position sizing and stop-loss strategies.

Leverage limits by region

These limits apply to retail clients and are correct in general terms as of 2026. They change, so check your regulator's current rules.

Region Major FX pairs Other FX pairs
European Union (ESMA rules, since 2018) 30:1 20:1
United Kingdom (FCA) 30:1 20:1
Australia (ASIC, since 2021) 30:1 20:1
United States (CFTC and NFA) 50:1 20:1
Japan (FSA) 25:1 25:1
Many offshore brokers 500:1 or more Varies

Under the EU and UK rules, "major" pairs are combinations of the US dollar, euro, yen, pound, Canadian dollar and Swiss franc. Professional clients and offshore brokers can get more leverage, but higher limits come with fewer protections; see forex brokers. The rules on CFDs specifically are in CFDs and spread betting.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing pips with pipettes. A move of 10 on a five-digit quote is 1 pip, not 10.
  • Assuming every pip is worth $10. That is only true for a standard lot on pairs quoted in US dollars, in a US dollar account.
  • Sizing by available leverage. The maximum the broker allows is not a target.
  • Forgetting the conversion rate moves. On USD/JPY and crosses, pip value changes as rates change.
  • Ignoring margin on several positions. Each open trade uses margin, and losses on one reduce the cushion for all.

FAQ

How much is 1 pip worth?

For a standard lot of 100,000 units on a pair quoted in US dollars, such as EUR/USD, one pip is worth $10 in a US dollar account. For other pairs or account currencies, multiply the pip size by the number of units and convert at the current rate.

How many pips is 1.0850 to 1.0900?

It is 50 pips, because the difference is 0.0050 and one pip is 0.0001. For yen pairs, one pip is 0.01, so 150.00 to 150.50 is also 50 pips.

What does 30:1 leverage mean?

It means you can control a position up to 30 times the money set aside as margin, so a $3,000 margin deposit can hold a $90,000 position. Gains and losses are calculated on the full $90,000.

What lot size should a beginner use?

Many beginners start with micro lots, where one pip on a dollar-quoted pair is worth about 10 cents. The right size depends on account balance, risk per trade and stop distance, not on a fixed rule.
