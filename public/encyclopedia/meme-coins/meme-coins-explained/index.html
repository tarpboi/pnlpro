Meme Coins Explained

A meme coin is a crypto token whose value comes almost entirely from attention, community and jokes rather than from cash flows, a product or a working protocol. Most are created in seconds on launchpads, trade on decentralized exchanges from the first minute, and either fade to nothing within hours or, very rarely, reach market values in the billions. They are the most speculative, fastest-moving and most heavily manipulated corner of crypto, and the large majority of people who trade them lose money.

Key takeaways

  • A meme coin has no underlying earnings or utility, so its price is set by attention, flows of new buyers, and the behavior of a few large holders.
  • Since 2024, bonding-curve launchpads have made creating a token nearly free, and tens of thousands of new tokens can appear on a single busy day.
  • Most new launches go to zero or close to it; by most published estimates only around 1 to 2 percent of launchpad tokens even reach a regular DEX pool.
  • The reliable winners are the infrastructure (launchpads, bots, DEXs, validators) and the earliest insiders; late retail buyers usually supply the exit liquidity.
  • If you trade them at all, treat each position as a lottery ticket you can afford to lose entirely.

What makes a coin a "meme coin"

There is no formal definition, but meme coins share a recognizable profile:

  • No claim on anything. Holding the token gives no share of revenue, no governance that matters, and no redemption right. Compare this with the token models discussed in tokenomics and token unlocks.
  • A simple, shareable idea. A dog, a frog, a cat in a hat, a viral video, a political figure, a news event. The meme is the product.
  • Fixed or capped supply. Many launchpad tokens have a supply of exactly one billion units, with the ability to mint more permanently disabled at launch.
  • Trading starts immediately. Instead of a sale to investors and a later listing, the token is tradable on-chain from the moment it exists, usually through an automated market maker (AMM) or a bonding curve.
  • Price discovery driven by flows. With nothing to value, price is simply where the latest buyer and seller met. It moves when attention arrives or leaves.

The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission's staff said in a February 2025 statement that typical meme coins are generally not securities, because buyers are not investing in a business run by others. That does not make fraud or manipulation legal; it mainly means the investor protections that apply to stocks do not apply here. Rules differ by country and continue to change.

A short history

Dogecoin and the first wave (2013 to 2021)

Dogecoin was created in December 2013 by software engineers Billy Markus and Jackson Palmer as a joke based on the Shiba Inu "doge" meme. It ran on its own blockchain, had an inflationary supply, and survived for years as a tipping currency. In 2021, social media attention, including posts by Elon Musk, pushed it into the top tier of crypto by market value. Dogecoin showed that a coin with no roadmap could hold a large valuation if enough people cared about it.

Ethereum tokens: SHIB and PEPE

Shiba Inu (SHIB), launched in August 2020 as an Ethereum token by a pseudonymous creator, rode the 2021 Dogecoin wave. Pepe (PEPE), launched on Ethereum in April 2023, ran from obscurity to a multi-billion-dollar market value within weeks. These showed that a meme coin did not need its own chain: any token on a smart-contract platform could do it, and early buyers could turn tiny sums into fortunes, while later buyers often held through drawdowns of 80 percent or more.

Solana and the launchpad era (2023 onward)

Bonk (BONK) was airdropped to the Solana community around Christmas 2022, after the FTX collapse had battered the ecosystem, and became a symbol of its recovery. Dogwifhat (WIF), launched on Solana in late 2023, reached a multi-billion-dollar valuation in 2024. Solana's low fees and fast blocks made it practical to trade tokens worth a few thousand dollars.

In January 2024, pump.fun launched on Solana. It let anyone create a token in under a minute for a trivial cost, with trading on a bonding curve until the token reached a threshold and "graduated" to a DEX pool. Copies followed on most major chains. Token creation exploded: busy days saw tens of thousands of new tokens. Celebrity and political tokens followed in early 2025, including the TRUMP token launched days before the U.S. presidential inauguration and the LIBRA token promoted by Argentina's president, which collapsed within hours and triggered investigations. See KOLs, insiders and cabals.

Why people trade them

People trade meme coins for a mix of reasons, and it helps to be honest about which ones apply to you:

Motive What it looks like The catch
Asymmetric payoff Small bets hoping for 10x to 1,000x The tail is real but tiny; most tickets lose
Speed and excitement Trades that resolve in minutes Feels like gaming; easy to overtrade
Community and culture Joining a group around a joke Group loyalty can stop you from selling
Low entry barrier No KYC, no minimums, a phone and a wallet No investor protection either
Perceived edge Being early, using tools, following wallets Insiders and bots are earlier still

Attention economics: how price actually forms

A meme coin's price at any moment reflects how many people want it right now versus how many want out. Three forces dominate:

  1. Attention inflow. New buyers arrive from X (formerly Twitter), Telegram, DEX screener trending lists, influencer posts and copy-trading bots. Each wave pushes price up along a thin liquidity curve.
  2. Holder concentration. If a few wallets hold a large share, their selling decides the chart. Insiders who bought at near-zero prices can sell into the attention wave at any time.
  3. Liquidity depth. Pools are thin, so modest orders move price a lot in both directions. A $5,000 sell can knock 10 percent or more off a small token. See bid-ask spread and liquidity.

Because there is no anchor value, there is also no floor. Once attention moves to the next token, buyers stop arriving and holders compete to sell. That is why the typical chart is a sharp spike followed by a long bleed.

The odds, bluntly

The numbers are discouraging by design. Published estimates of how many pump.fun tokens ever graduate from the bonding curve to a DEX pool cluster around or below 1 to 2 percent, depending on the period and method. Of the tokens that graduate, most still lose the bulk of their value within days. A coin that survives for months and reaches a market value of tens of millions of dollars is a rare event, and the ones that reach billions are a handful per cycle.

Separately, on-chain analytics firms that track trader wallets have repeatedly found that a large majority of wallets trading launchpad tokens are net losers after fees, with a small minority capturing most of the gains. Exact figures vary by study and period, so treat any single percentage with caution, but the shape of the distribution is consistent.

Worked example: the lottery math

Suppose a trader puts $50 into each of 100 new tokens shortly after launch, $5,000 in total, and pays about $1.50 per round trip in priority fees, bot fees and slippage, so $150 in costs. A plausible spread of outcomes for unfiltered launches:

Outcome Count Value returned per $50 Total
Goes to near zero (sold at 5 percent) 75 $2.50 $187.50
Sold at a loss (half back) 15 $25 $375
Sold at 2x 7 $100 $700
Sold at 10x 2 $500 $1,000
Sold at 50x 1 $2,500 $2,500
Total 100 $4,762.50

After $150 in costs, the trader ends with $4,612.50, a loss of $387.50 (7.75 percent), even though one trade returned 50x. Without the single 50x, the result would have been $2,262.50 returned before costs, a loss of more than half. The outcome depends almost entirely on catching rare outliers and actually selling them near the top, which is far harder than the table makes it look. These numbers are illustrative, not measured averages.

Who profits

Meme coin trading is negative-sum after costs: every trade pays fees to someone, and that money leaves the pool of traders. The groups that tend to profit, roughly in order of reliability:

  • Infrastructure. Launchpads earn a fee on every bonding-curve trade and often on post-graduation trades. Trading bots charge around 1 percent per trade. DEXs, validators and MEV searchers collect fees and tips.
  • Creators and insiders. Developers and early wallets who buy at the lowest prices, sometimes through bundled buys that hide their share, can sell to everyone who comes after.
  • Promoters. Paid influencers and groups that receive tokens or fees for promotion. Their followers are often the exit liquidity.
  • Fast, disciplined specialists. A small number of traders with tooling, speed and strict rules profit over time. They are competing against each other and against bots.
  • Lucky retail. Some ordinary traders hit big winners. Their stories spread widely; the much larger group of losers is rarely mentioned. This is survivorship bias; see cognitive biases in trading.

How trading actually happens

Most meme coin trading today follows the same path. A token is created on a launchpad and trades on its bonding curve; if enough money flows in, it graduates to a DEX pool and goes through the stages described in meme coin lifecycle. Traders hold SOL, ETH or BNB in a self-custody wallet or, more often, in a wallet created by a Telegram trading bot or web terminal. They pay network fees, priority fees to land quickly, launchpad or pool fees, and often a bot fee on every swap. Speed-focused traders try sniping new launches; others follow wallets that look profitable. Which chain you use changes costs and risks; see meme coins by chain.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a meme coin like an investment. There is no value to wait for. Holding through a 90 percent drop rarely ends with a recovery.
  • Sizing up after a win. One 20x can lead to oversized bets that give it all back.
  • Buying what is already trending. By the time a token tops a public list, early holders are often looking to sell.
  • Ignoring costs. Priority fees, bot fees and slippage compound quickly over dozens of trades.
  • Skipping basic checks. Many losses come from tokens that a two-minute holder and authority check would have flagged.
  • Keeping funds in a hot wallet tied to bots. Keep only trading money there; see crypto wallet security.

Meme coin trading can resemble gambling: fast outcomes, intermittent big wins, and constant availability. If you find yourself chasing losses or trading money you need, it is worth stepping back. Help for problem gambling is available through national helplines and groups such as Gamblers Anonymous in many countries.

FAQ

Can you get rich from meme coins?

A small number of people have, mostly by being very early or by being insiders. For most traders, meme coins lose money once fees, slippage and the high rate of tokens going to zero are counted, so they are best treated as money you can afford to lose entirely.

Why do most meme coins go to zero?

They have no underlying value, so their price depends on continuous new attention. When attention moves on, buyers stop arriving, early holders sell, and thin liquidity lets the price fall very quickly with no floor.

Are meme coins legal?

Creating, buying and selling them is legal in many countries, but rules vary and change. U.S. SEC staff said in 2025 that typical meme coins are generally not securities, which also means securities-law protections usually do not apply; fraud and manipulation can still be prosecuted under other laws.

What is the difference between a meme coin and an altcoin?

"Altcoin" means any cryptocurrency other than Bitcoin, including tokens with real protocols and revenue. A meme coin is a type of altcoin whose value rests on attention and community rather than on a product. See altcoin trading.
