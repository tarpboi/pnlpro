Telegram Trading Bots

Telegram trading bots are automated services, operated through a Telegram chat or a companion web app, that let traders buy and sell on-chain tokens in a few taps, set limit orders, copy other wallets and snipe new launches. They became the default trading tool for many meme coin traders because they are faster and simpler than connecting a wallet to a DEX. The trade-off is custody: most bots generate and hold a private key for you, which means your funds are only as safe as the bot's security and honesty.

Key takeaways

  • Bots offer speed, convenience and automation that are hard to match by hand, which matters in markets that move in seconds.
  • Most bots hold a private key for a wallet they create for you, so a bot breach, insider theft or a compromised Telegram account can empty it.
  • Fees typically run around 1 percent per trade, plus network and priority fees, which adds up fast for active traders.
  • Several popular bots have been exploited in the past; some reimbursed users, but that is not something to count on.
  • Keep only what you are prepared to lose in a bot wallet, and withdraw profits to a wallet you control.

What trading bots do

Feature What it does Why traders use it
Fast swaps Buy or sell by pasting a contract address Saves time versus a DEX interface
Limit orders Buy or sell when a price or market value is hit DEXs often lack native limit orders
Take-profit and stop-loss Automatic exits at set levels Discipline when away from the screen
Copy trading Mirrors trades of wallets you choose Follow perceived smart money
Sniping Buys a new token at launch or at a trigger Speed at the first block
Anti-MEV / private routing Sends transactions through protected routes Reduce sandwich attacks
Priority fee settings Pay more to be included faster Beat other traders in busy moments
Multi-wallet Trade from several wallets Splitting positions, managing risk

Well-known examples have included Trojan, BONKbot, Maestro, Banana Gun and Sigma, among many others, and web-based terminals offer similar functions with a browser interface. Names, features and fees change frequently. This is a neutral list of examples, not a recommendation.

On limit orders: most AMM DEXs do not keep an order book, so a bot's "limit order" is usually the bot watching the price and sending a market swap when your trigger is hit. Fills can be worse than the trigger in fast markets. See order types.

How custody works

There are two broad models:

  1. Custodial-style bot wallets. The bot generates a new wallet, stores the private key on its servers, and signs trades for you. You can usually export the key. This is the most common model for Telegram bots.
  2. Non-custodial or delegated designs. Some web terminals use embedded wallets with key-sharding or a third-party key provider, or ask you to sign with your own wallet. Security varies a lot between designs, and marketing terms like "non-custodial" deserve scrutiny.

In the first model, if the bot's servers are breached, if an insider goes rogue, or if the operators disappear, your funds can be taken. That is the core risk of using bots. See crypto wallet security.

Other ways funds are lost

  • Telegram account takeover. Anyone who gets into your Telegram account can trade or withdraw from the bot. Use Telegram's two-step verification password and protect the phone number.
  • Fake bots. Impersonator bots with near-identical usernames ask you to import an existing wallet or deposit funds. Only use links from the bot's official, long-standing channels.
  • Importing your main wallet. Some bots let you import a private key. Doing this with a wallet that holds savings puts all of it at the bot's mercy.
  • Malicious "helper" scripts and browser extensions promising faster sniping or free features.

Past exploits, in general terms

Several popular Telegram bots suffered security incidents in 2023 and 2024. Some involved bugs in the bots' router smart contracts that let attackers move tokens users had approved; at least one involved a flaw that allowed attackers to drain wallets the bot controlled. In some cases the operators reimbursed affected users from their own funds; in others, losses were not fully covered. The broader lesson is that a bot is a single point of failure holding many users' keys, which makes it an attractive target.

Fees

Trading bots usually charge a percentage of each trade, often around 1 percent, sometimes less with referral codes or holding the bot's token. On top of that you pay network fees, priority fees or validator tips you choose, and slippage.

Worked example: fees on an active day

A trader makes 40 trades in a day (20 buys and 20 sells), averaging 2 SOL each, using a bot with a 1 percent fee. They pay an average of 0.002 SOL per transaction in priority fees and tips.

  • Bot fees: 40 × 2 SOL × 1% = 0.8 SOL.
  • Priority fees: 40 × 0.002 = 0.08 SOL.
  • Total explicit costs: 0.88 SOL, before slippage and launchpad or pool fees.

If their working balance is 10 SOL, that is 8.8 percent of the balance in one day just to trade. Even a trader who breaks even on price will lose steadily at that pace. See trading costs and fees.

Many bots also run referral programs that pay a share of fees to whoever referred you. This is one reason so many people on social media promote particular bots.

Priority fees, slippage and MEV settings

Bots expose settings that decide whether and how your trades land:

  • Priority fee or tip. On Solana, a priority fee and optional Jito tip increase the chance your transaction is included quickly. On EVM chains the equivalent is the priority gas tip. Higher fees help in busy moments but cost money on every trade.
  • Slippage tolerance. The maximum price move you accept between sending and execution. High slippage makes trades land but invites sandwich attacks, where a bot buys just before you and sells just after.
  • MEV protection. Many bots offer private or protected routing that hides your transaction from public mempools or sandwich bots. It reduces, but does not remove, the risk.

See risk and exits for how to choose slippage and fee settings.

Choosing and using a bot more safely

  • Track record. Prefer bots that have operated for a long time, have a public team or company, and responded transparently to past incidents.
  • Official links only. Get the bot's username from its official site or long-standing channels and double-check it.
  • Fresh wallet, small balance. Use the bot's generated wallet with only trading funds, never a wallet you also use for savings.
  • Sweep profits. Regularly withdraw profits to a self-custody wallet you control, ideally a hardware wallet.
  • Secure Telegram. Enable two-step verification, review active sessions, and be careful with SIM swaps.
  • Back up the key. Export and securely store the bot wallet's private key so you can access funds if the bot goes offline.
  • Test with small amounts before trusting a new bot or feature.

Common mistakes

  • Leaving large balances in a bot wallet. It is a hot wallet held by a third party.
  • Importing a main wallet's private key. That extends the bot's risk to everything in that wallet.
  • Ignoring fees. One percent per trade sounds small until you trade dozens of times a day.
  • Maxing slippage by default. It makes trades land but also makes you an easy sandwich target.
  • Clicking bot links in DMs or replies. Impersonator bots and phishing are common.

FAQ

Are Telegram trading bots safe?

They carry real risk because most hold the private key to your trading wallet, so a breach, insider theft or a hacked Telegram account can drain it. Many traders use them anyway, but only with small balances they are prepared to lose.

How much do Telegram trading bots charge?

Many charge around 1 percent per trade, sometimes less with referrals, plus network fees, priority fees or tips, and slippage. Fees vary by bot and change over time, so check the current rate.

What is the difference between a trading bot and a DEX?

A DEX is the on-chain exchange where the swap actually happens. A trading bot is an app that sends swaps to DEXs or launchpads for you, adding speed, automation and features like limit orders and copy trading.

Can a Telegram bot steal my crypto?

If it holds your private key, a malicious or compromised bot can move your funds. That is why traders use a dedicated wallet with a small balance and withdraw profits to a wallet only they control.
