Sniping New Launches

Sniping is buying a newly created token in its first block or first few seconds, usually with a bot, in the hope of getting the lowest possible price before other buyers arrive. It looks like the obvious way to win at meme coins: buy first, sell to everyone later. In practice, snipers compete with each other on speed and fees, often lose to the creator and insiders who are already holding before the launch is public, and buy thousands of tokens that go nowhere. For most people who try it, sniping has negative expected value.

Key takeaways

  • Sniping is a speed contest decided by infrastructure, priority fees and tips, not by skill at picking tokens.
  • The creator, bundled insider wallets and pre-coordinated groups are usually earlier than any sniper.
  • Most sniped tokens fail, so a sniper needs rare big winners to cover many small losses plus fees on every attempt.
  • Creators increasingly design launches to punish snipers, for example by dumping into them immediately.
  • If you try it, cap the size per snipe and the total daily spend, and track results honestly.

How sniping works

A sniper watches for a trigger, then sends a buy as fast as possible. Common triggers:

  • New token creation on a launchpad, such as any new pump.fun token matching a filter.
  • Graduation or pool creation, when a token migrates from a bonding curve to a DEX pool.
  • Liquidity added or trading enabled on an EVM token launched directly on a DEX.
  • A specific event, such as a known account posting a contract address, or a wallet the sniper tracks deploying a token.

Bots monitor the chain through fast data connections (RPC nodes and streaming feeds) and submit transactions with high priority fees. Most retail snipers use Telegram or web trading bots with sniping features; more advanced operators run their own infrastructure close to validators.

What decides who gets in first

Priority fees and tips

On Solana, transactions can include a priority fee, paid per unit of compute, and many traders also send a tip to Jito, a block-building system used by a large share of validators, to get their transactions included in bundles. On EVM chains, the equivalent is the priority gas tip. In a contested launch, the highest bidders tend to land first. See MEV and sandwich attacks.

This creates an auction. When many snipers chase the same token, they bid fees up, and much of the expected profit goes to validators and block builders as tips.

Latency

Speed depends on how quickly your bot sees the new token and how quickly its transaction reaches the current block leader. Professional operators pay for dedicated nodes, low-latency connections and co-located servers. A retail user on a shared bot is usually several hundred milliseconds behind them, which on Solana can mean landing a block or more later. The same dynamic exists in traditional markets; see high-frequency trading.

Being there before the launch

The creator can buy in the same transaction that creates the token. Using bundles, the creator or partners can also buy from many wallets in that same block. No sniper can beat a buy that is part of the creation itself. Groups that coordinate a launch in private chats know the token before it exists. See KOLs, insiders and cabals.

Why snipers often lose

Problem What happens
Insiders are earlier Creator and bundled wallets hold cheap supply and sell into snipers
Snipe-bait launches Tokens created specifically to attract snipers, then dumped on them within seconds
Fee auctions Priority fees and tips eat into profits on every attempt, winning or losing
Selection Snipers buy many tokens with no information; most fail
Crowded exits Many snipers try to sell to the same small wave of manual buyers
Bot fees and slippage About 1 percent per trade on many bots, plus slippage on thin curves

The snipe-bait problem deserves emphasis. Because sniper behavior is predictable, some creators launch tokens designed to be sniped: they hold supply through bundles, let snipers buy, and sell into them immediately. From a sniper's chart it looks like a token that pumped for two seconds and died.

Worked example: the expected value of indiscriminate sniping

A trader snipes 200 new tokens over a week at 0.2 SOL each, for 40 SOL in total. Each snipe costs 0.005 SOL in priority fees and tips on the buy and the same on the sell, plus a 1 percent bot fee each way. Assume this hypothetical distribution of outcomes:

Outcome Count Sold at SOL returned
Dumped on by insiders or dead 140 0.3x 140 × 0.06 = 8.4
Small move 45 1.2x 45 × 0.24 = 10.8
Decent run 12 3x 12 × 0.6 = 7.2
Big winner 3 15x 3 × 3.0 = 9.0
Total 200 35.4

Costs:

  • Priority fees and tips: 200 snipes × 0.01 SOL = 2.0 SOL.
  • Bot fees: 1 percent of 40 SOL in buys = 0.4 SOL, plus 1 percent of 35.4 SOL in sells ≈ 0.35 SOL, so about 0.75 SOL.

Net: 35.4 − 40 − 2.0 − 0.75 = −7.35 SOL, an 18 percent loss on the capital deployed, even with three 15x winners. Selling the big winners at 15x is also optimistic; it assumes the trader sold near the top and that liquidity was there. These numbers are illustrative, not measured averages, but the structure is typical: many losses, a few wins, and steady fees.

Who sniping works for

Sniping can be profitable for operators who have some combination of: their own low-latency infrastructure, strong filters that skip most launches, information about which creators or groups to follow, and the discipline to exit within seconds. Even then, the edge tends to erode as competitors copy successful filters. For a retail user on a shared bot, the odds are much worse. See risk/reward and expectancy for why a strategy with many small losses needs large, reliable winners.

Risk controls if you snipe

  • Fixed size per snipe. A small, fixed amount, so no single token can hurt much.
  • Daily spend cap. A hard limit on total SOL or ETH committed per day, including fees.
  • Filters. Skip tokens with active mint or freeze authority, heavy bundling, or a creator wallet with a history of rugs. See meme coin due diligence.
  • Automatic exits. Take-profit and stop-loss orders, set before you buy, because these trades move in seconds.
  • Fee ceilings. Cap priority fees and tips so a fee war cannot eat the whole position.
  • Track every attempt. Record wins, losses and costs, and review after a set number of snipes. If the numbers are negative, stop. See trading journal.

The rapid-fire, slot-machine rhythm of sniping makes it easy to keep going after losses. If stopping feels hard, help for problem gambling is available through national helplines and support groups in many countries.

Common mistakes

  • Sniping everything. Without filters, you mostly buy snipe-bait and dead tokens.
  • Raising priority fees after losing races. That can turn a marginal strategy into a guaranteed loss.
  • Counting only the winners. One screenshot of a 50x hides a hundred quiet losses.
  • Holding sniped tokens hoping for a run. Snipes are short-term trades; most tokens die quickly.
  • Using a wallet with savings. Sniping bots hold hot keys; see Telegram trading bots.

FAQ

What is a sniper bot in crypto?

A sniper bot is software that watches the blockchain for a trigger, such as a new token or new liquidity, and immediately sends a buy with high priority fees to get in before other traders. Many Telegram trading bots include sniping features.

Is sniping meme coins profitable?

For most retail traders it is not, because creators and insiders are earlier, many launches are designed to dump on snipers, and fees are paid on every attempt. A small number of well-equipped, selective operators can profit, though their edge tends to fade as others copy them.

How do priority fees affect sniping?

Higher priority fees and validator tips increase the chance your transaction is included first. In contested launches snipers bid fees up against each other, which transfers much of the potential profit to validators and block builders.

Can you snipe tokens on pump.fun?

Yes, many bots can buy pump.fun tokens in their first block, but the creator can buy in the creation transaction itself and insiders can bundle buys into the same block, so snipers rarely get the very lowest prices.
