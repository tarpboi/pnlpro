KOLs, Insiders and Cabals

6 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

In meme coins, KOLs (key opinion leaders, meaning influencers), insider groups and cabals (coordinated groups that launch or pump tokens together) are the people who move attention, and attention is what moves price. Many of them hold tokens before they promote them, are paid to post, or coordinate buying and selling in private. The pattern that results is simple and repeated thousands of times: insiders get cheap supply, the public is told about the token, the public buys, and insiders sell to them. Recognizing this pattern is one of the most important defensive skills in meme coin trading.

Key takeaways

  • Exit liquidity means the buyers that early holders sell to; in most meme coins, that role falls to retail traders who arrive through promotion.
  • Paid and undisclosed promotion is widespread, and a post from a large account often means the poster already holds the token.
  • Cabals coordinate launches, bundle supply across many wallets and time their sells against followers.
  • Celebrity and political tokens have repeatedly seen early holders take large profits while late buyers lost most of their money.
  • Laws on disclosure and manipulation exist in many countries, but enforcement in meme coins has been limited and slow.

What "exit liquidity" means

To sell a token, someone must buy it. In a market with no underlying value, early holders profit only if later buyers pay more. When people say "retail is exit liquidity," they mean the late buyers are not partners in the trade; they are the counterparty to the people selling. See market participants.

KOLs and paid promotion

Influencers on X, Telegram, YouTube and TikTok shape which tokens get attention. Common arrangements include:

  • Token allocations. The KOL receives tokens free or very cheaply before posting.
  • Cash payments for a post, a pinned tweet or a mention in a Telegram call channel.
  • Front-running their own calls. Buying before posting, then selling into followers' buys.
  • Referral and bot deals. Earning a share of trading fees from followers who use a particular bot or platform.

Not every KOL post is paid or dishonest, and some disclose their positions. But undisclosed promotion is common enough that a large account suddenly posting a small token should be treated as a sales pitch, not neutral information. The same incentive problems exist across trading education; see courses, gurus and signals.

Worked example: a promoted post

A KOL with 300,000 followers receives 2 percent of a token's 1 billion supply, 20 million tokens, for free when the token's market value is $80,000. The KOL posts about it, and buying from followers pushes the market value to $1.2 million within an hour.

  • At $1.2 million, the KOL's 20 million tokens are worth 2 percent × $1.2 million = $24,000 at spot.
  • Selling moves the price down, so suppose they realize an average market value of $900,000 across their sells: 2 percent × $900,000 = $18,000.
  • Followers who bought between $500,000 and $1.2 million in market value are now holding a token that, as selling continues, may fall back toward $200,000. A follower who paid at $1 million in market value is down 80 percent.

The KOL made $18,000 for one post and no money at risk. The followers provided all of it.

Insider groups and cabals

A cabal is a group, often organized in private Telegram or Discord chats, that coordinates around launches. Typical behavior:

  1. Decide on a token idea and a launch time in advance.
  2. Have the creator and group members buy in the creation block, often through bundles of fresh wallets. See meme coin due diligence.
  3. Push the token onto trending lists with coordinated buys and, sometimes, wash volume. See finding meme coins.
  4. Activate promoters, call channels and "smart money" wallets that copy traders follow. See copy-trading wallets.
  5. Sell gradually, or all at once, into the buying that follows.

Some groups also run repeat launches: the same team launches token after token under different names, following the same script each time.

Bundled supply

Bundling lets a group take a large share of supply in the first block while spreading it across dozens of wallets. On a holder list, it looks like a broad distribution. Clustering tools that link wallets by funding source can reveal it; many terminals now show a "bundled" percentage. Treat a high bundled share as a sign that one group can dump on everyone else.

Celebrity and political coins

Celebrity tokens have a long history of ending badly for late buyers. A few well-documented examples:

  • In 2022, the U.S. SEC charged Kim Kardashian with failing to disclose that she was paid to promote a crypto token, EthereumMax; she settled for $1.26 million without admitting or denying the findings.
  • In December 2024, the HAWK token linked to internet personality Hailey Welch crashed by more than 90 percent within hours of launch, amid allegations that insiders sold early.
  • In January 2025, the TRUMP token launched days before the U.S. presidential inauguration and reached a multi-billion-dollar market value before falling sharply; most of its supply was held by entities connected to its organizers.
  • In February 2025, Argentine President Javier Milei promoted the LIBRA token on social media. It surged and then collapsed within hours, with on-chain analysis showing insiders cashing out, and it led to investigations and legal action.

The common thread: a famous name brings a huge, sudden wave of buyers, and whoever holds supply before the announcement can sell into it.

How to avoid becoming exit liquidity

  • Ask when the poster bought. If a large account posts after a token has already run several times over, assume they or their partners are selling to you.
  • Check holder concentration and bundles before buying anything promoted. See due diligence.
  • Look for disclosure. No disclosure on a promoted post is a reason to assume it is paid.
  • Distrust urgency. "Last chance", "going to 100x", "this is the one": these phrases sell your buy to someone else's sell.
  • Watch the wallets. If promoter or insider wallets are sending tokens to exchanges or selling into the pool, the pitch is over.
  • Avoid buying the first minutes of celebrity coins. That is when early holders have the most to sell.

Legal issues in general terms

The legal position depends on the country and on whether a token counts as a security or other regulated asset:

  • United States. SEC staff said in February 2025 that typical meme coins are generally not securities, so securities rules on promoter disclosure may not apply. Fraud, wire fraud and market manipulation can still be prosecuted, and the Federal Trade Commission's endorsement guides require influencers to disclose paid relationships. Private lawsuits against creators, promoters and platforms have been filed.
  • United Kingdom. Since October 2023, the FCA's financial promotion rules have applied to cryptoasset marketing aimed at UK consumers, including by influencers.
  • European Union. The MiCA regulation includes market abuse rules, such as bans on insider dealing and market manipulation, for crypto-assets admitted to trading on regulated crypto platforms.

Rules change often, and enforcement in meme coins has been sporadic. For the broader picture, see regulators and investor protection. In practice, the existence of a law rarely helps someone who has already lost money on a dumped token.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a large follower count as credibility. Big accounts are the most valuable for paid promotion.
  • Assuming a celebrity token has protection. Fame brings buyers, not a floor.
  • Following call channels after the move. You are buying from the people who called it.
  • Ignoring bundles because the holder list looks spread out. One group can control many wallets.
  • Believing "community takeover" claims without checking wallets. The original insiders may still hold.

FAQ

What is exit liquidity in crypto?

Exit liquidity is the pool of buyers that early holders sell to when they take profit. In meme coins, late retail buyers attracted by promotion often play this role.

What is a crypto cabal?

A cabal is a coordinated group that launches or pumps tokens together, usually buying supply early through many wallets, promoting the token, and selling into the buyers that follow.

Are crypto influencers required to disclose paid promotions?

In many countries, yes, under advertising or financial promotion rules, such as the FTC's endorsement guides in the US and the FCA's rules in the UK. Compliance and enforcement in meme coins are inconsistent, so many promotions go undisclosed.

Why do celebrity meme coins crash?

A famous name brings a sudden wave of buyers, and insiders who held supply before the launch can sell into that demand. Once the initial attention fades, there is little to support the price.
