Rug Pulls and Honeypots

A rug pull is a scam in which the people behind a token take buyers' money and leave, either by withdrawing the liquidity that lets the token trade or by dumping a hidden supply onto the market. A honeypot is a token whose contract lets people buy but stops them from selling. Both are common in meme coins because anyone can create a token in minutes, with no identity checks and almost no cost. This article explains how each type works so you can recognize the warning signs; it is not a guide to running one.

Key takeaways

  • Hard rugs drain the pool; soft rugs dump insider supply; honeypots block sells; all leave holders with tokens they cannot turn back into money.
  • Most hard rugs and honeypots can be detected in advance with a few contract and liquidity checks.
  • Soft rugs are harder to spot, because the contract is clean and the damage comes from concentrated supply.
  • "Renounced", "LP locked" and "audited" are claims to verify, not guarantees.
  • Once you are rugged, funds are almost never recovered; anyone offering recovery for a fee is very likely running a second scam.

Types of rug pulls

Type How holders lose Main warning signs
Liquidity pull (hard rug) Pool emptied; token cannot be sold for value LP not burned or locked; deployer holds LP tokens
Dev dump Creator sells a large hidden or open allocation High dev or cluster share; bundled launch
Soft rug (slow rug) Team sells gradually and abandons the project Insider wallets trickling out; fading socials
Honeypot Buys work, sells fail or are taxed to nothing Sell simulation fails; few or no successful sellers
Hidden or changeable tax Sell tax raised to an extreme level Tax functions in contract; owner can change fees
Mint exploit New supply minted and sold Mint authority or mint function still active
Freeze trap (Solana) Holder accounts frozen Freeze authority not revoked

Liquidity pull (hard rug)

In a classic DEX launch, the creator deposits the token and a quote asset such as SOL or ETH into a pool and receives LP tokens representing that liquidity. If the creator keeps those LP tokens, they can withdraw the pool at any time. After a withdrawal, the token still exists but there is almost nothing to sell it for; its price collapses to near zero in one transaction.

Bonding-curve launchpads reduced this risk for their own tokens, because they seed the pool themselves at graduation and typically burn or lock the LP. See launchpads and bonding curves. Tokens launched directly on a DEX, which is still common on EVM chains, do not have that protection.

How to spot it: check the LP status on a block explorer or token checker. Burned LP (sent to a dead address) cannot be withdrawn. Locked LP is safe only until the lock expires, and only if the locker contract is a known one. Watch for very short locks that expire within hours or days.

Dev dump

The creator, or insiders working with the creator, accumulate a large share of supply at the lowest prices, often through many wallets in the launch block so the holder list looks spread out. When buyers arrive, they sell. The contract is clean and the liquidity is locked, but the price still collapses because most of the supply was sold by a few people to everyone else.

How to spot it: look for bundled buys at launch, wallets funded from the same source, and a high combined share for the top holders after excluding the pool. See meme coin due diligence.

Soft rug (slow rug)

In a soft rug, the team does not disappear in one move. They keep posting for a while, sell gradually into every bounce, and eventually stop communicating. It can be hard to distinguish from a project that simply failed, and in meme coins the line is blurry.

How to spot it: track the known team or insider wallets. Regular outflows from them while socials promise "big things coming" is a classic pattern.

Honeypots

A honeypot contract allows buys but makes selling impossible or worthless. Common mechanisms on EVM chains include:

  • A sell function that reverts (fails) for everyone except whitelisted addresses.
  • A blacklist that the owner fills with buyer addresses.
  • A sell tax set at or near 100 percent.
  • A maximum sell amount so small it is useless.
  • Trading "paused" for everyone but the owner.

One of the best-known examples was the Squid Game-themed SQUID token in late 2021, which rose sharply while holders found they could not sell, before its creators cashed out and the price went to near zero.

On Solana, standard SPL tokens cannot block sells through the token contract itself, but an active freeze authority can freeze holder accounts, and some Token-2022 extensions (such as transfer hooks or a permanent delegate) can be abused in similar ways.

How to spot it:

  • Run the token through a honeypot simulator, which attempts a test buy and sell.
  • Look at the transaction list for successful sells by ordinary wallets, not just buys.
  • Read the verified contract on the explorer for blacklist, pause, fee-setting or max-transaction functions controlled by the owner.
  • Be suspicious of unverified contracts, which cannot be read.

Some honeypots are designed to fool simulators, for example by allowing sells for the first few minutes or for small sizes. Simulation is a strong signal, not a guarantee.

Worked example: the cost of a changeable tax

A trader buys $500 of an EVM token with a 5 percent buy tax, so $475 worth of tokens arrive. The token doubles, and the position is worth $950. The owner then changes the sell tax from 5 percent to 90 percent.

  • Expected proceeds at 5 percent: $950 × 0.95 = $902.50.
  • Actual proceeds at 90 percent: $950 × 0.10 = $95.
  • Net result: an 81 percent loss on the original $500, on a token that went up.

A contract that lets the owner change fees without a hard cap allows exactly this outcome.

Fake safety signals

Scammers know which checks traders run and try to fake them:

  • Fake renounce. Ownership is renounced on the main contract, but the logic sits behind an upgradeable proxy, or a separate privileged role can still mint, blacklist or change fees.
  • Fake or short LP lock. Liquidity is "locked" for a few hours, on an unknown locker, or only a small part of the LP is locked.
  • Fake audits. A badge from an unknown firm, or an audit of a different contract.
  • Copycat contracts. A token with the same name and ticker as a popular coin, waiting for buyers who search by name instead of contract address.
  • Fake "migration" or "V2". Holders are told to swap old tokens for new ones through a link, which can lead to a wallet drainer. See crypto wallet security.

A defensive routine

Before buying any token you have not checked before:

  1. Copy the contract address from a trusted source and confirm it on a block explorer.
  2. Check mint authority, freeze authority (Solana) or owner functions (EVM).
  3. Check LP status: burned, locked (until when, where), or unlocked.
  4. Run a honeypot simulation on EVM tokens and look for real sells.
  5. Check holder concentration after excluding the pool, and look for bundled or linked wallets.
  6. Never sign a transaction from a link promising airdrops, claims or migrations.

If you get rugged

On-chain transactions cannot be reversed, and funds sent to a scammer are rarely recovered. You can report the token and wallets to the platforms involved and, for larger losses, to the police or the relevant authority in your country. Be very wary of anyone who contacts you offering to recover funds for an upfront fee; recovery scams target people who have just lost money. See trading scams.

Common mistakes

  • Trusting a clean token checker score. It filters obvious scams but misses linked wallets and new tricks.
  • Buying by name or ticker. Copycats exploit this constantly.
  • Assuming launchpad tokens cannot rug. They avoid hard rugs, but dev dumps and soft rugs remain.
  • Ignoring the sell side. Always confirm that ordinary wallets have sold successfully.
  • Doubling down to "average down" after a rug. There is nothing left to recover.

FAQ

How can you tell if a meme coin is a rug pull?

Check whether liquidity is burned or locked, whether mint and freeze authority are revoked, and whether a few linked wallets hold a large share of supply. Unlocked liquidity, active authorities and heavy insider concentration are the main warning signs.

What is a honeypot token?

A honeypot is a token whose contract lets people buy but blocks or heavily taxes selling, so buyers cannot exit. It is mostly found on EVM chains, where token contracts can include custom sell restrictions.

What is a soft rug?

A soft rug is when a token's team or insiders gradually sell their holdings and abandon the project rather than pulling liquidity in one move. The contract may be clean, which makes it harder to detect.

Can you get money back after a rug pull?

Almost never, because blockchain transactions cannot be reversed and scammers move funds quickly. Report it, but avoid anyone offering paid recovery services, as these are commonly scams.
