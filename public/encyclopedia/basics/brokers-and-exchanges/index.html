Brokers and Exchanges

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

An exchange is a marketplace where buy and sell orders meet, a broker is a firm that gives you access to markets and holds your account, and a dealer is a firm that trades with you directly from its own book. Most retail traders never touch an exchange directly; they reach it through a broker, or trade against a dealer without realizing it. Which kind of firm sits between you and the market affects your costs, your conflicts of interest and, above all, the safety of your money.

Key takeaways

  • Exchanges match orders; brokers route your orders and hold your account; dealers take the other side of your trades.
  • Custody, meaning who holds your assets and how they are separated from the firm's own money, is the most important safety question.
  • Regulated firms in major jurisdictions must meet capital, segregation and conduct rules, while offshore and unregulated firms often do not.
  • Brokers earn money through commissions, spreads, financing, payment for order flow, interest on cash and, for dealers, client losses.
  • Investor protection schemes cover failure of a firm in some countries, never trading losses, and limits apply.

Exchanges, brokers and dealers

Exchange Broker (agency) Dealer (market maker)
What it does Runs the marketplace and matching engine Routes your orders to markets and holds your account Quotes prices and trades with you from its own book
Your counterparty Another market participant, usually via a clearing house Another market participant The dealer itself
How it earns Trading, listing and data fees Commissions, spreads, financing, order routing payments The spread and, in some models, client losses
Examples by type Stock exchanges, futures exchanges, centralized crypto exchanges Stock and futures brokers Many retail forex and CFD providers

The lines blur. Many centralized crypto exchanges act as exchange, broker and custodian at once. Many retail forex and CFD providers are dealers that pass some client orders to outside liquidity providers and keep others in-house, an arrangement sometimes called A-book and B-book. When a dealer keeps the other side of your trade, your loss is its gain, which is a direct conflict of interest. See forex brokers.

Exchanges for stocks and futures usually work with a clearing house that stands between buyer and seller, guaranteeing that trades settle even if one party fails. This central clearing is one reason exchange-traded markets are considered safer than many over-the-counter ones. See order book and market depth.

Custody: who holds your money

Custody is the question that matters most when things go wrong.

  • Segregated client accounts. Regulated brokers in major jurisdictions must keep client money and assets separate from the firm's own, so that creditors cannot claim them if the firm fails.
  • Omnibus accounts. Brokers often hold client assets pooled in one account at a custodian, with their own records of who owns what. This is normal, but it depends on accurate records and honest management.
  • Crypto custody. On a centralized crypto exchange, you hold a claim on the exchange, not coins in your own wallet. The collapse of FTX in 2022 showed that customer funds can be misused when segregation is weak or ignored. Some exchanges publish proof of reserves, which helps but does not prove that liabilities are fully covered. See CEX vs DEX.
  • Self-custody. On decentralized exchanges, you keep control of your assets in your own wallet, but you also take full responsibility for security.

Regulated vs offshore

A broker licensed by a well-established regulator, such as those in the US, the UK, the EU, Australia, Japan, Singapore or Hong Kong, typically has to meet minimum capital requirements, segregate client money, report to the regulator, handle complaints fairly and, in some cases, offer access to a compensation scheme.

Offshore firms registered in jurisdictions with light supervision often offer higher leverage, looser account rules and bonuses that major regulators restrict. In exchange, clients usually get weaker protection, less recourse if something goes wrong and more risk of withdrawal problems. Some firms claim to be regulated by naming a company registration, not a financial license, or by citing a license held by a different entity in the same group. You can verify a license on the regulator's own public register. See regulators and investor protection.

How brokers make money

Understanding a broker's revenue tells you where its incentives lie.

  • Commissions per trade or per contract.
  • Spreads, either the market spread plus a markup or a spread set by the dealer.
  • Financing: margin interest, overnight swaps and funding charges.
  • Interest on client cash: the broker earns interest on uninvested balances and passes on some or none of it.
  • Payment for order flow from market makers, where legal.
  • Securities lending: lending out client shares to short sellers.
  • Currency conversion fees.
  • Client losses, in dealer models that keep client trades in-house.

All the trading-related items are covered in trading costs and fees.

Worked example: comparing two forex accounts

A trader expects to trade 40 standard lots of EUR/USD a month, where one pip is worth $10 per lot.

  • Account A (spread only): average spread 1.2 pips, no commission. Cost per round trip = 1.2 x $10 = $12.
  • Account B (raw spread plus commission): average spread 0.2 pips plus $3.50 commission per lot each side. Cost per round trip = 0.2 x $10 + $7.00 = $9.

Monthly cost: Account A = 40 x $12 = $480; Account B = 40 x $9 = $360. Account B saves $120 a month, or $1,440 a year, at this volume. For a trader doing 2 lots a month, the difference is $6 a month and other factors, such as regulation and execution quality, matter far more.

Investor protection schemes

Several countries run schemes that compensate clients if a regulated firm fails and client assets are missing. They do not cover losses from trading or falling prices. Examples, described in general terms:

  • United States: the Securities Investor Protection Corporation (SIPC) covers customers of member broker-dealers up to $500,000, including up to $250,000 for cash, as of 2026. It does not cover most futures, forex or crypto accounts.
  • United Kingdom: the Financial Services Compensation Scheme covers eligible investment claims up to a set limit per person per firm.
  • European Union: national investor compensation schemes must cover at least a minimum amount set by EU law, and some countries go higher.
  • Elsewhere: many other countries have schemes, and many do not. Offshore brokers rarely offer any.

Limits and eligibility change, so check the current rules for the specific firm and account type.

Checklist for choosing a broker or exchange

  1. License. Is the specific entity you will contract with licensed by a respected regulator? Check the regulator's register yourself.
  2. Custody. How are client funds held and segregated? Is there a compensation scheme?
  3. Markets and products. Does it offer what you actually plan to trade, with the order types you need?
  4. Total costs. Compare spreads, commissions, financing and fees on your expected trading pattern, not on headline claims.
  5. Execution. Is it an agency broker or a dealer? Does it publish execution quality data?
  6. Withdrawals. How fast and at what cost? Search for user reports of withdrawal problems.
  7. Platform and reliability. Is the platform stable during busy markets? Are stops held at the exchange or on the broker's servers?
  8. Support and history. How long has the firm operated, and how does it handle complaints?
  9. Negative balance protection. Can you lose more than your deposit?

Red flags

  • Unsolicited contact by phone, social media or messaging apps.
  • Promises of guaranteed returns or "risk-free" trading.
  • Pressure to deposit more, especially to unlock a withdrawal or pay a "tax" or "fee" first.
  • Large deposit bonuses with conditions that lock your money.
  • No verifiable license, or a license number that does not match the regulator's register.
  • An "account manager" who trades for you or urges you to trade more.
  • An app or website that cannot be found in official app stores or that copies a well-known brand.

Many of these are signs of outright fraud rather than a bad broker. See trading scams and how to start trading.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing the broker with the highest leverage or biggest bonus. These are often signs of weaker regulation.
  • Trusting a regulator's logo on a website. Verify on the regulator's own register.
  • Keeping all funds on one crypto exchange. Counterparty risk is real even at large exchanges.
  • Ignoring withdrawal terms until you need your money. Test a small withdrawal early.

FAQ

What is the difference between a broker and an exchange?

An exchange is the marketplace where orders are matched. A broker is the firm that gives you access to that marketplace, holds your account and sends your orders on your behalf.

How do I know if a broker is regulated?

Find the broker's license number and look it up on the official public register of the regulator it names. Make sure the entity on the register is the same one named in your account agreement.

Is my money protected if my broker goes bust?

In some countries, compensation schemes cover missing client assets up to a limit if a regulated firm fails, such as SIPC in the US. They never cover trading losses, and many offshore and crypto firms offer no such protection.

How do zero-commission brokers make money?

They earn from spreads, payment for order flow where legal, interest on client cash, margin lending, securities lending and other fees.
