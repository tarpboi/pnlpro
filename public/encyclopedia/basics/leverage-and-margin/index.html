Leverage and Margin

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Leverage means controlling a position larger than the cash you put up, and margin is the cash or collateral a broker or exchange requires you to deposit to hold that position. Leverage multiplies gains and losses by the same factor, so a small price move against a highly leveraged position can wipe out the account. Margin rules decide when you are asked to add money and when your position is closed for you. Leverage is the single biggest reason retail traders lose money quickly.

Key takeaways

  • Leverage is position size divided by your own capital; at 10:1, a 1% price move changes your equity by 10%.
  • Initial margin is what you need to open a position, and maintenance margin is the minimum you must keep to hold it.
  • Falling below maintenance margin triggers a margin call or, at many brokers and crypto exchanges, automatic liquidation.
  • Cross margin shares your whole balance across positions, while isolated margin limits each position's loss to the margin assigned to it.
  • Regulators in many regions cap retail leverage, and those caps differ by country and change over time.

What leverage is

Leverage ratio = position value / your equity. If you hold a $50,000 position with $5,000 of your own money, your leverage is 10:1, or 10x.

The rest of the position is effectively financed. In a stock margin account, the broker lends you cash and charges interest. In futures, CFDs and crypto perpetuals, you do not borrow the full amount; you post margin as a performance bond and gain or lose the full value of the price change.

How leverage scales results

The effect of a 5% price move on your equity, ignoring costs:

Leverage Gain if price rises 5% Loss if price falls 5%
1:1 +5% -5%
5:1 +25% -25%
10:1 +50% -50%
20:1 +100% -100% (equity wiped out)
50:1 +250% Wiped out after a 2% fall

A useful rule of thumb: at leverage L, a price move of about 1/L against you erases your entire equity. At 20:1 that is a 5% move; at 100:1 it is 1%. Many assets move that much in a day.

Initial and maintenance margin

  • Initial margin is the deposit needed to open a position. It may be stated as a percentage of the position (for example 10%) or as a fixed amount per contract.
  • Maintenance margin is the minimum equity you must keep while the position is open. It is lower than initial margin.

Your equity is your deposit plus or minus unrealized profit and loss. When equity falls below maintenance margin, the broker acts.

Margin calls and liquidation

A margin call is a demand to bring your account back above the required level, either by depositing more money or by reducing positions. Traditional stock and futures brokers may give you a short window to respond, but they are not obliged to wait in fast markets.

Liquidation is the forced closing of your position. Many CFD brokers, forex brokers and crypto exchanges liquidate automatically, with no call at all, once equity hits a threshold. In the EU and UK, retail CFD brokers must close positions when equity falls to 50% of the required margin. Crypto exchanges typically liquidate when equity reaches the maintenance level and may charge a liquidation fee. See crypto liquidations.

Liquidation is not a safety net that guarantees you lose only your margin. In a gap or a fast market, the closing price can be worse than the liquidation level, and in some account types you can end up owing money. Negative balance protection, required for retail CFD clients in the EU and UK and offered by some other brokers, prevents losses beyond the account balance.

Worked examples

Stock margin account (US-style)

In the US, Regulation T has long set initial margin for stock purchases at 50%, and FINRA requires maintenance margin of at least 25%, though many brokers require more.

A trader with $10,000 buys $20,000 of stock, borrowing $10,000. Leverage is 2:1.

  • The loan stays at $10,000 whatever the stock does.
  • A margin call comes when equity / position value falls to 25%: (V - 10,000) / V = 0.25.
  • Solving: V = 10,000 / 0.75 = $13,333.33.

The position can fall from $20,000 to about $13,333, a drop of one third, before a call at the 25% minimum. At that point the trader's equity is $3,333, a loss of two thirds of the original $10,000.

Forex at 30:1

One standard lot of EUR/USD is 100,000 euros. At a rate of 1.1000, that is $110,000 of exposure. At the EU and UK retail cap of 30:1 on major pairs, required margin is $110,000 / 30 = $3,666.67.

A move of 110 pips (1.1000 to 1.0890), which is a 1% move, costs 110 x $10 = $1,100. That is 30% of the margin. A 3.3% move would erase the margin entirely, and the broker's 50% close-out rule would trigger well before that.

Crypto perpetual at 10x, isolated margin

A trader posts $1,000 of isolated margin and opens a $10,000 long position in Bitcoin at $50,000, which is 0.2 BTC. Assume the exchange's maintenance margin is 0.5% of position value, or $50.

  • Liquidation occurs roughly when losses reach $1,000 - $50 = $950.
  • Price drop that causes this: $950 / 0.2 = $4,750.
  • Approximate liquidation price: $50,000 - $4,750 = $45,250, a fall of 9.5%.

Real exchanges use their own formulas, mark prices and fees, so the actual level differs slightly. The point is that a 10x position is closed after a move of less than 10%, which Bitcoin has made within a single day many times.

Cross vs isolated margin

These terms are most common on crypto derivatives exchanges, though the idea applies elsewhere.

Isolated margin Cross margin
Collateral Only the margin assigned to that position Your whole account balance
Maximum loss on one position The isolated margin (barring gaps and fees) Potentially the whole account
Liquidation distance Closer, because less collateral backs the position Further, because the whole balance backs it
Best for Limiting damage from any one trade Hedged or offsetting positions

Cross margin can keep a losing position open longer, which feels safer but can turn one bad trade into a lost account. Isolated margin caps the loss per position but liquidates sooner.

Regulatory leverage caps

Many regulators limit the leverage offered to retail clients. As of 2026, the broad picture is as follows, but rules change and differ for professional clients, so check the current rules where you live.

  • EU and UK (CFDs and spread bets): 30:1 on major currency pairs; 20:1 on non-major currency pairs, gold and major stock indices; 10:1 on other commodities and minor indices; 5:1 on individual shares; and, in the EU, 2:1 on cryptocurrencies. The UK instead bans the sale of crypto derivatives to retail clients outright.
  • Australia: similar caps to the EU for retail CFD clients.
  • United States: retail forex leverage is capped at 50:1 on major pairs and 20:1 on others; retail CFDs are not permitted; stock margin is governed by Regulation T and FINRA rules.
  • Japan: retail forex leverage is capped at 25:1.
  • Offshore brokers and exchanges: some offer 500:1 or more on forex and over 100x on crypto. Higher available leverage usually comes with weaker client protection. See brokers and exchanges.

Leverage caps for futures are set indirectly through exchange margin requirements, which change with volatility.

Common mistakes

  • Using maximum available leverage. The leverage a broker offers is a limit, not a recommendation. Effective leverage should come from your position sizing, not the account setting.
  • Confusing margin with risk. Posting $1,000 of margin on a 20x position does not mean you are risking $1,000 safely; it means a 5% move ends the trade.
  • Adding margin to save a losing trade. Repeatedly depositing to avoid liquidation often turns a small loss into a large one.
  • Ignoring financing costs. Leveraged positions held overnight usually pay interest, swaps or funding. See trading costs and fees.
  • Forgetting gaps. Weekends, news and halts can move price past your liquidation level. Historical blowups are covered in leverage risk and black swans.

FAQ

What does 10x leverage mean?

It means your position is 10 times larger than the money you put up. A 1% move in price changes your equity by about 10%, and a 10% move against you can wipe it out.

What is the difference between initial and maintenance margin?

Initial margin is the deposit required to open a position. Maintenance margin is the lower minimum you must keep while holding it; falling below it triggers a margin call or liquidation.

Can you lose more than your deposit with leverage?

Yes, in some accounts. If the market gaps past your liquidation level, losses can exceed your margin. Retail CFD clients in the EU and UK have negative balance protection, but this is not universal.

Is cross margin or isolated margin safer?

Isolated margin limits the loss on each position to the margin assigned to it, which protects the rest of your account. Cross margin delays liquidation by using your whole balance, but a single bad trade can then consume everything.
