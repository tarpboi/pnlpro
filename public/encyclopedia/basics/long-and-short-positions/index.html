Long and Short Positions

6 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Going long means buying an asset to profit if its price rises; going short means selling an asset you do not own, or taking an equivalent derivative position, to profit if its price falls. Both are basic tools in almost every market. They are not mirror images, though: a long position can lose at most what was paid, while a short position can in theory lose without limit. Understanding the mechanics and the costs of each is essential before using either.

Key takeaways

  • A long position gains when price rises and loses when it falls; the maximum loss on an unleveraged long is the amount invested.
  • A short position gains when price falls and loses when it rises; because prices have no ceiling, losses on a short are theoretically unlimited.
  • Shorting can be done by borrowing and selling shares, or through CFDs, futures, perpetual futures, put options and inverse products.
  • Short positions usually carry ongoing costs, such as borrow fees, dividends owed or funding payments.
  • Hedging uses an offsetting position to reduce risk on an existing one, at the cost of giving up some potential gain.

Going long

A long position is the intuitive one: buy now, sell later at a hopefully higher price. Buying 100 shares at $40 and selling them at $46 earns $600 before costs. If the shares fall to $34, the loss is $600. If the company goes bankrupt and the shares go to zero, the loss is the full $4,000, but no more.

Long positions in shares and spot crypto can be held indefinitely without a time limit. Leveraged longs, such as on margin, CFDs or perpetual futures, add financing costs and the risk of forced liquidation. See leverage and margin.

Going short

A short position profits from a fall. The classic version in shares works in three steps:

  1. Borrow the shares, usually arranged automatically by your broker from its own inventory or from other clients and lenders.
  2. Sell the borrowed shares at the current price and receive the cash.
  3. Buy back the same number of shares later (called covering) and return them to the lender.

If the price fell in between, you buy back for less than you sold and keep the difference. If it rose, you buy back for more and lose the difference. Detailed mechanics, including locates, hard-to-borrow stocks and short-sale restrictions, are covered in short selling.

Worked example: a short stock trade

A trader shorts 100 shares at $40, receiving $4,000. The broker charges a borrow fee of 3% a year on the position value, and the trade is held for 30 days.

  • Borrow fee: $4,000 x 3% x 30 / 365 = about $9.86
  • If the price falls to $30: buy back for $3,000. Gross profit $1,000, net about $990.
  • If the price rises to $50: buy back for $5,000. Gross loss $1,000, net loss about $1,010.
  • If the price rises to $80: buy back for $8,000. Gross loss $4,000, which equals 100% of the original sale proceeds.
  • If the price rises to $120: gross loss $8,000, or 200% of the proceeds.

The long side of the same trade could never lose more than $4,000. The short side has no such limit. If the company also paid a dividend during the 30 days, the short seller would owe that amount to the lender.

The asymmetry of short risk

Long Short
Profit when Price rises Price falls
Maximum profit Unlimited The price falling to zero (100% of entry value)
Maximum loss (unleveraged) 100% of the amount invested Unlimited in theory
Effect of a winning trade on position size Position grows as a share of the account Position shrinks as a share of the account
Effect of a losing trade on position size Position shrinks Position grows, adding risk
Typical ongoing costs Margin interest if leveraged Borrow fees, dividends, funding, financing

The last two rows matter in practice. When a short goes wrong, the position grows larger relative to the account, which can force the trader to cover at the worst moment. When many short sellers are forced to buy back at once, the buying itself drives the price higher, which is called a short squeeze. The rise of GameStop shares in January 2021 is a well-known example.

Ways to go short

Different markets offer different ways to profit from a fall.

Method How it works Key costs and risks
Borrow and sell shares Classic short sale through a margin account Borrow fees, dividends owed, recall risk, squeeze risk
CFDs and spread bets Sell a contract with a broker; no borrowing needed by you Financing charges, broker as counterparty; not available to US retail clients. See CFDs and spread betting
Futures Sell a futures contract; shorting is as easy as buying Leverage, daily mark-to-market, expiry and rollover. See futures basics
Crypto perpetual futures Open a short perpetual position Funding payments, liquidation risk. See perpetual futures
Buy put options Pay a premium for the right to sell at a set price Loss limited to the premium, but time decay works against you. See options basics
Inverse ETFs Buy a fund designed to rise when an index falls Daily reset causes drift over longer periods

Buying a put is the only common method where the maximum loss is fixed in advance. The trade-off is that the premium is lost entirely if the price does not fall far enough before expiry.

Hedging

A hedge is a position taken to offset the risk of another. Hedges reduce losses in a bad scenario but also reduce gains in a good one, and they usually cost something.

Worked example: hedging a crypto holding

A trader holds 0.5 BTC bought long ago and does not want to sell it, perhaps for tax reasons, but expects a short-term fall. They open a short perpetual futures position of 0.5 BTC at $60,000.

  • If Bitcoin falls to $50,000: the spot holding loses 0.5 x $10,000 = $5,000. The short gains 0.5 x $10,000 = $5,000. Net change: about zero, minus funding and fees.
  • If Bitcoin rises to $70,000: the spot holding gains $5,000 and the short loses $5,000. Net change: about zero again.

The hedge locks in the current value for as long as it is held. The trader gives up the upside to remove the downside, and pays funding whenever it turns negative while the short is open (when it is positive, the short receives it). Partial hedges, such as shorting 0.25 BTC, reduce rather than remove the exposure.

Hedging is common among businesses as well as traders. Airlines hedge fuel costs, farmers hedge crop prices and exporters hedge exchange rates. These hedgers are an important group of market participants.

Common mistakes

  • Shorting without a stop or defined exit. A short that keeps rising can grow into a loss larger than the account.
  • Shorting heavily shorted, small-float stocks. These are the most prone to violent squeezes.
  • Ignoring carry costs. Borrow fees on hard-to-borrow stocks can exceed 50% a year, and funding on crowded perpetual shorts can be costly over time.
  • Treating a hedge as free. Offsetting positions still pay spreads, fees and financing.
  • Assuming falling markets are easier to trade. Declines are often fast and interrupted by sharp rallies that stop out short sellers.

FAQ

What does it mean to go short?

Going short means taking a position that profits when the price falls. In shares, it usually means borrowing shares, selling them, and buying them back later to return to the lender.

Why is short selling risky?

A share price can rise without limit, so a short position's potential loss is unlimited in theory. Short positions also grow larger as they lose, and crowded shorts can be hit by squeezes that force buying at high prices.

What is the difference between a long and a short position?

A long position profits when the price rises and can lose at most the amount invested if unleveraged. A short position profits when the price falls and can lose more than the amount of the original sale.

Can you short crypto?

Yes. Traders can short crypto through perpetual futures, regular futures, options and, in some countries, CFDs. Each method carries leverage, funding or financing costs, and liquidation risk.
