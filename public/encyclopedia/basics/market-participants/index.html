Market Participants

Market participants are the people and organizations that buy and sell in financial markets, from individual retail traders to pension funds, hedge funds, market makers, high-frequency trading firms, hedgers and central banks. Every trade has someone on the other side, and that someone has their own goals, information and costs. Knowing who they are helps explain how prices move, why spreads exist and why a typical retail trader is at a disadvantage in some markets.

Key takeaways

  • Participants differ in their goals: some seek profit from price moves, some need to hedge business risks, some provide liquidity and some pursue policy aims.
  • Market makers and high-frequency firms are on the other side of a large share of retail trades in liquid markets, earning the spread rather than betting on direction.
  • Hedgers and long-term investors are often willing to pay to transfer risk, which is one source of returns for those who take it.
  • Better-informed or faster participants tend to win at the expense of slower ones, an effect called adverse selection.
  • A buyer and seller can both be acting sensibly; they may simply have different goals or time horizons.

The main participants

Retail traders

Individuals trading their own money through brokers and apps. Retail activity has grown with zero-commission brokers, mobile apps and crypto. Retail traders are diverse, but as a group they tend to trade smaller sizes, use more leverage in some markets, and react more to news and social media. The evidence on their results is summarized in what is trading.

Institutional investors

Pension funds, insurance companies, mutual funds, sovereign wealth funds and endowments manage very large pools of money, mostly for the long term. Their size means they move prices when they trade, so they split orders over hours or days using execution algorithms. Their flows, such as month-end rebalancing or index changes, can create predictable demand that others try to anticipate.

Hedge funds and proprietary trading firms

Hedge funds manage money for wealthy clients and institutions using a wide range of strategies, from long-short equity to macro bets on currencies and rates to systematic quantitative models. Proprietary trading firms trade their own capital. Both employ full-time researchers and pay for data and technology that individual traders cannot match.

Market makers

Market makers continuously quote both a bid and an ask, standing ready to buy from sellers and sell to buyers. They aim to earn the bid-ask spread many times over while keeping their inventory small. Their main risks are holding inventory when prices move and trading against better-informed participants. In US stocks, much retail order flow is filled by wholesale market makers. In crypto and prediction markets, professional trading firms and automated strategies supply much of the liquidity.

High-frequency trading firms

HFT firms use very fast computers, often located next to exchange servers, to trade in fractions of a second. Most of their activity is market making and arbitrage between related prices. They have narrowed spreads in many markets, but they also compete hard to react before others, which makes speed-based strategies unrealistic for retail traders. See high-frequency trading.

Hedgers

Hedgers are businesses and investors who trade to reduce a risk they already face, not to profit from price moves. Examples include an airline locking in fuel costs, a farmer selling crop futures before harvest, a miner selling gold forward and an exporter selling foreign currency it expects to receive. Hedgers are often willing to accept a slightly worse price for certainty, which creates opportunities for those who take the other side.

Central banks and governments

Central banks set interest rates, buy and sell government bonds, and occasionally intervene directly in currency markets. Their aim is policy, such as stable prices or a stable currency, not profit, so they can trade in huge size regardless of price. Japan's Ministry of Finance, for example, intervened to support the yen in 2022 and again in 2024. See central banks and interest rates.

Whales

In crypto and other thinly traded markets, a "whale" is an individual or entity holding enough of an asset to move its price. Because many blockchains are public, traders track large wallets for clues, though wallets can be misleading and large holders can split activity across many addresses. See on-chain analysis.

Who is on the other side of your trade?

The answer depends on the market and how your order is handled.

  • US stocks through a retail broker: often a wholesale market maker that buys your order flow, or another participant on an exchange.
  • Futures: anyone else trading that contract, from hedgers to hedge funds to other retail traders, with a clearing house in the middle.
  • Retail forex and CFDs: frequently the broker itself, if it keeps your trade in-house. See brokers and exchanges.
  • Crypto exchanges: other traders on the order book, with professional market makers supplying much of the resting liquidity.
  • Prediction markets: other traders, including specialists and automated market makers.

The key question is not who they are, but whether they know more than you. When you trade against someone with better information, you tend to get filled precisely when the trade is about to go against you. This adverse selection is why market makers widen spreads around news and why retail traders who react to headlines tend to arrive late.

Worked example: two participants with different goals

The market maker

A market maker quotes a stock at $100.00 bid, $100.02 ask. Over a morning it buys 5,000 shares at $100.00 from sellers and sells 5,000 shares at $100.02 to buyers, earning 5,000 x $0.02 = $100 in spread, before fees.

Suppose instead it buys 5,000 shares at $100.00 and, before it can sell them, the price drops to $99.90 on news. The inventory is now worth 5,000 x $99.90 = $499,500, a loss of $500. One bad move wiped out the spread from five good round trips. This is why market makers manage inventory tightly and widen quotes when risk rises.

The hedger

A European exporter expects to receive $1,000,000 in three months. At today's EUR/USD rate of 1.1000, that is worth $1,000,000 / 1.1000 = about 909,091 euros. If the dollar weakens to 1.2000, the same dollars would be worth only $1,000,000 / 1.2000 = about 833,333 euros, a loss of about 75,758 euros.

To remove this risk, the exporter agrees a forward contract to sell the dollars at close to today's rate, adjusted for interest rate differences. If the dollar weakens, the hedge pays off; if it strengthens, the exporter gives up the gain. The bank or trader on the other side takes on the currency risk, perhaps because it has an offsetting exposure or expects to be compensated for bearing it.

Neither the market maker nor the hedger is trying to predict the market in the way a speculator is. Recognizing that much trading is driven by goals other than forecasting helps explain why prices are not always "right" and why some patterns persist.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming "smart money" always wins. Institutions make mistakes too, and many underperform their benchmarks.
  • Assuming every large order reveals a view. Large trades are often rebalancing, hedging or index tracking, not a forecast.
  • Trading against faster participants on speed. Retail traders cannot win a race against firms located next to the exchange.
  • Blaming market makers for every loss. Most losses come from costs, leverage and lack of edge, not manipulation.

FAQ

Who is on the other side of my trade?

It depends on the market. It may be a market maker, another trader on the exchange, an institution, a hedger or, for many retail forex and CFD accounts, the broker itself.

What does a market maker do?

A market maker continuously offers to buy at the bid and sell at the ask, providing liquidity to other traders. It aims to earn the spread while managing the risk of holding inventory.

What is the difference between retail and institutional traders?

Retail traders are individuals trading their own money, usually in small sizes. Institutional traders manage large pools of money for funds, pensions, insurers or firms, and have greater resources, data and market access.

What is a whale in crypto?

A whale is an individual or entity holding a large enough amount of a cryptocurrency to move its price when they buy or sell.
