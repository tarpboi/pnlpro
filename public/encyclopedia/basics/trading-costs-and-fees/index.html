Trading Costs and Fees

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Trading costs are everything you pay to open, hold and close positions, including visible fees such as commissions and less visible ones such as spreads, slippage, financing and taxes. Individually they look small, often a fraction of a percent. Multiplied across hundreds or thousands of trades, they are frequently the difference between a profitable strategy and a losing one. High-frequency retail strategies are especially vulnerable, because their expected profit per trade is often smaller than their cost per trade.

Key takeaways

  • Costs fall into three groups: costs to trade (commissions, spreads, exchange fees, slippage), costs to hold (swaps, funding, borrow fees, margin interest) and costs on profits (taxes).
  • "Zero commission" does not mean free; brokers earn through spreads, payment for order flow, financing and other charges.
  • Maker fees, for orders that add liquidity, are usually lower than taker fees, for orders that remove it.
  • Holding leveraged positions overnight adds financing costs that grow with time.
  • The more often you trade, the larger the edge you need per trade just to break even.

Costs to trade

Commissions

A commission is a fee charged by the broker per trade, per share, per contract or as a percentage of the trade value. Many stock brokers in the US and elsewhere now advertise zero commissions on shares and ETFs. Futures and options are usually charged per contract, and some forex accounts charge a fixed commission per lot in exchange for tighter spreads.

The spread

Buying at the ask and selling at the bid costs the bid-ask spread on every round trip. For spread-only accounts, such as many retail forex and CFD accounts, the spread is where the broker earns most of its revenue, and it is often widened beyond the underlying market spread.

Maker and taker fees

Many exchanges, especially in crypto and on some prediction markets, charge different fees depending on whether your order adds or removes liquidity.

  • A maker order rests in the order book and waits to be filled. Maker fees are lower, sometimes zero, and some venues pay a rebate.
  • A taker order fills immediately against resting orders. Taker fees are higher.

Fee schedules usually fall as monthly volume rises, which favors large professional traders.

Exchange and regulatory fees

Exchanges, clearing houses and regulators charge small fees that brokers often pass on. In the US, for example, stock sales carry a small transaction fee that funds the Securities and Exchange Commission, plus a FINRA trading activity fee; the rates are adjusted periodically. Futures carry exchange and clearing fees per contract. Market data subscriptions are another recurring cost for active traders.

Payment for order flow

Some brokers route customer orders to wholesale market makers, who pay the broker for the right to fill them. This is called payment for order flow (PFOF). Supporters argue it funds zero-commission trading and often produces prices slightly better than the public quote. Critics argue it creates a conflict of interest and may leave customers with worse prices than they could get elsewhere. PFOF is legal in the US with disclosure requirements, banned in the UK, and the EU has adopted rules to phase it out; check the current status in your country.

Gas fees

On blockchains, every transaction pays a network fee, known as gas. On some networks, fees are fractions of a cent; on others, they can reach several dollars or more when the network is busy. Traders on decentralized exchanges may also pay priority fees or tips to get transactions included quickly, plus the pool's swap fee.

Slippage

Slippage is the difference between the expected price and the fill price. It is a real cost even though no one sends you a bill for it. It grows with order size, volatility and thin liquidity.

Costs to hold

Overnight financing and swaps

Leveraged positions in CFDs, spread bets and retail forex are charged (or occasionally paid) a daily financing amount, often called a swap or rollover. It is usually based on a benchmark interest rate plus a broker markup. Many forex brokers charge triple swaps on one weekday to cover the weekend.

Example: a $10,000 CFD position held for 30 days at an annual financing rate of 7% costs $10,000 x 7% x 30 / 365 = about $57.53.

Funding rates

Crypto perpetual futures use periodic funding payments between longs and shorts to keep the contract price near the spot price. Intervals are often every 8 hours, though some venues use 1 hour. At a rate of 0.01% per 8 hours, a $10,000 position pays $1 per interval, or $3 a day and about $90 over 30 days. Rates can be much higher in strong trends.

Borrow fees and margin interest

Short sellers pay a fee to borrow shares, from well under 1% a year for widely available stocks to very high rates for hard-to-borrow names. They also owe any dividends paid while short. Traders who buy on margin pay interest on the borrowed amount. See long and short positions.

Inactivity, withdrawal and conversion fees

Some brokers charge for dormant accounts, withdrawals or converting currencies. A currency conversion fee on every trade in a foreign market can add up quietly.

Costs on profits: tax drag

Taxes reduce what you keep. In many countries, short-term trading gains are taxed at higher rates than long-term investment gains, and frequent trading can turn what would have been lightly taxed long-term gains into heavily taxed short-term ones. Rules vary widely by country and change over time. See trading taxes overview.

Worked example: how costs kill a high-frequency retail strategy

A trader with a $10,000 account scalps a crypto perpetual, trading $10,000 of notional value per trade (1x effective leverage). Assume these illustrative costs, which are in the range seen at major exchanges:

  • Taker fee: 0.05% each side, so 0.10% per round trip = $10
  • Spread and slippage: 0.02% per round trip = $2
  • Total cost per round trip: $12

The trader makes 20 round trips a day, every day, since crypto trades 24/7.

  • Daily cost: 20 x $12 = $240
  • Monthly cost (30 days): $240 x 30 = $7,200
  • As a share of the account: $7,200 / $10,000 = 72% per month

To break even, the strategy must make a gross profit of more than 0.12% per trade on average, after accounting for the losing trades. That is a high bar in a market where professional firms with lower fees and faster connections compete for the same small moves. Using limit orders that earn maker fees and trading less often both help, but the lesson stands: at high frequency, costs are often larger than the edge.

Compare a swing trader who makes 4 round trips a month at the same $12 cost: $48 a month, or 0.48% of the account. The same cost structure is a minor drag for one style and fatal for another. See trading styles.

Common mistakes

  • Judging a broker by its commission alone. Spreads, financing and execution quality often matter more.
  • Ignoring costs in backtests. A strategy that looks profitable without costs may lose money with them. See backtesting.
  • Holding leveraged positions for months. Financing charges compound into a large drag.
  • Overtrading after losses. Each extra trade adds cost without adding edge.
  • Forgetting about taxes until year end. Short-term gains can be taxed heavily, and records are needed for every trade.

FAQ

Is commission-free trading really free?

No. Brokers that charge no commission still earn money through the spread, payment for order flow, interest on cash, margin lending, currency conversion and other fees. You also pay market costs such as slippage.

What are maker and taker fees?

A maker fee applies to an order that rests in the order book and adds liquidity. A taker fee applies to an order that fills immediately against existing orders. Maker fees are usually lower.

What is an overnight swap fee?

It is a daily financing charge, or occasionally a credit, applied to leveraged forex, CFD and spread bet positions held past the daily rollover time. It is based on interest rates plus a broker markup.

How much do trading costs affect profits?

It depends on trade frequency. A cost of 0.1% per round trip is minor for someone trading a few times a month but can consume most or all of the gross profit for someone trading dozens of times a day.
