Financial Markets Overview

A financial market is any place, physical or electronic, where buyers and sellers trade assets or contracts at prices they agree on. Traders today can reach a wide range of markets from one screen, from company shares and currencies to futures, options, cryptocurrencies and contracts on real-world events. Each market has its own trading hours, typical leverage, costs and participants, and those differences matter more to a trader's results than most people expect.

Key takeaways

  • The main asset classes are stocks, bonds, currencies, commodities and crypto, and most other products (ETFs, futures, options, CFDs) are wrappers or derivatives built on them.
  • Derivatives such as futures, options, CFDs and perpetuals let traders take large positions with little capital, which raises both potential gains and the risk of rapid loss.
  • Trading hours range from a few hours a day for many stock exchanges to nonstop for crypto.
  • Markets are linked through interest rates, the US dollar and risk appetite, so a shock in one often spreads to others.
  • Choosing a market is a trade-off between liquidity, costs, leverage, hours and the kind of analysis you want to do.

Underlying assets and derivatives

  • Underlying assets are the things themselves: a share, a bond, a currency, a barrel of oil, a bitcoin.
  • Derivatives are contracts whose value comes from an underlying asset: futures, options, contracts for difference (CFDs) and crypto perpetual futures. They usually allow leverage and short selling, and many are zero-sum between the two parties.

Some products sit in between. An exchange-traded fund (ETF) holds a basket of assets but trades like a single share. A stock index is just a number; traders reach it through futures, ETFs, options or CFDs.

The asset classes

Stocks

A share is a small piece of ownership in a company, traded on exchanges such as the New York Stock Exchange, Nasdaq, the London Stock Exchange, the Tokyo Stock Exchange and Hong Kong Exchanges. Retail leverage on shares is modest at regulated brokers. See stock market basics.

ETFs

An ETF holds a portfolio, such as an index, gold bullion or bonds, and its shares trade on an exchange. Leveraged and inverse ETFs exist but drift away from their target multiple over periods longer than a day. See ETFs and index trading.

Forex

The foreign exchange market trades currencies in pairs, such as EUR/USD. The Bank for International Settlements' 2022 Triennial Survey put average daily turnover at about $7.5 trillion, making it the largest market. It trades over the counter through banks and dealers, 24 hours a day on weekdays. See forex basics.

Futures

A futures contract is a standardized agreement to buy or sell a set quantity at a set price on a future date, traded on exchanges such as CME Group, ICE and Eurex. Contracts cover indices, interest rates, energy, metals, grains and crypto, and margin is a fraction of the contract's value. See futures basics.

Options

An option gives the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy (a call) or sell (a put) at a set price by a set date. A buyer's loss is limited to the price paid; some selling strategies carry large or unlimited risk. See options basics.

CFDs and spread betting

A CFD is an agreement with a broker to exchange the change in an asset's price without owning it. CFDs are common in Europe, the UK and Australia but not available to US retail traders. The broker is usually the counterparty. See CFDs and spread betting.

Crypto

Cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin and Ether trade around the clock on centralized exchanges and decentralized protocols, in spot markets and through perpetual futures. They are far more volatile than most traditional assets. See crypto trading basics.

Meme coins

Meme coins are tokens driven by internet culture and attention rather than any cash flow. Thousands launch every day on some blockchains, and most lose nearly all their value. See meme coins explained.

Commodities

Energy, metals and agricultural products, reached through futures, ETFs, CFDs and producer shares. Prices respond to supply, demand, weather, inventories and geopolitics. See commodities trading.

Bonds

A bond is an interest-paying loan to a government or company. Prices move inversely to interest rates, and most retail traders use ETFs or rate futures. See bonds and interest rate trading.

Indices

Indices such as the S&P 500, Nasdaq 100, DAX, FTSE 100 and Nikkei 225 track groups of shares and are heavily traded through futures, options, ETFs and CFDs. See stock index trading.

Prediction markets

Prediction markets list contracts that pay a fixed amount, often $1, if an event happens. The price, between $0 and $1, can be read as the market's probability estimate. Access rules differ by country. See prediction markets explained.

Worked example: one view, three instruments

Suppose a trader expects a stock index at 6,000 to rise 1%. Here is the same view through three instruments, ignoring costs. The futures margin is an assumed figure for illustration; real margins vary by contract, broker and volatility.

Instrument Capital committed Exposure Result of a 1% move As a share of capital
Index ETF, no leverage $3,000 $3,000 $30 1%
CFD at 20:1 $150 margin $3,000 $30 20%
One micro index future at $5 per point $2,000 margin (assumed) 6,000 x $5 = $30,000 60 points x $5 = $300 15%

The index move is identical in each case. What changes is how much capital is at risk and how fast a move the wrong way erodes it. A 1% fall costs the ETF holder 1% of capital, the CFD trader 20% of margin and the futures trader 15%. Options add another layer, because their value also depends on time and volatility.

How markets connect

A few forces link almost all markets:

  • Interest rates. When a central bank raises rates, bond prices tend to fall, the currency often strengthens, and stocks and crypto can come under pressure. See central banks and interest rates.
  • The US dollar. Many commodities are priced in dollars, and much global borrowing is in dollars, so its moves ripple widely.
  • Risk appetite. In calm periods, money tends to flow into stocks, high-yield currencies and crypto. In fearful periods it tends to flow to government bonds, the dollar, the Japanese yen, the Swiss franc and gold. Correlations between assets can jump sharply during crises.
  • Arbitrage links. Futures prices are tied to their underlying asset, ETF prices to the value of their holdings, and options prices to the underlying and its volatility. Professional traders enforce these links by trading away gaps.

For more, see intermarket analysis.

Common mistakes

  • Trading several markets at once as a beginner. Each market has its own quirks; learning one well tends to work better than dabbling in many.
  • Choosing a market for its leverage. High available leverage is a risk, not a feature.
  • Ignoring the counterparty. With CFDs and many forex accounts, the broker may be on the other side of your trade. See brokers and exchanges.
  • Assuming 24-hour markets are always liquid. Liquidity thins out at night, on weekends and on holidays, and spreads widen.

FAQ

What is the biggest financial market in the world?

By daily trading volume, the foreign exchange market is the largest. The Bank for International Settlements' Triennial Survey estimated average turnover of about $7.5 trillion a day in April 2022 and roughly $9.6 trillion a day in April 2025.

What is the difference between a stock and a derivative?

A stock is ownership of part of a company. A derivative, such as a future, option or CFD, is a contract whose value depends on the price of something else, and it usually allows leverage and short selling.

Which market is best for beginners?

No market is best for everyone. Many beginners start with unleveraged stocks or ETFs because the products are simple, regulation is strong and a mistake is less likely to wipe out the account quickly.

Can you trade markets 24 hours a day?

Crypto trades nonstop, forex trades 24 hours on weekdays, and many futures trade nearly around the clock on weekdays. Most stock exchanges are open only during set daytime hours, with limited extended sessions.
