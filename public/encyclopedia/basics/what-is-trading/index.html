What Is Trading?

9 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Trading is buying and selling financial assets with the aim of profiting from changes in their price, usually over periods from seconds to a few months. Unlike long-term investing, a trader's return depends mostly on timing and on beating other market participants, not on the slow growth of a business or an economy. That makes trading a competitive activity with real costs, and the evidence shows that most retail traders who try it lose money. Understanding why is the first step to doing it sensibly, or deciding not to.

Key takeaways

  • Trading seeks profit from price changes over short to medium horizons, while investing seeks long-run returns from owning productive assets.
  • Many trading markets are close to zero-sum before costs, which makes them negative-sum for traders as a group once fees, spreads and taxes are paid.
  • A trader needs an edge, a repeatable reason to expect a positive result after costs, and most people who start trading do not have one.
  • Large studies of retail day traders and regulator-mandated broker disclosures consistently show that a majority lose money.
  • Survival depends more on risk control, cost awareness and discipline than on finding perfect entries.

What trading actually is

At its simplest, a trade is an exchange: you give cash for an asset, or an asset for cash, at a price agreed with someone else. A trader opens a position (owns or owes something whose value changes with the market) and later closes it. The profit or loss is the difference between the entry and exit prices, minus costs.

You can profit from rising prices by buying first and selling later, which is called going long. You can also profit from falling prices by selling first and buying back later, which is called going short. Both are explained in long and short positions.

Trading happens in many markets: company shares, currencies, futures, options, cryptocurrencies, commodities, bonds and event contracts on prediction markets. The financial markets overview describes each one. The mechanics differ, but the core logic is the same everywhere: buy below where you sell, and make sure the gap is large enough to cover your costs.

Trading vs investing vs gambling

People often blur these three activities. They overlap, but they differ in time horizon, in where returns come from, and in the expected result for an average participant.

Investing Trading Gambling
Typical horizon Years to decades Seconds to months Minutes to days
Main source of return Business profits, interest, economic growth Price changes, beating other traders Outcome of a game or event
Is the pie growing? Yes, historically, over long periods Barely, at short horizons No; the house takes a cut
Expected result for an average participant Positive over long periods, historically Negative after costs Negative
Role of skill Moderate (asset choice, staying invested) High, and rare Low to moderate, depending on the game

Why investing tends to have a positive expectation

When you own a broad basket of shares for many years, you collect a share of company earnings and dividends. Historically, broad stock markets in many countries have delivered positive real returns over long periods, though with deep falls along the way and no guarantee for the future. The average investor can earn the market's return simply by holding it at low cost.

Why trading is different

Over a day or a week, the expected drift in most asset prices is tiny compared with the size of the random moves. A short-term trader is therefore not collecting much growth. Their result depends almost entirely on whether they buy and sell at better prices than the people on the other side of their trades. That is a contest, and every contest has losers.

Where trading meets gambling

Trading becomes indistinguishable from gambling when there is no edge, the position size is driven by excitement rather than a plan, and the trader cannot explain why the expected value is positive. Very short-dated options, highly leveraged crypto positions and newly launched meme coins are areas where this line is easy to cross. If trading starts to feel compulsive, or you find yourself chasing losses, help for problem gambling is available in most countries, including free and confidential helplines.

Zero-sum and negative-sum markets

In a zero-sum market, one side's gain is exactly the other side's loss. Futures and options are the clearest cases. If one trader buys a futures contract from another at 100 and the price later settles at 110, the buyer gains 10 points and the seller loses 10 points. Nothing was created; money moved from one account to another.

Stocks are not strictly zero-sum over the long run, because companies generate profits. Over short horizons, though, the gains from those profits are so small that short-term stock trading behaves almost like a zero-sum game.

Now add costs. Every trade pays something: a commission, the bid-ask spread, exchange fees, financing charges or network gas fees. Those payments leave the pool of traders and go to brokers, exchanges and market makers. A zero-sum game with fees is a negative-sum game for the players as a group. For traders in aggregate to break even, someone else would have to be losing money to them, and at short horizons there is little else to take it from.

Worked example: how costs add up

Suppose a day trader with a $20,000 account makes 10 round trips a day (a round trip is one buy and one sell). Between commissions, the spread and a little slippage, each round trip costs an average of $4.

  • Daily cost: 10 x $4 = $40
  • Trading days per year: about 250
  • Annual cost: $40 x 250 = $10,000
  • Cost as a share of the account: $10,000 / $20,000 = 50%

Before this trader earns anything, they must make 50% a year in gross trading profit just to break even. Very few people can do that. The trading costs and fees article breaks down each cost in detail.

How traders make money

Profitable traders have an edge: a reason, grounded in how markets work, why their trades should be worth more than they cost on average. Common sources include:

  • Information or analysis. Understanding something, such as how a central bank decision affects a currency or how a prediction market will resolve, better or sooner than the price reflects.
  • Providing liquidity. Market makers earn the spread by standing ready to buy and sell, in exchange for taking on inventory risk. See market participants.
  • Bearing risk others want to shed. Some traders are paid, on average, for holding positions that hedgers want to offload, or for supplying insurance-like positions. These strategies can lose heavily in rare events.
  • Behavioral patterns. Some price patterns, such as momentum over certain horizons, have been documented in academic research and are often attributed partly to how people react to news. They tend to be weak, crowded and inconsistent.
  • Speed and technology. Professional firms invest heavily in fast execution. Retail traders cannot compete on this.

An edge is never certain. Even a genuinely profitable approach has long losing streaks, and edges tend to shrink as more people find them.

How traders lose money

Most losses come from a short list of causes:

  • No edge. Trades are based on hunches, tips or patterns with no tested advantage, so the result tends toward the cost of trading.
  • Costs. Frequent trading multiplies small costs into large ones, as the example above shows.
  • Adverse selection. When you trade with someone better informed, you tend to get filled on exactly the trades that go against you.
  • Leverage. Borrowed money magnifies losses as much as gains, and can wipe out an account in a single move. See leverage and margin.
  • Behavior. Cutting winners early, holding losers too long, revenge trading after a loss and oversizing after a win. The psychology section covers these.
  • Poor risk control. Risking too much per trade means a normal losing streak becomes a fatal one.

What the evidence says about retail traders

Several bodies of evidence point the same way.

  • Taiwan. Research by Brad Barber, Yi-Tsung Lee, Yu-Jane Liu and Terrance Odean, using complete trading records from the Taiwan Stock Exchange, found that the great majority of day traders lost money, and that fewer than 1% were able to earn predictable profits net of fees year after year.
  • Brazil. Fernando Chague, Rodrigo De-Losso and Bruno Giovannetti studied individuals who day traded Brazilian mini-index futures for at least 300 days. About 97% lost money, and only around 1% earned more than the Brazilian minimum wage from the activity.
  • CFD broker disclosures. Under rules introduced by the European Securities and Markets Authority and adopted by national regulators, brokers offering contracts for difference to retail clients in the EU must show the percentage of retail accounts that lose money. The figures brokers publish are typically between 70% and 80% or higher, and the UK has a similar requirement.

None of this proves that profitable trading is impossible. It shows that the default outcome is a loss, that the losses are larger for frequent and leveraged trading, and that the few consistent winners are hard to tell apart from lucky ones in the short run.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a winning streak as proof of skill. A few good weeks are well within the range of luck.
  • Starting with leverage. Leverage shortens the time it takes to make every other mistake.
  • Ignoring costs. Many strategies that look profitable on a chart lose money once spreads and fees are included.
  • Trading too large. Risking 10% or more of an account per trade makes ruin likely even with a decent strategy.
  • Learning from marketing. Many people selling courses and signals earn more from their audience than from trading. See courses, gurus and signals.

FAQ

Is trading the same as gambling?

Not necessarily. Trading with a tested edge, controlled position sizes and a written plan is a skill-based activity, but trading without an edge, driven by excitement, has a negative expected value just like gambling.

Can you make a living from trading?

A small minority of people do, but studies of retail day traders in Taiwan and Brazil found that only around 1% earned consistent profits. Most people who try lose money, so trading should not be relied on as a primary income without a long, verified track record.

Why do most traders lose money?

Short-term trading is close to zero-sum before costs and negative-sum after them, so the average trader loses roughly the cost of trading. Leverage, overtrading and emotional decisions push many traders well below that average.

What is the difference between trading and investing?

Investing means holding productive assets for years to share in their long-run growth. Trading means buying and selling over shorter periods to profit from price changes, which depends on outperforming other participants.
