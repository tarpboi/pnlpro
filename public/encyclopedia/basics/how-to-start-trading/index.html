How to Start Trading

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Starting to trade sensibly means learning how markets work, choosing one market and style, practicing without real money, then trading small with strict risk limits while recording and reviewing every trade. The goal of the first year is not to get rich; it is to survive, learn and find out honestly whether you have an edge. Most people who begin trading lose money, so the safest path is slow, cheap and built around protecting your capital.

Key takeaways

  • Trade only with risk capital: money you can afford to lose entirely without affecting your living costs, debts or savings goals.
  • Focus on one market and one style at first, and learn its mechanics and costs in depth.
  • Paper trading builds familiarity with the platform and the process, but it does not reproduce real emotions or fills.
  • When you go live, risk a small, fixed fraction of your account per trade, often 1% or less, and keep a journal of every trade.
  • Increase size only after a meaningful sample of trades shows positive results after costs.

Step 1: Learn the basics

Before placing a trade, understand the vocabulary and mechanics. At a minimum, you should be able to explain:

  • How order types work, especially market, limit and stop orders.
  • What the bid-ask spread is and what a round trip costs.
  • How leverage and margin magnify losses and when liquidation happens.
  • What trading costs and fees apply to the market you plan to trade.
  • How risk management works, including position sizing and stop placement.

The articles in the Trading Basics and Risk and Money Management sections of this encyclopedia cover these in order. High-quality free material exists; be wary of expensive courses that promise income. See courses, gurus and signals.

Step 2: Pick one market and one style

Each market has its own hours, costs, leverage, rules and behavior. Learning one well tends to work better than dabbling in several. Consider:

  • Your schedule. If you work during the main hours of a market, choose a longer style or a market active at times you are free. See market hours and sessions.
  • Your capital. Some markets have practical minimums, such as contract sizes or account rules.
  • Simplicity. Unleveraged stocks or ETFs are easier to understand than options, perpetual futures or meme coins.

Choose a trading style that fits your time and temperament. Many beginners do better with swing trading, which is slower and cheaper, than with day trading or scalping.

Step 3: Write a simple plan

Before trading, write down:

  • What you will trade and when.
  • The setups you will take and the exact entry rules.
  • Where your stop goes and how you will exit winners.
  • How much you will risk per trade and the maximum you can lose in a day or week before stopping.

A plan does not need to be perfect, but it turns vague intentions into rules you can test. See writing a trading plan.

Step 4: Paper trade

Paper trading means simulating trades without real money, using a broker's demo account or a spreadsheet. It is useful for:

  • Learning the platform so you do not make costly clicking errors.
  • Checking whether you can follow your plan's rules consistently.
  • Gathering a first rough sample of how the setups behave.

Its limits are real. Demo fills are often better than live fills, especially for market orders in thin markets. More importantly, there is no fear or greed when nothing is at stake. Treat paper results as a test of process, not proof of profitability. A few weeks to a few months, or a few dozen trades, is usually enough to move on to small live size.

Step 5: Go live with small size

Real money changes behavior. Start with the smallest position size your market allows, and risk a fixed small fraction of your account per trade.

Worked example: budgeting risk capital

A person has $50,000 in savings. They keep six months of living expenses, $18,000, as an emergency fund, and have $26,000 set aside for a home deposit. They decide that $3,000 is money they could lose entirely without changing their life. That is their trading account.

  • Risk per trade at 1%: $3,000 x 1% = $30.
  • For a stock trade with a stop 2% below the entry price, position size = $30 / 0.02 = $1,500.
  • If the stock is $50, that is $1,500 / $50 = 30 shares.
  • If the stop is hit, the loss is 30 x ($50 x 0.02) = 30 x $1 = $30, plus costs.

At 1% risk per trade, ten losses in a row cost about $300, or 10% of the account. That is painful but survivable, and losing streaks of that length happen to sound strategies. At 10% risk per trade, the same streak would leave about $1,046 of the original $3,000. Position sizing methods are covered in position sizing.

The trader also sets a rule: if the account falls below $2,400 (a 20% drawdown), they stop live trading and go back to review and paper trading.

Step 6: Keep a journal

Record every trade: date, market, setup, entry, stop, target, exit, size, result after costs, and a short note on why you took it and how you felt. Screenshots of the chart at entry and exit are useful. Without a journal, it is almost impossible to tell skill from luck or to find your recurring mistakes. See trading journal.

Step 7: Review regularly

Once a week, review your journal. Useful questions:

  • Did I follow my rules on every trade? If not, which rules did I break and why?
  • Which setups made money after costs, and which lost?
  • What is my average win, average loss and win rate? What is my expectancy per trade? See risk/reward and expectancy.
  • Am I trading more after losses or wins?

Change one thing at a time, and give each change enough trades to judge it.

Step 8: Scale slowly

Increase size only when the evidence supports it. A reasonable standard is a sample of at least 50 to 100 live trades following the same rules, with positive results after costs, and drawdowns within your limits. Even then, increase in small steps, such as from 0.5% to 0.75% risk per trade, rather than doubling. Results often worsen at larger size because emotions intensify and liquidity becomes a constraint.

Realistic expectations

  • Most people who try trading lose money. Large studies of retail day traders in Taiwan and Brazil found that only about 1% earned consistent profits.
  • Profitable traders still have long losing periods.
  • A year or more of learning before you know whether you have an edge is normal.
  • If trading starts to feel like a compulsion, or you hide losses or chase them with bigger bets, those are warning signs of problem gambling. Help is available in most countries, including free confidential helplines. See handling losses and tilt.

Avoiding scams

Beginners are the main target of trading fraud. Common warning signs:

  • Strangers on social media or messaging apps offering to teach you or trade for you.
  • Guaranteed or unusually high, steady returns.
  • Platforms that let you deposit easily but demand fees or "taxes" before withdrawals.
  • Signal groups or "AI bots" that ask for deposits into their own platform.
  • Anyone offering to recover lost money for an upfront fee.

Use firms regulated in a well-established jurisdiction and verify their license on the regulator's own register. See brokers and exchanges and trading scams.

Common mistakes

  • Starting with leverage or complex products. Options, perpetuals and meme coins are poor first markets.
  • Depositing money you need. Trading with rent or borrowed money creates pressure that leads to bad decisions.
  • Skipping the journal. Without records, you cannot learn from your trades.
  • Increasing size after a lucky streak. A few wins are not evidence of an edge.
  • Changing strategies constantly. Every strategy has losing runs; switching after each one prevents learning.
  • Forgetting taxes. Keep records of every trade for tax purposes. See trading taxes overview.

FAQ

How much money do I need to start trading?

Technically, many brokers allow very small accounts. What matters more is that the money is risk capital you can afford to lose completely, and that the account is large enough for sensible position sizes after costs in your chosen market.

Should beginners use a demo account?

A demo account is useful for learning the platform and testing whether you can follow your rules. It does not reproduce real emotions or fills, so move to small live size once the process feels routine.

How long does it take to learn trading?

Most traders need at least many months, and often a year or more, of study, practice and live trading to know whether they have a repeatable edge. Many never find one.

What percentage of my account should I risk per trade?

Many traders risk 1% or less of their account on each trade. This keeps a normal losing streak from causing serious damage while you learn.
