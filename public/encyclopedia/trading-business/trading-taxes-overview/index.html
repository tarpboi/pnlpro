Trading Taxes Overview

8 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Prop Firms, Tax, Regulation & Scams

Trading profits are taxable in most countries, but how they are taxed depends on where you live, what you trade, how long you hold it and whether the tax authority sees you as an investor or as someone running a trading business. The same $10,000 gain can be taxed at very different rates depending on these factors. This article explains the main concepts in general terms. It is not tax advice, rules change often, and readers should check the current rules in their own country or ask a qualified tax professional.

Key takeaways

  • Most countries tax trading gains either as capital gains or as ordinary income, and the difference can be large.
  • Many tax systems reward holding periods, such as the US split between short-term and long-term gains at one year.
  • Some rules are specific to one country, such as the US wash sale rule and Section 1256 treatment for futures.
  • Many countries treat cryptocurrency as property or an asset, so swapping one coin for another can be a taxable event.
  • Good records of every trade, fee and currency conversion are essential everywhere.

Capital gains vs income

Tax systems generally sort trading profits into two buckets:

  • Capital gains: profit from selling an asset for more than it cost. Many countries tax these at lower rates, offer an annual allowance, or give a discount for long holding periods. Capital losses can usually offset capital gains, but often have limits on offsetting other income.
  • Ordinary or business income: profits taxed like wages or business profits, usually at higher marginal rates, though losses may offset other income more freely.

Which bucket applies depends on the country and on the facts. Tax authorities typically look at how often you trade, how long you hold positions and whether trading is your main activity, so frequent day trading may be treated as a business in some countries. There are rarely bright-line tests.

Holding periods

Many systems tax short holding periods more heavily. In the United States, as of 2026:

  • Assets held one year or less produce short-term gains, taxed at ordinary income rates.
  • Assets held more than one year produce long-term gains, taxed at lower rates of 0, 15 or 20 percent depending on income, plus a possible 3.8 percent net investment income tax for higher earners.

Other examples, described generally and subject to change:

  • Australia: individuals who hold an asset for at least 12 months can generally reduce the taxable capital gain by 50 percent.
  • Germany: gains on cryptocurrency held privately for more than one year are generally tax-free for individuals, while most securities gains are taxed at a flat rate regardless of holding period.

Short-term traders usually get little or no benefit from holding-period rules.

US rules traders often meet

These rules apply in the United States. Other countries have different rules, some with similar aims.

Capital loss limits

In the US, capital losses first offset capital gains. If losses exceed gains, up to $3,000 of net capital loss a year ($1,500 for married people filing separately) can offset ordinary income, and the rest carries forward to future years.

Example: a trader has a net capital loss of $12,000 for the year. They can deduct $3,000 against other income and carry $9,000 forward.

The wash sale rule

The wash sale rule stops traders from claiming a loss on a sale if they buy the same or a "substantially identical" security within 30 days before or after the sale. The loss is not lost permanently; it is added to the cost basis of the replacement shares.

Worked example:

  • Buy 100 shares at $50: cost $5,000.
  • Sell all 100 at $40: proceeds $4,000, a loss of $1,000.
  • Two weeks later, buy 100 shares again at $42: cost $4,200.
  • Because the repurchase came within 30 days, the $1,000 loss is disallowed for now. It is added to the new shares' basis: $4,200 + $1,000 = $5,200, or $52 per share.
  • If the trader later sells at $55, the taxable gain is $5,500 − $5,200 = $300, not $1,300.

Active traders who repeatedly trade the same stock can end up with many deferred losses, and broker statements do not always capture every case, such as purchases in a different account. The rule has historically been applied to stocks, securities and options. As of the most recent rules this guide reflects, it has not been extended by statute to cryptocurrency, which the IRS treats as property, though proposals to change this have been made. Check the current position.

Section 1256 contracts

Regulated futures contracts, broad-based index options and certain currency contracts fall under Section 1256 of the US tax code. Two features stand out:

  • 60/40 treatment: 60 percent of the gain or loss is treated as long-term and 40 percent as short-term, regardless of how long the position was held.
  • Marked to market: open positions are treated as if sold at fair value on the last business day of the year.

Worked example: a futures trader makes a net $10,000 gain. Assume, for illustration only, a 32 percent rate on short-term gains and 15 percent on long-term gains.

  • Long-term portion: $6,000 × 15% = $900.
  • Short-term portion: $4,000 × 32% = $1,280.
  • Total: $2,180, an effective rate of 21.8 percent.
  • The same gain taxed entirely as short-term: $10,000 × 32% = $3,200.

This is one reason US day traders sometimes prefer futures. See futures vs other instruments.

By contrast, US gains and losses on spot and rolling retail forex are generally ordinary income or loss under Section 988 by default, with elections available for some contracts.

Trader tax status and the mark-to-market election

The IRS recognizes some individuals as traders in securities, which is a business, rather than investors. There is no fixed test; trading generally has to be substantial, frequent, regular and continuous. Traders who qualify can make a Section 475(f) mark-to-market election, under which:

  • gains and losses are ordinary rather than capital;
  • the wash sale rule does not apply to covered securities;
  • the $3,000 capital loss limit does not apply to those losses.

The election must generally be made by the due date of the prior year's tax return, and it is hard to reverse. Gains also lose any long-term capital gains benefit.

UK examples

These figures applied in the 2025–26 tax year. Check whether they have changed for later years.

  • Individuals have a capital gains tax annual exempt amount of £3,000.
  • Capital gains on most assets are taxed at 18 percent within the basic rate band and 24 percent above it.
  • Share sales are matched first with purchases on the same day, then with purchases in the following 30 days (often called the bed and breakfast rule), then with a pooled average cost.
  • Gains inside an ISA are not taxed.
  • Spread betting profits are generally free of capital gains tax for individuals, while CFD gains are generally taxable. See CFDs and spread betting.
  • HMRC treats cryptoassets held by most individuals as assets subject to capital gains tax.

Cryptocurrency

Many countries, including the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada and Australia, treat cryptocurrency as property or a capital asset rather than as currency. Common consequences:

  • Selling crypto for fiat is a taxable disposal.
  • Swapping one coin for another is usually a disposal of the first coin.
  • Spending crypto on goods can be a disposal.
  • Rewards from staking, mining, airdrops or interest may be taxed as income when received, with a later gain or loss on sale.
  • Moving coins between your own wallets is usually not a disposal, but you need records to prove it.

Reporting is tightening. US brokers began issuing Form 1099-DA for digital asset sales from the 2025 tax year, and the EU's DAC8 rules and the OECD's Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework extend exchange reporting in many countries from 2026. A few jurisdictions, such as Singapore, generally do not tax individuals' capital gains, though trading as a business can be taxed.

Currency conversion

Tax is usually calculated in your home currency. That means exchange rate moves can create or remove gains.

Example: a UK trader buys US shares for $10,000 when GBP/USD is 1.2500, a cost of £8,000. Later they sell for $11,000 when GBP/USD is 1.3750, proceeds of £8,000. The trade made $1,000 in dollars, but the gain in pounds is zero.

Record keeping

Whatever the country, keep records of:

  • date, asset, quantity and price of every buy and sell;
  • commissions, fees and financing charges;
  • exchange rates used for foreign currency trades;
  • transfers between accounts and wallets;
  • income such as dividends, interest, swaps, staking rewards and airdrops.

Broker and exchange statements are a start, but they can be incomplete, especially for crypto spread across several platforms and wallets. A trading journal that records costs also helps. Keep records for as long as your tax authority can review past returns, which is several years in most countries.

Taxes are also a real trading cost. A strategy that is barely profitable before tax can be unprofitable after it, as noted in trading costs and fees.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming crypto-to-crypto swaps are tax-free. In many countries they are taxable disposals.
  • Ignoring wash sales across accounts. In the US, a repurchase in an IRA or another account can trigger the rule.
  • Not converting to home currency. Gains in dollars may be losses in your currency, or the reverse.
  • Waiting until tax season to gather records. Missing exchange histories can be hard or impossible to recover.

FAQ

Do I have to pay tax on trading profits?

In most countries, yes. Profits are usually taxed either as capital gains or as income, depending on local law and how you trade. Check your country's current rules or ask a tax professional.

What is the wash sale rule?

It is a US rule that disallows a loss on selling a security if you buy the same or a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the sale. The disallowed loss is added to the cost basis of the new shares.

How are futures taxed in the US?

Most regulated futures are Section 1256 contracts: gains and losses are split 60 percent long-term and 40 percent short-term regardless of holding period, and open positions are marked to market at year end.

Is crypto taxed like stocks?

In many countries, including the US and UK, crypto is treated as property or an asset and taxed under capital gains rules similar to stocks, though swaps between coins and rewards from staking can create extra taxable events. Rules vary by country and change often.
