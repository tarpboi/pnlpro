Regulators and Investor Protection

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Prop Firms, Tax, Regulation & Scams

Financial regulators are the government agencies and self-regulatory organizations that license brokers and exchanges, write conduct rules, police market abuse and, in many countries, oversee schemes that compensate clients when a firm fails. For a trader, regulation is mostly about one practical question: what happens to my money if the firm I use misbehaves or goes bust? Knowing who regulates what, what protection actually covers, and how to verify a firm is one of the simplest ways to avoid serious losses.

Key takeaways

  • Each country has its own regulators, often split by product, such as securities versus derivatives in the United States.
  • Regulation typically requires client money segregation, capital requirements, fair dealing and complaint handling, but it never protects you from trading losses.
  • Compensation schemes cover some losses when a regulated firm fails and client assets are missing, up to limits that vary by country.
  • Always verify a firm on the regulator's own register, using contact details from that register.
  • Crypto regulation is changing fast; the EU's MiCA regulation became fully applicable at the end of 2024.

What regulation does and doesn't protect

Usually protected or required Not protected
Client money held separately from the firm's own funds Losses from your own trades
Minimum capital and liquidity for the firm Bad advice from unregulated sources
Fair treatment, disclosure and best execution rules Products the firm offers through an unregulated entity
Complaint handling and access to an ombudsman or arbitration Fraud by firms that were never licensed
Compensation, up to a limit, if client assets go missing when a firm fails Losses above compensation limits
Rules against market manipulation and insider dealing Price moves, gaps and slippage

United States

  • SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission): oversees securities markets, including stocks, ETFs and listed stock options, along with exchanges, broker-dealers and investment advisers.
  • FINRA (Financial Industry Regulatory Authority): a self-regulatory organization that supervises broker-dealers and their staff, writes conduct rules and runs BrokerCheck, a public database of firms and registered individuals.
  • CFTC (Commodity Futures Trading Commission): oversees futures, options on futures, swaps and retail forex. It also oversees event contracts traded on designated exchanges. See prediction market regulation.
  • NFA (National Futures Association): the self-regulatory organization for the futures and retail forex industry. Its BASIC database lists registered firms and individuals.
  • State regulators: state securities regulators and others oversee some advisers and firms, and handle many fraud cases.

Banking regulators and the Treasury also play roles. US crypto oversight has been shifting since 2025, including a federal stablecoin law passed that year and ongoing debate over how responsibility should be split between the SEC and the CFTC. Check the current position.

United Kingdom

  • FCA (Financial Conduct Authority): authorizes and supervises investment firms, brokers, CFD and spread betting providers, and crypto firms for anti-money-laundering purposes. It runs the Financial Services Register and publishes warning lists of unauthorized firms.
  • Financial Ombudsman Service: resolves complaints against authorized firms.
  • FSCS (Financial Services Compensation Scheme): compensates eligible clients of failed authorized firms. As of 2026, the limit for most investment claims is £85,000 per person per firm.

European Union

  • ESMA (European Securities and Markets Authority): coordinates national regulators, writes technical standards and can take product intervention measures, as it did for retail CFDs in 2018. See CFDs and spread betting.
  • National regulators: each member state has its own, such as BaFin (Germany), AMF (France), CONSOB (Italy), AFM (Netherlands), CNMV (Spain) and CySEC (Cyprus).
  • MiFID II: the main framework for investment services. A firm licensed in one member state can "passport" its services across the EU.
  • Investor compensation: each member state runs a scheme. The EU minimum is €20,000 per investor, and some countries offer more.

MiCA for crypto

The Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA) is the EU's framework for crypto-asset issuers and service providers. Its rules on stablecoins applied from June 2024 and the rest became fully applicable on December 30, 2024. Crypto service providers need authorization from a national regulator and can then passport across the EU. Transitional periods for firms already operating ended no later than July 2026, depending on the member state. MiCA sets rules on disclosure, custody, conduct and market abuse, but it does not make crypto assets safe investments.

Many other countries have capable regulators. Some offshore jurisdictions offer light licensing or none for forex and CFDs, which is why the forex brokers article treats the regulator as a key part of choosing a broker.

Compensation schemes in general terms

Compensation schemes step in when a regulated firm fails and client assets are missing, for example because of fraud or poor record keeping. They do not cover investment losses.

Worked example: United States (SIPC)

The Securities Investor Protection Corporation (SIPC) protects customers of member broker-dealers up to $500,000 each, of which no more than $250,000 can be cash.

A customer's account at a failed broker should hold $400,000 in securities and $300,000 in cash, $700,000 in total. Suppose, as a worst case, none of it can be recovered from the firm's estate.

  • Securities claim: $400,000, within the overall limit.
  • Cash claim: $300,000, but the overall limit leaves only $100,000 of room.
  • SIPC protection: $500,000. The remaining $200,000 becomes a claim against the failed firm's estate.

In practice, most customer assets are usually recovered from segregated accounts, and SIPC protection covers shortfalls. SIPC does not cover futures, retail forex or most crypto assets, and it does not cover losses from market declines.

Worked example: United Kingdom (FSCS)

An investor has £120,000 in investments at a UK authorized firm that fails, and £40,000 of client assets are missing. The FSCS can cover up to £85,000 of the eligible shortfall per person per firm, so the full £40,000 shortfall would fall within the limit. If £100,000 were missing, compensation would be capped at £85,000.

Limits, eligibility and coverage change over time. Check the current details with the relevant scheme.

How to verify a firm

  1. Get the firm's full legal name and license number. Legitimate firms display these.
  2. Go to the regulator's website yourself. Examples include FINRA BrokerCheck and NFA BASIC in the US, the FCA Register in the UK, national registers in EU member states, and ASIC's professional registers in Australia.
  3. Match the details. Website address, phone number, email domain and address should match the register entry. Clone firms copy real firms' names and numbers but use different contact details.
  4. Check permissions. A firm may be licensed for some activities but not others, or only in some countries.
  5. Check which entity you are signing with. International groups often have entities under different regulators with very different protections.
  6. Search warning lists. Many regulators publish lists of unauthorized firms, and IOSCO, the international body of securities regulators, runs a portal that collects alerts from around the world.

The trading scams and forex risks and scams articles cover the tactics that make verification necessary.

Common mistakes

  • Trusting a logo or "regulated" claim on a website. Verify on the regulator's register.
  • Confusing company registration with a financial license. Many scam firms are legally incorporated somewhere but licensed nowhere.
  • Assuming compensation covers trading losses. It only covers missing assets when a firm fails, up to a limit.
  • Ignoring which entity holds your account. The same brand can offer very different protection in different countries.
  • Assuming crypto platforms have the same protections as brokers. Many do not, even where they are licensed. See centralized vs decentralized exchanges.

FAQ

How do I check if a broker is regulated?

Look up the firm on the official register of the regulator it claims, such as FINRA BrokerCheck or NFA BASIC in the US or the FCA Register in the UK, and confirm that the website and contact details match the register entry.

What does SIPC cover?

SIPC protects customers of failed member broker-dealers up to $500,000, including up to $250,000 in cash, when securities and cash are missing. It does not cover market losses, futures, retail forex or most crypto.

Who regulates forex trading?

It depends on the country. Examples include the CFTC and NFA in the US, the FCA in the UK, national regulators under ESMA rules in the EU, ASIC in Australia and the FSA in Japan.

What is MiCA?

MiCA is the European Union's Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation, which sets licensing, disclosure and conduct rules for crypto-asset issuers and service providers. It became fully applicable on December 30, 2024.
