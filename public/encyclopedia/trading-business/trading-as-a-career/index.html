Trading as a Career

6 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Prop Firms, Tax, Regulation & Scams

  • Trading Taxes Overview
  • Regulators & Investor Protection

A trading career can mean very different things: an individual trading their own savings from home, a trader at a proprietary trading firm, a market maker or salesperson at a bank, a portfolio manager at a fund, or a quantitative researcher writing models. These paths differ in how you get in, whose money is at risk, how you are paid and how likely you are to last. For independent traders in particular, the evidence on long-term success is sobering, and understanding it is part of making a sensible decision.

Key takeaways

  • Institutional trading careers pay salaries and use the firm's capital; independent trading depends entirely on your own capital and results.
  • Large studies of retail day traders, in Taiwan and Brazil, found that only a very small fraction earned consistent profits over time.
  • Living off trading needs far more capital than most people expect, because returns vary from year to year while living costs do not.
  • Relevant skills include probability, risk management, discipline and, increasingly, programming and data analysis.
  • Isolation, stress and income swings are real mental health risks, especially for people trading alone.

Independent retail trading

This is the path most people mean when they say "trading for a living". It requires no permission and has no boss, but also no salary, training, risk manager or team. All losses come out of your pocket. The how to start trading article lays out a sensible, slow path for beginners.

Prop firms

Traditional prop firms back traders with real capital and usually provide training and risk oversight. Online evaluation firms sell challenges that lead to funded accounts, often simulated. The differences, rules and business model are explained in prop firms and funded accounts.

Banks, brokers and funds

Banks employ traders who make markets for clients, alongside salespeople and structurers. Since the 2008 financial crisis, rules such as the Volcker Rule in the United States have limited banks' proprietary trading, so bank traders mainly manage risk from client business. Asset managers and hedge funds employ traders who execute orders and portfolio managers who take investment decisions. Entry usually comes through graduate programs and internships, and some roles require licensing exams, for example FINRA qualification exams in the US.

Quantitative trading

Quant roles design, test and run systematic strategies. They suit people with strong mathematics, statistics and programming skills, and much modern trading, from market making to high-frequency trading, runs on their work. The algorithmic trading intro gives an overview of the field.

What the evidence says about independent traders

Two large studies used complete trading records rather than surveys.

  • Taiwan: Brad Barber, Yi-Tsung Lee, Yu-Jane Liu and Terrance Odean studied years of data covering day traders on the Taiwan Stock Exchange. They found that fewer than 1 percent of day traders were able to earn predictable profits after fees. Many quit after poor results, while others kept trading despite persistent losses.
  • Brazil: Fernando Chague, Rodrigo De-Losso and Bruno Giovannetti studied individuals who day-traded Brazilian mini-index futures. Among those who persisted for more than 300 days, about 97 percent lost money, and only around 1 percent earned more than the Brazilian minimum wage.

Mandatory disclosures from EU and UK CFD providers tell a similar story, with 70 to 80 percent or more of retail accounts typically losing money. None of this means success is impossible. It means the base rate is very low, and anyone planning a career should assume they are average until their own long, verified record proves otherwise.

Capital needed to live off trading

The arithmetic is simple and often ignored.

Annual living costs Return achieved (after costs, before tax) Capital needed
$50,000 10% $500,000
$50,000 20% $250,000
$50,000 50% $100,000

A consistent 20 percent a year after costs would be an excellent long-term result even for professionals. Promises of 5 or 10 percent a month are not realistic.

Worked example: variable returns and fixed withdrawals

A trader starts with $250,000 and withdraws $50,000 at the end of each year to live on. Taxes are ignored to keep it simple.

Year Start Return After return Withdrawal End
1 $250,000 +30% $325,000 $50,000 $275,000
2 $275,000 −10% $247,500 $50,000 $197,500
3 $197,500 +20% $237,000 $50,000 $187,000

The average annual return was (30 − 10 + 20) ÷ 3 = 13.3 percent, which most investors would envy. Yet the account fell by a quarter, from $250,000 to $187,000, because withdrawals continued through the losing year. Each year that follows starts from a smaller base. This is why many full-time independent traders keep another income source, or a large cash buffer, and why drawdown and risk of ruin matters so much when you depend on trading income.

Skills that matter

  • Risk management: sizing, loss limits and survival come first. See risk management fundamentals.
  • Probability and statistics: thinking in expected value, distributions and sample sizes.
  • Market knowledge: how the instruments you trade work, including costs and microstructure.
  • Discipline and self-review: following rules, keeping a trading journal and reviewing honestly.
  • Programming and data analysis: Python and SQL are common; C++ matters for low-latency work.
  • Communication: essential for bank and fund roles, where traders work with clients, colleagues and risk managers.

Income variability

Even successful traders have losing months and sometimes losing years. In institutional roles, a salary cushions this, but bonuses can fall sharply and traders who lose money may be let go. Independent traders have no cushion at all. Practical ways people manage this include:

  • keeping one to two years of living expenses in cash, separate from trading capital;
  • starting part-time while keeping a job;
  • setting a written rule for when to stop, such as a maximum loss of capital or a time limit for reaching a target.

Mental health

Trading alone can be isolating and stressful. Long screen hours, poor sleep, money worries and the emotional swings of wins and losses take a toll. Warning signs include trading to escape stress, hiding losses from family and chasing losses. Building routines, setting hours, exercising and keeping social contact all help. See discipline and routine and handling losses and tilt, which also covers when gambling-like patterns call for outside help.

Common mistakes

  • Quitting a job before having a long, proven record. A few good months are not evidence of skill.
  • Underestimating capital needs. Small accounts cannot support living expenses without dangerous leverage.
  • Ignoring taxes and costs. Both reduce the return available to live on. See trading taxes overview.
  • Paying for shortcuts. Expensive courses and signals rarely create a career. See trading education and gurus.
  • Measuring success by lifestyle images. Social media rarely shows the losses.

FAQ

Can you make a living from trading?

Some people do, but large studies of retail day traders found that only a very small percentage earn consistent profits over time. Living off trading also needs substantial capital and the ability to survive losing years.

How much money do you need to trade for a living?

It depends on living costs and realistic returns. At a strong 20 percent annual return, covering $50,000 a year requires about $250,000 of capital, before allowing for taxes and losing years.

What qualifications do you need to become a trader?

Independent traders need no formal qualifications. Banks, funds and prop firms usually hire graduates in finance, economics, mathematics, engineering or computer science, and some roles require licensing exams.

Is day trading a good career?

For most people who try it, day trading has not been profitable over time. It may suit a small minority with a tested edge, enough capital and strong discipline, and it is safest explored part-time first.
