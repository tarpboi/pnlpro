Courses, Gurus and Signals

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Prop Firms, Tax, Regulation & Scams

Trading education is a large industry of courses, mentorships, signal groups and social media personalities. Some of it is useful. Much of it is marketing, and some is outright fraud. Because the people selling education are often paid by the brokers, exchanges and prop firms their students use, their incentives may have little to do with whether students make money. Learning how to judge claims, and where to find good free material, can save both money and years of wasted effort.

Key takeaways

  • Lifestyle images, screenshots of winning trades and testimonials are not evidence of trading skill.
  • Many educators earn money from course sales, subscriptions and affiliate rebates on students' trading, not from their own trading.
  • A credible track record is long, independently verified, based on real money and shows losing periods.
  • Survivorship bias means you mostly hear from the winners, real or fake.
  • High-quality free education exists from exchanges, regulators, textbooks and university courses.

How the guru business works

A typical trading influencer earns from several sources:

  • Course and mentorship sales, often tiered from cheap introductions to expensive "inner circle" programs.
  • Subscriptions to signal groups or private chat communities.
  • Broker rebates: an introducing broker (IB) or affiliate receives a share of the spread or commission on every trade their referred clients place, or a one-off payment for each funded account.
  • Exchange referral programs: many crypto exchanges share a percentage of referred users' trading fees.
  • Prop firm affiliate commissions on each challenge sold through a discount code.
  • Sponsorships and paid promotions, sometimes undisclosed.

None of these depend on students making money. Several of them reward students trading more often, regardless of the outcome.

Worked example: rebates without results

An educator refers students to a forex broker that pays a rebate of $5 per standard lot traded.

  • 200 active students each trade 10 standard lots a month: 200 × 10 = 2,000 lots.
  • Monthly rebate: 2,000 × $5 = $10,000. Annual: $120,000.
  • If students lose money, the rebate is unchanged. If they trade more because of frequent signals, it goes up.

Rebates are legal in many countries when disclosed. They are a strong reason to ask whether an educator's advice is shaped by how much you trade rather than how well.

Fake screenshots and rented lifestyles

Common ways traders' success is exaggerated or faked:

  • Demo account screenshots presented as real money. Many platforms make demo and live accounts look almost identical.
  • Cherry-picking: sharing winning trades and hiding losers, or showing a single profitable day.
  • Edited images: balances and profits can be altered in a browser or image editor in seconds.
  • Selective accounts: showing one account that did well out of many that did not.
  • Opposite calls: posting bullish and bearish calls in different places and only highlighting the winner.
  • Rented luxury: sports cars, villas and private jet interiors can be rented by the hour or day, and some studios are built for this kind of content.
  • Income from students shown as trading income: a large bank balance may come from course sales.

The forex risks and scams and trading scams articles cover where exaggeration crosses into fraud.

What a verified track record looks like

Weak evidence Stronger evidence
Screenshots of individual trades Complete statements covering every trade
A few weeks or months Several years across different market conditions
Demo or unspecified accounts Live accounts with stated size and deposits and withdrawals
Only percentages shown Percentages, money amounts and account size
No losing periods Drawdowns shown openly
Self-reported Read-only verification by an independent third party, broker statements, or audited results

Professional money managers are generally required to register with regulators and follow rules on how they present performance. In the US, for example, people managing futures or forex accounts for others generally must register with the CFTC and be NFA members. Some asset managers follow the Global Investment Performance Standards (GIPS) for presenting results. An educator who offers to trade for you without the relevant license is a warning sign.

Even a genuine track record may not help you. Strategies that worked in the past can stop working, results can depend on the trader's own execution, and a few lucky years can look like skill. The copy trading and social trading article explains these limits.

Survivorship bias

Thousands of people try to become trading influencers. Those who lose money tend to stop posting, while those who had a lucky run, or who fake it convincingly, stay visible. The result is a feed full of apparent winners. Large studies of retail day traders, such as Barber, Lee, Liu and Odean's work on Taiwan, found that only a very small fraction earned consistent profits after costs. Anyone claiming to turn most students into full-time traders is claiming something the evidence does not support.

Regulators and "finfluencers"

Regulators in several countries have taken a closer interest in social media financial promotion. Authorities in the UK, the US and elsewhere have brought cases against influencers who promoted unauthorized trading schemes, or who promoted stocks or tokens they were secretly selling. Rules on financial promotions often apply to social media posts as well as traditional ads. If an influencer is paid to promote a product, many jurisdictions require them to say so.

How to evaluate a course or mentor

Ask these questions before paying:

  1. What exactly will I learn? A syllabus should cover risk management, testing and costs, not just entry setups.
  2. How does the educator make most of their money? Course sales, rebates, affiliate deals or trading?
  3. Is there a verified, long-term track record? If not, the course is teaching theory, which is fine if priced and described honestly.
  4. Are claims realistic? Phrases like "guaranteed", "financial freedom in months", or "90 percent win rate" are red flags.
  5. Does it push a particular broker, exchange or prop firm? Check whether there is an affiliate link.
  6. Can I see the material before paying, or get a refund?
  7. Does the price make sense relative to my capital?

Worked example: price relative to capital

A trader with a $5,000 account considers a $2,000 course.

  • The course costs $2,000 ÷ $5,000 = 40 percent of the account.
  • If the fee comes out of the account, only $3,000 is left to trade.
  • To earn back the $2,000 in one year, the trader would need a return of $2,000 ÷ $3,000 = 66.7 percent on what remains.

Few professionals earn returns like that consistently. For small accounts, free resources and paper trading are usually a better first investment.

Free and low-cost learning paths

High-quality material is widely available:

  • Exchange education: major exchanges such as CME Group and Cboe publish free courses on futures, options and market structure.
  • Regulator investor education: for example, the SEC's Investor.gov in the US, the FCA's consumer pages in the UK and ASIC's Moneysmart in Australia, which also explain scams.
  • Textbooks: classic references include Larry Harris's Trading and Exchanges on market structure, John Hull's Options, Futures, and Other Derivatives and Sheldon Natenberg's Option Volatility and Pricing on derivatives, and Ernest Chan's books on quantitative trading.
  • University courses: many universities publish free lecture material in finance, statistics and programming.
  • This encyclopedia: a structured path starts with what is trading, risk management fundamentals and how to start trading.

The most useful education is often your own record. Paper trading, small real positions and a detailed trading journal show what works for you far more reliably than someone else's screenshots.

Common mistakes

  • Judging skill by lifestyle. Cars, travel and cash say nothing about trading results.
  • Paying for signals instead of learning. Following signals without understanding them leaves you dependent and exposed.
  • Ignoring incentives. Free courses can be costly if they push you to trade very frequently through a broker that pays the course rebates.
  • Course hopping. Buying one system after another after each losing streak.
  • Believing win rates without payoff data. A high win rate means nothing without the size of wins and losses.

FAQ

Are trading courses worth it?

Some are, if they teach risk management, testing and market structure honestly and are reasonably priced. Many are mostly marketing, and free material from exchanges, regulators and textbooks covers much of the same ground.

How can you tell if a trading guru is fake?

Look for a long, independently verified, real-money track record that shows losing periods. Be skeptical of lifestyle images, screenshots, guaranteed returns and heavy promotion of specific brokers or prop firms with affiliate links.

How do trading influencers make money?

Many earn from course sales, subscriptions, affiliate rebates from brokers and exchanges, prop firm commissions and sponsorships. These income sources often do not depend on their followers making money.

Are paid trading signals worth it?

Most paid signal groups do not provide verified evidence of profitable, repeatable results after costs. If you consider one, look for a long, independently verified record and understand how the provider is paid.
