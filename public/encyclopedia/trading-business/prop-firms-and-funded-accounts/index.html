Prop Firms and Funded Accounts

10 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Prop Firms, Tax, Regulation & Scams

A proprietary trading firm, or prop firm, is a company that trades with its own money rather than clients' money. The term now covers two very different businesses: traditional prop firms that hire and back traders with real capital, and online evaluation firms that sell "challenges" in which retail traders try to qualify for a funded account. Understanding how each makes money, what the rules really test, and what usually happens to the people who sign up is essential before paying for one.

Key takeaways

  • Traditional prop firms trade their own capital through employed or partnered traders, often with training and strict risk oversight.
  • Online evaluation firms charge a fee for a trading challenge with a profit target and loss limits; passing earns a "funded" account with a profit split.
  • For many online firms, funded accounts are simulated, and evaluation fees are the main source of revenue; most buyers never receive a payout.
  • Rules such as daily loss limits, trailing drawdowns and consistency requirements decide most outcomes, and they vary between firms and change over time.
  • The online prop industry has seen sudden closures, frozen accounts and payout disputes, so a firm's track record and terms matter as much as its prices.

Two kinds of prop firm

Feature Traditional prop firm Online evaluation firm
How you join Application, interviews, often a trading or quantitative test Buy a challenge online
Capital The firm's real money Often simulated, sometimes mirrored to a real account
Your cost Usually none for employees; some firms charge desk fees or require a deposit An evaluation fee, plus possible reset, activation or data fees
Pay Salary and bonus, or a share of profits A share of simulated profits paid as a payout
Oversight Risk managers, training, team Automated rule checks
Typical markets Futures, options, equities, rates, crypto Forex and CFDs, futures, sometimes crypto

Traditional prop firms

Traditional prop trading has a long history, from Chicago trading pits and London trading arcades to today's electronic market makers and quantitative firms. In the United States, the Volcker Rule, part of the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010, restricted banks from proprietary trading, so most of this activity now sits in independent firms.

The largest modern prop firms are technology-driven market makers and quantitative traders. They hire graduates in mathematics, computer science and engineering, pay salaries and bonuses, and run strict risk controls. Smaller firms may offer a profit split to traders who bring their own experience, sometimes with a capital contribution or desk fee. A trader's losses at a traditional firm are the firm's real losses, so the firm is selective about whom it backs. The career side of this path is covered in trading as a career.

Online evaluation firms

Online evaluation firms grew quickly from the mid-2010s and boomed in the early 2020s. The basic offer:

  1. Pay a fee for a challenge on an account of a stated size, such as $10,000, $50,000 or $100,000.
  2. Reach a profit target without breaking any loss limits or other rules, sometimes over two phases.
  3. If you pass, receive a "funded" account and keep a share of its profits, often stated as 70 to 90 percent.
  4. If you break a rule, the account is closed. You can usually buy another challenge or pay for a reset.

The fee is the most you can lose, which is the main appeal. A trader who would otherwise risk $10,000 of their own money can, in effect, pay a few hundred dollars for a chance at payouts on a much larger account.

Common rules

Rules differ across firms and change often. Read the current terms before buying. Common elements include:

Rule What it means Typical form
Profit target Gain needed to pass a phase A percentage of the starting balance
Daily loss limit Maximum loss allowed in one day A percentage of starting or daily balance, sometimes including open losses
Maximum loss (static) Account may never fall below a fixed level A percentage below the starting balance
Trailing drawdown The loss limit rises as the account makes new highs A fixed amount below the highest balance or equity
Minimum trading days Must trade on a set number of days A handful of days
Consistency rule No single day may make up too much of total profit A percentage cap on the best day
Trading restrictions Limits on news trading, weekend holds, certain strategies or copying between accounts Varies widely

Worked example: static loss limits

A $100,000 challenge has a 5 percent daily loss limit and a 10 percent maximum loss.

  • Maximum loss: the account may never drop below $90,000.
  • Daily loss: if a day starts at $103,000 and the daily limit is measured from the starting balance of the account, the daily floor is $103,000 − $5,000 = $98,000. If it is measured as 5 percent of that day's starting balance, the floor is $103,000 − $5,150 = $97,850.

The same strategy can pass at one firm and fail at another simply because of how these limits are calculated, and whether open losses count.

Worked example: a trailing drawdown

A $50,000 futures-style evaluation has a $2,500 trailing drawdown.

  • At the start, the account fails if it falls to $47,500.
  • The trader makes $1,800 and the account peaks at $51,800. The failure level trails up to $51,800 − $2,500 = $49,300.
  • The market reverses and the account drops to $49,300. The account fails, even though the trader is only $700 below the starting balance.

Many firms stop the trailing level once it reaches the starting balance, and some measure the peak using open, unrealized profits rather than closed trades. A trade that is up $1,500 and then closes flat can permanently raise the failure level. The general mathematics of drawdowns is covered in drawdown and risk of ruin.

The business model

For a typical online evaluation firm, revenue comes from:

  • evaluation fees, often discounted heavily through affiliate codes;
  • resets and repeat purchases after failures;
  • add-ons such as faster payouts or higher profit splits;
  • for futures-style firms, monthly subscriptions and activation fees.

Costs include payouts to traders, platform and data fees, payment processing, staff, and marketing, which often runs through affiliates and social media influencers paid per sale.

Many firms state in their terms that evaluation and funded accounts are simulated, meaning no real trades reach the market. Some firms say they copy the trades of consistently profitable traders into real accounts. Either way, the payouts to funded traders are funded largely from fee revenue. This is a legitimate business model when it is disclosed and payouts are honored. It does mean the firm's income depends on most buyers failing, and that a firm can get into trouble if too many traders succeed or if new sales slow.

Worked example: the economics, from both sides

These numbers are hypothetical and chosen only to show the arithmetic.

The firm's view. In a month, a firm sells 10,000 challenges at $500 each.

  • Fee revenue: 10,000 × $500 = $5,000,000.
  • Suppose 10 percent pass: 1,000 funded accounts.
  • Suppose 30 percent of those receive at least one payout, averaging $2,000, plus a refund of the $500 fee: 300 × $2,500 = $750,000.
  • Payouts and refunds equal 15 percent of fee revenue. The rest covers costs and profit.

The trader's view. Using the same assumptions for one $500 challenge:

  • Probability of reaching a payout: 10% × 30% = 3%.
  • Expected payout: 3% × $2,500 = $75.
  • Expected result per attempt: $75 − $500 = −$425.

To break even on these terms, the trader's own chance of passing and then getting paid would need to be 20 percent ($500 ÷ $2,500), several times the assumed average. Only traders with a real, tested edge and strict risk discipline should expect to beat the averages, and the firm's rules are designed to filter out everyone else.

Reliable, audited pass and payout statistics for the industry are scarce. Where firms or researchers have published figures, the share of evaluation buyers who ever receive a payout has been small. Treat any marketing claim about pass rates with caution.

Payout realities

Reaching a funded account is not the same as getting paid. Common friction points include:

  • Minimum periods and thresholds before the first payout.
  • Identity checks at payout time, which some traders fail to anticipate.
  • Rule reviews after a payout request, where trades are checked against restrictions on news trading, high-frequency or latency-based strategies, hedging between accounts, copy trading, or "gambling" behavior such as one oversized trade.
  • Consistency rules that delay payouts until profit is spread over more days.
  • Changes in terms that apply to existing accounts.

Disputes over denied payouts are a frequent complaint about the industry. Some denials enforce clearly written rules; others rely on vague clauses. Reading the full terms, including the sections on prohibited strategies and payout reviews, before buying is the only real protection.

Firm closures and regulation

The online prop industry has been volatile:

  • Platform shocks. In 2024, restrictions on access to a widely used retail trading platform led several online prop firms to pause sign-ups, change platforms or close within weeks.
  • Regulatory action. In 2023, regulators in the United States and Canada took enforcement action against one large online prop firm, freezing its operations. Whatever the eventual outcome of a case like this, customers of a frozen firm can lose access to accounts and pending payouts for long periods.
  • Unclear regulatory status. Because many firms sell evaluations and operate simulated accounts, they often fall outside the licensing regimes for brokers and investment firms. That also means traders usually have no access to compensation schemes or financial ombudsman services if the firm fails. Regulators in several countries have looked at the model, and rules may change. See trading regulation overview.
  • Fake firms. Some websites copy the look of established prop firms to sell challenges that never pay. See trading scams.

How to evaluate a prop firm

  1. Who runs it? Look for named management, a registered company and a physical address you can verify.
  2. How long has it operated, and has it paid out through market shocks and rule changes?
  3. Is the funded account simulated? Read what the terms actually say about where trades go and where payouts come from.
  4. Are the rules precise? Check exactly how daily loss, drawdown and consistency are calculated, and which strategies are banned.
  5. What happens at payout? Look for the payout review process, identity requirements, minimums and timelines.
  6. Can terms change on existing accounts? Many terms allow it.
  7. Who provides the platform and data? Dependence on a single vendor is a risk.
  8. What do independent traders report? Weigh complaints about denied payouts more heavily than marketing testimonials, and be aware that reviews can be fake or incentivized.

Trading a challenge sensibly

  • Size for the loss limits, not the profit target. A daily limit of 5 percent means risking 1 percent or less per trade if you want to survive a losing streak. See position sizing.
  • Don't rush. Firms without time limits remove much of the pressure to overtrade.
  • Treat fees as a cost of business. Track total spending across all challenges and resets, not just the latest one.
  • Watch for chasing. Buying challenge after challenge to win back previous fees follows the same pattern as chasing losses in gambling. If that pattern feels familiar, handling losses and tilt covers warning signs, and help for problem gambling is available in most countries.

Payouts are often treated as self-employment or contractor income for tax purposes, but this varies by country. See trading taxes overview.

Common mistakes

  • Not reading the full rules. Many failed or denied accounts come from rules the trader did not know existed.
  • Taking oversized risk to hit the target fast. It usually breaches the daily or maximum loss limit instead.
  • Ignoring how drawdown is measured. Trailing and equity-based limits fail accounts that look fine on closed-trade balance.
  • Assuming funded means real capital. Often it does not, and the distinction affects how secure payouts are.
  • Choosing on price and profit split alone. A cheap challenge from a firm that may not pay is worthless.

FAQ

How do prop firms make money?

Traditional prop firms make money from their own trading. Online evaluation firms earn most of their revenue from challenge fees, resets and subscriptions, and pay a portion of that revenue to the minority of traders who pass and get paid.

Are funded accounts real money?

At many online evaluation firms, funded accounts are simulated, and payouts come from the firm's revenue. Some firms say they copy successful traders' trades into real accounts. Check the firm's terms.

What percentage of traders pass prop firm challenges?

Reliable industry-wide statistics are scarce, but published figures and the fee-based business model both point to a small minority of buyers passing and receiving payouts. Marketing claims about pass rates are rarely audited.

Are prop firms legit or a scam?

Many online prop firms are legitimate businesses that disclose their model and pay traders who follow the rules, but the industry has also seen closures, regulatory action, payout disputes and outright fake firms. Research the firm's history, terms and payout record before paying.
