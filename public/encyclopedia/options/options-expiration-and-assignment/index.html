Options Expiration and Assignment

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Expiration is the point at which an option stops existing, and assignment is what happens to an option seller when a buyer exercises. These mechanics decide whether an option turns into shares, cash or nothing at all. Most traders close positions before expiry, but those who hold into the final day, or who sell American-style options, need to understand exercise rules, early assignment, automatic exercise, pin risk and the differences between cash-settled and physically settled options.

Key takeaways

  • Exercising a call buys the underlying at the strike and exercising a put sells it; the seller who is assigned must take the other side.
  • American-style options, including standard US stock and ETF options, can be exercised and assigned on any business day before expiry.
  • In the US, options that finish at least $0.01 in the money at expiration are generally exercised automatically unless the holder instructs otherwise.
  • Pin risk arises when the underlying closes near a short strike, leaving you unsure whether you will be assigned.
  • Cash-settled index options such as SPX pay the difference in cash, while ETF options such as SPY deliver shares.

Exercise and assignment basics

Action Long call Long put Short call (assigned) Short put (assigned)
Result for physically settled options Buy 100 shares at strike Sell 100 shares at strike Sell 100 shares at strike Buy 100 shares at strike

When a holder exercises, the instruction goes through their broker to the clearinghouse. In the US, that is the Options Clearing Corporation (OCC). The OCC assigns the exercise to a clearing firm with a matching short position, using a random process, and the clearing firm then assigns it to one of its customers, using random selection or another fair method disclosed to customers. Sellers usually learn about assignment the next morning.

Most options never get exercised. Closing an option by selling it is usually better than exercising, because the sale also captures any remaining extrinsic value. See options basics.

Expiration timing

  • Standard monthly options on US stocks and ETFs expire on the third Friday of the month. Weekly and daily expirations are also listed on many popular underlyings.
  • Trading hours: most equity options stop trading at 4:00 p.m. ET on expiration day; some index and ETF options trade until 4:15 p.m. ET.
  • Exercise decisions: the OCC's cutoff for submitting exercise instructions on expiration day has been 5:30 p.m. ET, and brokers often set an earlier internal deadline. Check your broker's current policy.

The gap between the 4:00 p.m. close and the exercise cutoff creates a risk discussed below.

Automatic exercise

Under the OCC's exercise-by-exception procedure, US equity options that are $0.01 or more in the money at expiration are exercised automatically, unless the holder submits contrary instructions. Options that are out of the money expire worthless.

Worked example

You own one call with a $50 strike, and the stock closes at $50.08 on expiration day. The call is $0.08 in the money. Unless you tell your broker not to, it will be exercised, and on Monday you will own 100 shares bought at $50, a $5,000 position. If your account cannot support that purchase, your broker may sell the option before the close or liquidate the resulting shares, often at an unfavorable price.

Early assignment

American-style options can be exercised at any time, so sellers can be assigned before expiration. In practice, early exercise is uncommon because the buyer gives up the option's extrinsic value. It becomes likely in two main situations.

Before an ex-dividend date (calls)

A shareholder of record on the ex-dividend date receives the dividend; an option holder does not. If a call's remaining extrinsic value is less than the upcoming dividend, the call owner can gain by exercising the day before the ex-dividend date.

Worked example: A stock trades at $50 and will pay a $0.50 dividend, with the ex-dividend date tomorrow. A $40-strike call trades at $10.05. Its intrinsic value is $10.00, so its extrinsic value is only $0.05. Exercising captures the $0.50 dividend at a cost of $0.05 in forgone time value, a net gain of $0.45 per share. If you are short that call, expect a high chance of assignment tonight. If you are assigned on a covered call, your shares are called away and you miss the dividend. If you are assigned on an uncovered call, you become short 100 shares and owe the dividend.

Deep in-the-money puts

A deep in-the-money put with almost no extrinsic value can be exercised early so the holder receives the strike price in cash sooner and earns interest on it. This is more common when interest rates are higher.

Spreads and early assignment

If the short leg of a spread is assigned early, your long leg still protects you. For example, if the short call in a bull call spread is assigned, you are short 100 shares, but your long call at a lower strike still caps the loss. You can exercise the long call or close both positions. See vertical spreads.

Pin risk

Pin risk arises when the underlying closes at or very near the strike of a short option at expiration. You cannot know for sure whether the holder will exercise, because they may act on after-hours price moves or have their own reasons.

After-hours risk on expiration day

Suppose you are short one $50 put and the stock closes at $50.20 at 4:00 p.m. ET on expiration day. The put is out of the money and would normally expire worthless. After the close, the company releases bad news and the stock trades down to $48 in after-hours trading. The put holder can still submit an exercise instruction before the cutoff. You are assigned and own 100 shares at $50 when they are trading at $48, an immediate loss of $200 that could grow by Monday's open.

The same risk applies in reverse to short calls and to the short legs of spreads, condors and butterflies. When a spread's short strike is pinned and its long strike expires worthless, you can be left with a naked stock position over the weekend. See iron condors and butterflies.

The simplest defense is to close short options that are near the money before the final minutes of expiration day, even at a small cost.

Cash settlement vs physical settlement: SPX vs SPY

Feature SPX options SPY options
Underlying S&P 500 index SPDR S&P 500 ETF shares
Settlement Cash Physical delivery of shares
Exercise style European (expiry only) American (any time)
Early assignment risk None Yes
Contract size $100 x index level 100 shares

Worked example: cash settlement

You hold one SPX call with a 5,000 strike. The final settlement value is 5,020. You receive (5,020 - 5,000) x $100 = $2,000 in cash. No shares change hands, and there is nothing left to manage after expiration.

Worked example: physical settlement

You hold one SPY call with a $500 strike, and SPY closes at $502 on expiration day. The call is auto-exercised, so you buy 100 shares at $500, which requires $50,000 of cash or buying power. You now hold a $50,200 stock position that will move with the market when trading resumes.

AM vs PM settlement

Standard monthly SPX options are AM-settled: they stop trading the day before expiration, and their final value is based on the opening prices of the index's stocks on expiration morning, which can differ noticeably from the prior close. SPX weekly and daily options are PM-settled, based on the closing value. Always check which version you are trading. Same-day trading is covered in 0DTE options.

Options on futures

Options on futures, such as options on E-mini S&P 500 futures, generally exercise into a position in the underlying futures contract rather than into shares. Where an option expires at the same time as a cash-settled future, the result is effectively cash settlement. Contract terms differ by product, so read the specification. See futures basics.

Common mistakes

  • Holding near-the-money short options into the close. This invites pin risk and after-hours surprises.
  • Forgetting dividends. Short in-the-money calls before an ex-dividend date can be assigned early.
  • Letting a long option auto-exercise without the cash. The broker may liquidate at a poor price, or you may be left with an unplanned stock position.
  • Assuming a spread is closed at expiry. One leg can expire in the money while the other expires worthless.
  • Confusing SPX and SPY. One settles in cash, the other in shares, and the contract sizes differ by about ten times.

FAQ

What happens when an option is assigned?

The option seller must fulfil the contract. An assigned call seller sells 100 shares at the strike price, and an assigned put seller buys 100 shares at the strike price, for each contract.

Can I be assigned before expiration?

Yes, if you sell American-style options, which include standard US stock and ETF options. Early assignment is most common for deep in-the-money calls just before an ex-dividend date.

Do options automatically exercise?

In the US, options that are at least $0.01 in the money at expiration are generally exercised automatically unless the holder instructs otherwise. Out-of-the-money options expire worthless.

What is pin risk in options?

Pin risk is the uncertainty that arises when the underlying closes very near a short option's strike at expiration. You may or may not be assigned, and after-hours moves can make the outcome costly.
