Iron Condors and Butterflies

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Iron condors and butterflies are defined-risk options strategies that profit when the underlying stays within a range until expiration. An iron condor combines a bull put spread and a bear call spread to collect a credit across a wide range. A butterfly combines options at three strikes to profit most if the price finishes near one target. Both are popular in quiet, range-bound markets and when implied volatility is high, but they cap gains and can lose their full risk on a single sharp move.

Key takeaways

  • An iron condor is a short put spread plus a short call spread, with maximum profit equal to the credit received.
  • A butterfly uses three strikes and pays most when the underlying finishes exactly at the middle strike.
  • Both strategies have known maximum losses and breakevens, which can be calculated before entry.
  • They are short-volatility trades: they benefit from time decay and falling implied volatility and suffer from large moves.
  • Pin risk around the short strikes at expiration can leave an unexpected stock position, so many traders close before expiry.

Iron condor construction

An iron condor has four legs, all with the same expiry:

  1. Sell an out-of-the-money put.
  2. Buy a further out-of-the-money put (the protective "wing").
  3. Sell an out-of-the-money call.
  4. Buy a further out-of-the-money call.

Legs 1 and 2 form a bull put spread; legs 3 and 4 form a bear call spread, both explained in vertical spreads. The position collects a net credit.

Worked example: iron condor

A stock trades at $100. Option prices are illustrative, and each contract covers 100 shares.

Leg Strike Price Cash flow
Sell put $90 $1.20 +$1.20
Buy put $85 $0.50 -$0.50
Sell call $110 $1.10 +$1.10
Buy call $115 $0.40 -$0.40
Net credit +$1.40
  • Maximum profit: $1.40, or $140, if the stock is between $90 and $110 at expiry.
  • Maximum loss: width of one side minus the credit = $5.00 - $1.40 = $3.60, or $360. This happens below $85 or above $115. Only one side can lose at expiry, because the stock cannot be in both places.
  • Lower breakeven: $90 - $1.40 = $88.60.
  • Upper breakeven: $110 + $1.40 = $111.40.
Stock at expiry Put spread value Call spread value Profit or loss
$80 $5.00 $0 -$360
$88.60 $1.40 $0 $0
$95 $0 $0 +$140
$105 $0 $0 +$140
$111.40 $0 $1.40 $0
$120 $0 $5.00 -$360

The profit or loss is the $1.40 credit minus the value of whichever spread is in the money at expiry, times 100. The trade risks $360 to make $140. That ratio is typical: iron condors tend to win more often than they lose, but each loss is larger than each win.

Butterfly construction

A long call butterfly uses three equally spaced strikes:

  1. Buy one lower-strike call.
  2. Sell two middle-strike calls.
  3. Buy one higher-strike call.

The same shape can be built with puts. The position usually costs a small debit.

Worked example: long call butterfly

With the stock at $100:

Leg Strike Price Cash flow
Buy 1 call $95 $7.00 -$7.00
Sell 2 calls $100 $4.00 each +$8.00
Buy 1 call $105 $2.00 -$2.00
Net debit -$1.00
  • Maximum loss: $1.00, or $100, if the stock is at or below $95 or at or above $105.
  • Maximum profit: width of one wing minus the debit = $5.00 - $1.00 = $4.00, or $400, if the stock is exactly at $100 at expiry.
  • Lower breakeven: $95 + $1.00 = $96.00.
  • Upper breakeven: $105 - $1.00 = $104.00.

At $100 at expiry, the $95 call is worth $5.00 and the other calls are worthless, so the position is worth $5.00 against a $1.00 cost. At $102, the $95 call is worth $7.00, the two short $100 calls cost $2.00 each ($4.00 total), and the $105 call is worthless: $7.00 - $4.00 = $3.00, a $2.00 profit. Profit falls off steadily on either side of the middle strike.

Butterflies are cheap relative to their maximum payoff, but the maximum is reached only in a narrow zone, so most butterflies that are held to expiry finish with less than the full payoff.

Iron butterfly

An iron butterfly is an iron condor whose short put and short call share the same strike, usually at the money.

Worked example

With the stock at $100: sell the $100 call for $4.00, sell the $100 put for $3.80, buy the $110 call for $1.00 and buy the $90 put for $1.00.

  • Net credit: $4.00 + $3.80 - $1.00 - $1.00 = $5.80, or $580.
  • Maximum profit: $580, if the stock finishes exactly at $100.
  • Maximum loss: $10.00 - $5.80 = $4.20, or $420, below $90 or above $110.
  • Breakevens: $100 - $5.80 = $94.20 and $100 + $5.80 = $105.80.

Compared with the iron condor, the iron butterfly collects much more credit and has a better reward-to-risk ratio, but its profit zone is narrower and peaks at a single price.

Broken-wing butterflies

A broken-wing butterfly uses unequal wing widths, which shifts the risk to one side. It can often be opened for a small credit, so there is no loss if the stock moves away in one direction.

Worked example: call broken-wing butterfly

Buy the $95 call for $7.00, sell two $100 calls for $4.00 each, and buy the $110 call for $0.80.

  • Net: -$7.00 + $8.00 - $0.80 = +$0.20 credit, or $20.
  • Below $95: all calls expire worthless; profit is the $20 credit.
  • At $100: the $95 call is worth $5.00; profit = $5.00 + $0.20 = $5.20, or $520.
  • Above $110: the position is worth (S - 95) - 2 x (S - 100) + (S - 110) = -$5.00; loss = $5.00 - $0.20 = $4.80, or $480.
  • Upper breakeven: between $100 and $110 the position is worth $105 - S, so the breakeven is where $105 - S + $0.20 = 0, which is $105.20.

The trade has no downside risk at expiry but a larger loss on a strong rally than a standard butterfly. Put versions shift the risk to the downside.

When traders use these strategies

Condition Why it suits condors and butterflies
Range-bound market Profit depends on the price staying within a zone
High implied volatility Short options collect more premium, and a fall in IV helps
After an event Some traders sell condors once IV is high and the event risk has passed
Specific price target Butterflies can express a view on where the price will settle

These are short-volatility positions with negative gamma and positive theta near the short strikes; see options Greeks and implied volatility.

Managing positions

  • Profit targets: many traders close iron condors once a chosen share of the credit is captured, rather than wait for the last few cents while risk stays high.
  • Loss limits: decide in advance, such as closing when the loss reaches a set multiple of the credit.
  • Adjusting: one common adjustment is rolling the untested side closer to the price to collect more credit. Adjustments add costs and can enlarge risk, so they need rules.
  • Closing before expiry: the last days carry the most gamma risk relative to the remaining premium.

Pin risk

If the stock closes at or very near a short strike at expiration, you cannot be sure whether that option will be exercised. You might be assigned on a short option while your protective long option expires worthless, leaving you with a long or short stock position over the weekend. After-hours moves can make this costly. See options expiration and assignment.

Common mistakes

  • Treating high win rates as safety. A condor that wins most months can give back many months of gains in one loss.
  • Setting wings too narrow to save commissions or too wide to collect more. Width directly sets maximum loss.
  • Holding through earnings or major news. A gap can move the stock past a wing overnight.
  • Ignoring bid-ask spreads. Four legs means four spreads to cross on entry and exit.
  • Adjusting without a plan. Repeated rolling can turn a defined-risk trade into a much larger one.

FAQ

How does an iron condor make money?

It collects a credit by selling an out-of-the-money put spread and call spread. If the stock stays between the short strikes until expiration, all options expire worthless and the trader keeps the full credit.

What is the difference between an iron condor and an iron butterfly?

An iron condor's short strikes are apart, creating a wide flat profit zone. An iron butterfly sells both short options at the same strike, collecting more credit but peaking at a single price.

What is the max loss on an iron condor?

The width of one spread minus the net credit received, times 100. A $5-wide iron condor sold for $1.40 has a maximum loss of $360 per contract.

When should you close an iron condor?

Many traders close well before expiration, once a target share of the credit is captured or a preset loss is reached. Holding into the final days increases gamma and pin risk.
