Options Basics

9 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

An option is a contract that gives its buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an asset at a fixed price on or before a set date. The buyer pays a price called the premium; the seller collects it and takes on the obligation. Options are used to speculate, to hedge, and to generate income, and they let traders express views on direction, timing and volatility that plain stock trading cannot. They also lose value as time passes, which is why many option buyers lose money even when they get the direction right.

Key takeaways

  • A call gives the right to buy at the strike price, and a put gives the right to sell at the strike price.
  • An option's premium is made up of intrinsic value, which is what it would be worth if exercised now, and extrinsic value, which reflects time and volatility.
  • Option buyers risk only the premium paid, while option sellers collect the premium but can face losses far larger than it.
  • Standard US equity options control 100 shares, so a quoted premium of $2.00 costs $200 per contract.
  • Brokers grant options permissions in levels, with the riskiest strategies, such as selling uncovered options, restricted to experienced accounts.

The building blocks

Term Meaning
Underlying The asset the option is on: a stock, ETF, index, future or crypto asset
Call Right to buy the underlying at the strike price
Put Right to sell the underlying at the strike price
Strike price The fixed price at which the option can be exercised
Expiration date The last day the option exists
Premium The price of the option, paid by the buyer to the seller
Exercise The buyer using the right to buy or sell at the strike
Assignment The seller being required to fulfil the obligation after an exercise
Multiplier The number of units each contract controls; 100 shares for standard US equity options

Buyers are said to be "long" the option, and sellers, also called writers, are "short". Every option has both a buyer and a seller, so options are a zero-sum contract before costs: the buyer's gain is the seller's loss and the reverse.

The contract multiplier

Option prices are quoted per share, but a standard listed equity option in the US covers 100 shares. A quote of $3.40 therefore means $340 per contract, before commissions. Index options such as those on the S&P 500 (SPX) also use a $100 multiplier on the index level. Other markets use different multipliers, and options on futures use the futures contract's size, so always check the contract specification.

Calls: the right to buy

A call option gains value when the underlying rises. At expiration, a call is worth the stock price minus the strike, or zero if the stock is at or below the strike.

Worked example: buying a call

A stock trades at $100. You buy one call with a $105 strike expiring in 30 days for a premium of $2.00. Cost: $2.00 x 100 = $200.

  • Breakeven at expiry: strike + premium = $105 + $2.00 = $107.
  • If the stock is at $115 at expiry: the call is worth $115 - $105 = $10.00, or $1,000. Profit = $1,000 - $200 = $800.
  • If the stock is at $107: the call is worth $2.00, or $200. Profit = $0.
  • If the stock is at $105 or below: the call expires worthless. Loss = $200, the full premium.
Stock price at expiry Call value per share Profit or loss per contract
$95 $0.00 -$200
$105 $0.00 -$200
$107 $2.00 $0
$110 $5.00 +$300
$115 $10.00 +$800
$120 $15.00 +$1,300

The buyer's loss is capped at $200, and the potential gain has no fixed cap. The stock rose 15 percent in the $115 case, and the call position returned 400 percent on the $200 premium. In the $105 case the stock rose 5 percent, and the call position lost 100 percent. That leverage cuts both ways.

The call seller's side

The seller of that call collects $200 up front. If the stock stays at or below $105, the seller keeps the full $200. At $115, the seller loses $800. At $120, the seller loses $1,300. A seller who does not own the stock, called a naked or uncovered call writer, faces losses that grow without limit as the stock rises. A seller who owns 100 shares is writing a covered call, explained in covered calls and cash-secured puts.

Puts: the right to sell

A put option gains value when the underlying falls. At expiration, a put is worth the strike minus the stock price, or zero if the stock is at or above the strike.

Worked example: buying a put

A stock trades at $100. You buy one put with a $95 strike expiring in 30 days for $1.50. Cost: $150.

  • Breakeven at expiry: strike - premium = $95 - $1.50 = $93.50.
  • If the stock is at $85 at expiry: the put is worth $95 - $85 = $10.00, or $1,000. Profit = $1,000 - $150 = $850.
  • If the stock is at $93.50: the put is worth $1.50. Profit = $0.
  • If the stock is at $95 or above: the put expires worthless. Loss = $150.

The most a put buyer can make is if the stock goes to zero: ($95 - $0) x 100 - $150 = $9,350. The put seller has the mirror image: keeps $150 if the stock stays at or above $95, and loses up to $9,350 if it goes to zero.

Puts are commonly used as insurance. An investor who owns 100 shares at $100 and buys the $95 put above has locked in a minimum sale price of $95 until expiry, for a cost of $1.50 a share. This is called a protective put.

The four basic positions

Position Outlook Maximum gain Maximum loss Breakeven at expiry
Long call Bullish Unlimited Premium paid Strike + premium
Short call (uncovered) Neutral to bearish Premium received Unlimited Strike + premium
Long put Bearish Strike - premium (stock to zero) Premium paid Strike - premium
Short put Neutral to bullish Premium received Strike - premium (stock to zero) Strike - premium

Every other options strategy, from vertical spreads to iron condors and butterflies, is a combination of these four building blocks, sometimes with the underlying stock added.

Intrinsic and extrinsic value

An option's premium has two parts.

  • Intrinsic value is what the option would be worth if exercised immediately. For a call, it is the stock price minus the strike, if positive. For a put, it is the strike minus the stock price, if positive. It is never negative.
  • Extrinsic value, also called time value, is everything else: premium minus intrinsic value. It reflects the chance that the option gains more value before expiry, which depends on time remaining and expected volatility.

Worked example

A stock trades at $100.

Option Premium Intrinsic value Extrinsic value
$95 call $6.20 $100 - $95 = $5.00 $6.20 - $5.00 = $1.20
$100 call $3.50 $0.00 $3.50
$105 call $1.40 $0.00 $1.40
$105 put $6.10 $105 - $100 = $5.00 $6.10 - $5.00 = $1.10

Extrinsic value shrinks toward zero as expiration approaches, a process called time decay. At expiry, an option is worth only its intrinsic value. How fast it decays is measured by theta, one of the options Greeks. The market's expected volatility, called implied volatility, is the other major driver of extrinsic value.

Moneyness

Moneyness describes where the strike sits relative to the current price of the underlying.

Term Call Put
In the money (ITM) Stock price above strike Stock price below strike
At the money (ATM) Stock price at or near strike Stock price at or near strike
Out of the money (OTM) Stock price below strike Stock price above strike

ITM options have intrinsic value and cost more. OTM options are made entirely of extrinsic value. They are cheaper, but the stock must move past the strike before expiry just for the option to be worth anything at expiration. Cheap far-OTM options attract beginners because of their low price, but most of them expire worthless.

American vs European exercise

  • American-style options can be exercised on any business day up to and including expiration. Standard US options on individual stocks and ETFs are American-style.
  • European-style options can be exercised only at expiration. Many index options, including SPX options, are European-style and cash-settled.

In practice, most option positions are closed by selling them in the market rather than exercising them, because selling captures any remaining extrinsic value. Exercise and assignment rules are covered in options expiration and assignment.

Why traders use options

  1. Leveraged speculation: a call or put controls 100 shares for a fraction of the stock's price, with risk limited to the premium.
  2. Hedging: puts can protect a stock portfolio against a fall; see correlation and portfolio risk.
  3. Income: selling options collects premium, at the cost of taking on obligations and risk.
  4. Trading volatility: some strategies, such as straddles and strangles, profit from big moves or from quiet markets regardless of direction.

Options approval levels

Because selling options can create large losses, brokers require an application before allowing options trading and assign an approval level based on experience, income, net worth and stated objectives. Levels and their names vary by broker and country, but a typical scheme looks like this:

Level Typically allowed
1 Covered calls, cash-secured puts
2 Buying calls and puts
3 Defined-risk spreads, such as verticals and iron condors
4 Selling uncovered (naked) puts
5 Selling uncovered (naked) calls

In the US, brokers must also provide the Options Disclosure Document, "Characteristics and Risks of Standardized Options", published by the Options Clearing Corporation. Reading it is worthwhile.

Common mistakes

  • Buying far-OTM options because they are cheap. Low price reflects low probability of payoff.
  • Ignoring time decay. A stock that moves the right way too slowly can still produce a loss on a long option.
  • Forgetting the multiplier. A $5.00 option is $500 per contract, and 10 contracts is $5,000.
  • Selling naked options for "easy" income. Many small wins can be erased by one large move; size for the worst case.
  • Holding through earnings without understanding volatility. Option prices often drop sharply after the announcement even if the stock moves; see implied volatility.

Options are leveraged instruments, and studies of retail options activity have generally found that retail traders as a group lose money on them after costs, particularly on short-dated contracts.

FAQ

What is the difference between a call and a put?

A call gives the buyer the right to buy the underlying at the strike price, so it gains value when the price rises. A put gives the right to sell at the strike, so it gains value when the price falls.

How much does one options contract cost?

Multiply the quoted premium by the contract multiplier, which is 100 for standard US equity options. A call quoted at $2.50 costs $250 per contract, plus commissions and fees.

Can you lose more than you invest in options?

Option buyers can lose at most the premium they paid. Option sellers can lose much more than the premium received, and an uncovered call seller's potential loss is unlimited.

What happens if my option expires in the money?

In the US, listed equity options that finish at least $0.01 in the money are generally exercised automatically unless you instruct otherwise, which can leave you with a stock position. Check your broker's policy and close positions before expiry if you do not want that.
