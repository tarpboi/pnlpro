Vertical Spreads

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Options Trading

A vertical spread is an options position made by buying one option and selling another of the same type (both calls or both puts) on the same underlying and expiry, but at different strike prices. The sold option offsets part of the cost or risk of the bought one, so the trade has a fixed maximum profit and a fixed maximum loss known from the start. Vertical spreads are one of the most common ways to take a directional view with options while keeping risk defined.

Key takeaways

  • There are four basic verticals: bull call and bear put spreads are paid for (debit), while bull put and bear call spreads collect a credit.
  • Maximum profit, maximum loss and breakeven can all be calculated before entering the trade.
  • The distance between the strikes, called the width, sets the combined size of the maximum profit and maximum loss.
  • Credit spreads tend to win more often but make less per win than they risk; debit spreads tend to win less often but make more per win.
  • Spreads reduce exposure to time decay and implied volatility compared with single options, but they do not remove it.

The four vertical spreads

Spread Construction Outlook Debit or credit
Bull call spread Buy lower-strike call, sell higher-strike call Bullish Debit
Bear put spread Buy higher-strike put, sell lower-strike put Bearish Debit
Bull put spread Sell higher-strike put, buy lower-strike put Bullish or neutral Credit
Bear call spread Sell lower-strike call, buy higher-strike call Bearish or neutral Credit

All of the examples below use a stock trading at $100, options with the same expiry, and the standard US multiplier of 100 shares per contract. Option prices are illustrative.

Formulas

For a spread whose strikes are a set width apart:

Debit spread Credit spread
Maximum loss Net debit paid Width - net credit
Maximum profit Width - net debit Net credit received
Breakeven (call spreads) Lower strike + net debit Lower strike + net credit
Breakeven (put spreads) Higher strike - net debit Higher strike - net credit

Notice that maximum profit plus maximum loss always equals the width of the spread.

Bull call spread

Buy the $100 call for $4.00 and sell the $110 call for $1.50.

  • Net debit: $4.00 - $1.50 = $2.50, or $250 per spread.
  • Maximum loss: $250, if the stock is at or below $100 at expiry.
  • Maximum profit: ($110 - $100) - $2.50 = $7.50, or $750, if the stock is at or above $110.
  • Breakeven: $100 + $2.50 = $102.50.

Compared with buying the $100 call alone for $4.00, the spread costs $1.50 less and has a lower breakeven ($102.50 instead of $104.00). In exchange, it gives up every dollar of gain above $110.

Bear put spread

Buy the $100 put for $3.80 and sell the $90 put for $1.30.

  • Net debit: $3.80 - $1.30 = $2.50, or $250.
  • Maximum loss: $250, if the stock is at or above $100.
  • Maximum profit: ($100 - $90) - $2.50 = $7.50, or $750, if the stock is at or below $90.
  • Breakeven: $100 - $2.50 = $97.50.

Bull put spread (credit)

Sell the $95 put for $2.00 and buy the $90 put for $0.80.

  • Net credit: $2.00 - $0.80 = $1.20, or $120.
  • Maximum profit: $120, if the stock is at or above $95 at expiry.
  • Maximum loss: ($95 - $90) - $1.20 = $3.80, or $380, if the stock is at or below $90.
  • Breakeven: $95 - $1.20 = $93.80.

The stock can stay flat, rise, or even fall up to $5 and the spread still earns its full $120. The trade-off is risking $380 to make $120. Brokers usually hold the maximum loss, $380, as margin for a credit spread.

Bear call spread (credit)

Sell the $105 call for $2.10 and buy the $110 call for $0.90.

  • Net credit: $2.10 - $0.90 = $1.20, or $120.
  • Maximum profit: $120, if the stock is at or below $105.
  • Maximum loss: ($110 - $105) - $1.20 = $3.80, or $380, if the stock is at or above $110.
  • Breakeven: $105 + $1.20 = $106.20.

Debit vs credit: the probability trade-off

Debit spreads Credit spreads
Typical win rate Lower Higher
Size of win vs loss Win is often larger than loss Win is usually smaller than loss
Needs the stock to Move in your direction Not move too far against you
Time decay Usually works against you when OTM Usually works for you when OTM

Neither is better by default. Option prices already reflect the market's probability estimates, so a credit spread that wins 70 percent of the time is priced so that its occasional large losses offset the many small gains, before costs. Long-run results depend on whether your view is better than the market's, and on costs. See risk/reward and expectancy.

Choosing strikes

  • Width: wider spreads cost more (debit) or risk more (credit) and have larger potential gains. Narrow spreads keep risk small but commissions take a bigger share.
  • Moneyness: a debit spread bought at the money is cheaper than a single option but still needs a move. A credit spread sold further out of the money has a higher chance of success and a smaller credit.
  • Delta as a guide: traders often choose a short strike by delta. A short strike with delta around 0.30 is often read as having roughly a 30 percent chance of finishing in the money. See options Greeks.
  • Implied volatility: when IV is high, credit spreads collect more premium; when IV is low, debit spreads are cheaper. See implied volatility.
  • Liquidity: wide bid-ask spreads on each leg can eat much of the edge, so use options with active markets and use limit orders at a net price.

Managing a vertical spread

  • Taking profits early: many traders close credit spreads after capturing a chosen share of the maximum profit, rather than holding to expiry for the last few cents.
  • Cutting losses: set a loss level in advance, such as a fixed dollar amount or a multiple of the credit received.
  • Expiry risk: if the stock is between the strikes at expiration, one leg may expire in the money and the other out of the money. You could be assigned on the short leg and end up with a stock position of 100 shares. Close spreads before expiry if there is any doubt; see options expiration and assignment.
  • Early assignment: a deep in-the-money short leg on an American-style option can be assigned early, especially before an ex-dividend date. Your long leg still limits the risk, but you must handle the resulting stock position.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing win rates without payoffs. A 75 percent win rate on credit spreads can still lose money if losses are three to four times the size of wins.
  • Legging in. Entering one leg and waiting to add the second exposes you to an adverse move in between.
  • Holding into expiration with the stock near the short strike. Pin risk can turn a defined-risk trade into an unexpected stock position.
  • Ignoring commissions on narrow spreads. Four commissions (two legs in, two out) can be a large share of a small spread's profit.
  • Assuming defined risk means low risk. Maximum loss is known, but it is often realized in full.

FAQ

What is the difference between a debit spread and a credit spread?

A debit spread costs money to open and profits when the stock moves in your chosen direction. A credit spread pays you to open and profits when the stock stays on the right side of the short strike.

How do you calculate the max loss on a credit spread?

Subtract the net credit from the width of the strikes and multiply by 100. A $5-wide spread sold for $1.20 has a maximum loss of $3.80 per share, or $380 per contract.

Can you lose more than the max loss on a vertical spread?

Not at expiration if both legs are held. The risk of a surprise comes from early assignment or one leg expiring in the money, which can leave a stock position that keeps moving after the market closes.

Is a bull call spread better than buying a call?

It is cheaper and has a lower breakeven, but caps profit at the higher strike. It suits a moderately bullish view with a price target, while a single call suits a view on a large move.
