0DTE Options

6 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

0DTE options, short for "zero days to expiration", are options traded on the day they expire. They are most common on the S&P 500 index (SPX) and on the largest index ETFs such as SPY and QQQ, which now have expirations every trading day. Because they have only hours of life left, 0DTE options are cheap in dollar terms, extremely sensitive to small price moves, and lose their remaining time value by the close, a mix that makes them attractive to speculators and dangerous for almost everyone.

Key takeaways

  • 0DTE options expire the same day they are traded, so all of their extrinsic value disappears within hours.
  • SPX gained expirations on every weekday in 2022, and 0DTE trading has since grown into a large share of index options volume.
  • At-the-money 0DTE options have very high gamma, so their delta and value can swing from near zero to large amounts on small moves.
  • Buyers usually lose the premium, sellers collect small amounts often and risk large losses suddenly, and both face fast decisions with little time to correct mistakes.
  • Retail traders as a group have historically lost money on short-dated options, and the fast pace can encourage gambling-like behavior.

Why 0DTE trading exploded

Short-dated options have existed for years, first as monthly and then weekly expirations. Cboe added Tuesday and Thursday SPX expirations in 2022, which, together with the existing Monday, Wednesday and Friday expirations, created an SPX expiry every weekday. Daily expirations on major ETFs such as SPY and QQQ followed. Cboe's published figures have put same-day options at around half of SPX options volume in recent years.

Several forces drove the growth:

  • Low ticket price: an option with hours to live can cost a fraction of a longer-dated one, attracting small accounts.
  • No overnight risk: positions are closed or settled by the end of the day.
  • Precise event trading: traders can target a single day's economic release or central bank decision.
  • Institutional use: funds and market makers use same-day options to hedge or adjust exposure for specific sessions.
  • Easy access: commission-free trading apps and simplified options interfaces lowered barriers for retail traders.

SPX options are much larger contracts. With the index at 5,000 and a $100 multiplier, one contract covers $500,000 of notional exposure. A Mini-SPX option (XSP) is one-tenth the size and also cash-settled. For US taxpayers, broad-based index options such as SPX are generally treated as Section 1256 contracts, while ETF options are not; check current rules. Settlement differences are covered in options expiration and assignment.

Gamma risk and time decay

0DTE options behave differently from longer-dated options because two Greeks become extreme. See options Greeks for the definitions.

  • Gamma: with hours left, an at-the-money option's delta can swing from about 0.50 to 0.80 or more, or down toward 0.20 or less, on a move of a few tenths of a percent.
  • Theta: all remaining extrinsic value must vanish by the close. An option that is out of the money at 4:00 p.m. ET expires worthless, regardless of how close it came during the day.

Worked example: a 0DTE SPX call in the last hour

With about an hour left in the session, SPX is at 5,000. Suppose a 5,000-strike call costs $7.00, or $700 per contract, and has a delta near 0.50.

  • SPX rises 15 points (0.3 percent) and closes at 5,015: the call settles at $15.00, worth $1,500. Profit: $1,500 - $700 = $800, a 114 percent return.
  • SPX closes at 5,007: the call settles at $7.00. Profit: $0.
  • SPX closes at 5,000 or below: the call expires worthless. Loss: $700, or 100 percent.

A 0.3 percent move in the index produced a gain of more than 100 percent, and no move at all produced a total loss. That swing is gamma at work, and it is why 0DTE positions can change value by large amounts within minutes.

Common 0DTE strategies

Strategy Description Main risk
Buying calls or puts Directional bet on an intraday move Premium decays to zero if the move doesn't come quickly
Long straddles or strangles Bet on a large move either way, often on data days The move is smaller than priced in
Selling credit spreads Collect premium on a side the trader expects to hold Sharp move through the short strike late in the day
Iron condors and butterflies Profit if the index stays in a range or near a target A trend day breaks the range
Hedging Short-term protection for a portfolio or futures position Cost of repeatedly buying protection

Related strategies are covered in vertical spreads, straddles and strangles and iron condors and butterflies.

Worked example: a 0DTE credit spread

In the morning, SPX is at 5,000. A trader sells a 4,950/4,940 put spread for $1.00, collecting $100 per spread.

  • If SPX closes above 4,950: both puts expire worthless; profit is $100.
  • If SPX closes at or below 4,940: the spread is worth $10.00; loss is $10.00 - $1.00 = $9.00, or $900.
  • Breakeven: 4,950 - 1.00 = 4,949.

The trade needs only a 1.2 percent fall, from 5,000 to 4,940, to lose its maximum $900. That is nine winning days' worth of premium lost in one session. Intraday declines of that size are unusual in calm markets but not rare in volatile years.

Risks for buyers

  • Most 0DTE options expire worthless. The low price reflects a low chance of finishing in the money.
  • Timing must be nearly perfect. Being right about direction an hour too late is the same as being wrong.
  • Spreads and slippage matter more. On a cheap option, a few cents of bid-ask spread can be a large percentage of the premium.

Risks for sellers

  • Small gains, large losses. Selling far out-of-the-money options wins often but can lose many times the premium on a sharp move.
  • Gamma near the close. A position that looked safe at 3:00 p.m. ET can be deep in the money by 3:55.
  • Margin and liquidation. Brokers may raise requirements or close positions automatically as risk grows.
  • Assignment on ETF options. Short SPY or QQQ options that finish in the money are assigned, creating stock positions that carry risk into after-hours trading.

Effects on the wider market

Some analysts argue that heavy 0DTE activity can amplify or dampen intraday moves, depending on how dealers hedge. Others argue the effect is smaller than headlines suggest because positions are often balanced. The evidence is still debated. See gamma exposure and dealer hedging.

Common mistakes

  • Treating 0DTE options as lottery tickets. The occasional big win is memorable, but the many small losses add up.
  • Averaging down. Adding to a losing 0DTE position as time runs out compounds losses.
  • Selling premium without a stop. One trend day can erase weeks of credit.
  • Trading through data releases without a plan. CPI, jobs reports and central bank decisions can move the index sharply in seconds.
  • Ignoring the clock. Decay accelerates through the afternoon, and liquidity can thin near the close.

A note on gambling: the speed, low ticket price and lottery-like payoffs of 0DTE options can feed compulsive behavior. If trading feels like something you can't stop, or you are chasing losses, consider stepping away and talking to a problem gambling support service in your country. See handling losses and tilt.

FAQ

What does 0DTE mean in options?

0DTE means zero days to expiration: the option expires at the end of the same trading day it is traded. SPX, SPY and QQQ have options expiring every trading day.

Are 0DTE options profitable?

They can be for individual trades, but they carry extreme gamma and time decay. Retail traders as a group have historically lost money on short-dated options, and most 0DTE options bought expire worthless.

Why do traders prefer SPX for 0DTE?

SPX options are cash-settled and European-style, so there is no early assignment and no stock position at expiry. In the US, they may also qualify for Section 1256 tax treatment.

Can you lose more than you invest in 0DTE options?

Buyers can lose only the premium paid. Sellers of uncovered 0DTE options can lose far more than they collected, and sellers of spreads can lose the full width of the spread minus the credit.
