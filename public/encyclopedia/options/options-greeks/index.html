The Options Greeks

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

The Greeks are a set of measures that describe how an option's price is expected to change when one input changes and the others stay the same. Delta measures sensitivity to the underlying price, gamma measures how fast delta itself changes, theta measures time decay, vega measures sensitivity to implied volatility, and rho measures sensitivity to interest rates. Together they turn an option from a lottery ticket into a position whose risks can be measured, compared and managed.

Key takeaways

  • Delta tells you roughly how much an option's price moves for a $1 move in the underlying, and it also works as a share-equivalent exposure.
  • Gamma is the rate of change of delta, and it is largest for at-the-money options close to expiry.
  • Theta is the daily cost of holding an option, and it speeds up as expiration nears.
  • Vega measures exposure to changes in implied volatility, which can move option prices even when the stock does not.
  • Adding up the Greeks across all positions gives position Greeks, the clearest summary of what a portfolio is actually exposed to.

A reference option

The examples below use one option throughout. A stock trades at $100. A call with a $100 strike and 30 days to expiry is priced at $3.50, with implied volatility around 30 percent. Its Greeks, rounded and approximately as an option pricing model would give them, are:

Greek Value Plain meaning
Delta 0.52 The call gains about $0.52 per share if the stock rises $1
Gamma 0.046 Delta rises by about 0.046 if the stock rises $1
Theta -0.06 The call loses about $0.06 per share per day from time decay
Vega 0.11 The call gains about $0.11 per share if implied volatility rises 1 percentage point
Rho 0.04 The call gains about $0.04 per share if interest rates rise 1 percentage point

Standard US equity options cover 100 shares, so multiply each figure by 100 for the dollar effect per contract. A theta of -0.06 means about $6 a day per contract. The Greeks are estimates from a model, and they change continuously; see options basics for the contract mechanics.

Delta

Delta is the expected change in the option's price for a $1 change in the underlying. Calls have positive delta between 0 and 1. Puts have negative delta between 0 and -1.

What delta tells you

  • Price sensitivity: with delta 0.52, a $1 rise in the stock adds about $0.52 to the call, or $52 per contract.
  • Share equivalent: one contract with delta 0.52 behaves roughly like 52 shares for small moves.
  • Rough probability: traders often use delta as a loose estimate of the chance an option finishes in the money. A 0.30-delta option is often read as having roughly a 30 percent chance. This is a rule of thumb, not an exact probability.
Moneyness Typical call delta Typical put delta
Deep in the money Close to 1.00 Close to -1.00
At the money About 0.50 About -0.50
Far out of the money Close to 0 Close to 0

Gamma

Gamma is the change in delta for a $1 change in the underlying. Long options, calls or puts, always have positive gamma; short options have negative gamma.

Worked example: delta and gamma together

The stock rises from $100 to $101.

  • New delta is about 0.52 + 0.046 = 0.566.
  • Because delta grew during the move, the price change is better estimated using the average delta: 0.52 + (0.5 x 0.046) = 0.543.
  • New call price is about $3.50 + $0.543 = $4.04.

If the stock falls $1 instead, delta drops to about 0.474 and the call falls by about 0.52 - 0.023 = $0.497, to about $3.00. Positive gamma means the call gains a little more on the way up than it loses on the way down for the same size move. Short-option sellers have the opposite: their losses accelerate as the stock moves against them.

How gamma changes

Gamma is highest for at-the-money options and grows sharply as expiration approaches. A same-day at-the-money option can see its delta swing from about 0.50 to close to 1 or 0 on a small move. That is why 0DTE options are so sensitive, and why dealers' hedging of gamma can affect the underlying market, as explained in gamma exposure and dealer hedging.

Theta

Theta is the change in the option's price from one day passing, with everything else unchanged. Long options have negative theta; short options have positive theta. Theta is the cost of holding the right that an option gives you.

Worked example

Holding the reference call for 5 days with the stock flat and volatility unchanged costs about 5 x $0.06 = $0.30 per share, or $30 per contract, taking the call from about $3.50 to about $3.20. In reality theta is not constant: it increases as expiry gets closer.

How theta changes

  • Time: an at-the-money option loses extrinsic value slowly at first and faster in its final weeks. A 90-day option might lose a few cents a day, and the same strike with a week left can lose several times that.
  • Moneyness: theta is largest in dollar terms for at-the-money options, which carry the most extrinsic value. Deep in-the-money and far out-of-the-money options have less to lose.

Option sellers aim to collect theta. They are paid for it because they also take on negative gamma, which is the risk of a large move.

Vega

Vega is the change in the option's price for a 1 percentage point change in implied volatility. Long options have positive vega; short options have negative vega.

Worked example

Before an earnings report, implied volatility on the reference call rises from 30 percent to 36 percent with the stock unchanged. The call gains about 6 x $0.11 = $0.66, to about $4.16. After the report, if implied volatility drops back to 30 percent, that $0.66 disappears, even if the stock barely moved. This is called IV crush.

How vega changes

Vega is largest for at-the-money options and for longer-dated options, because there is more time for volatility to matter. A one-year option has far more vega than a one-week option at the same strike.

Rho

Rho is the change in the option's price for a 1 percentage point change in interest rates. Calls have positive rho and puts have negative rho, because higher rates make it relatively more attractive to hold a call than to buy the stock outright. Rho is small for short-dated options and usually the least important Greek for retail traders, but it matters for long-dated options such as LEAPS.

How the Greeks change together

Greek At the money Deep in the money Far out of the money As expiry approaches (ATM)
Delta About ±0.50 Near ±1.00 Near 0 Stays near ±0.50, but becomes unstable
Gamma Highest Low Low Rises sharply
Theta Largest decay Smaller Smaller Decay accelerates
Vega Highest Lower Lower Falls

Position Greeks

Position Greeks are the sum of each Greek across every option and stock position, multiplied by the number of contracts and the multiplier. Stock has a delta of 1 per share and no gamma, theta or vega.

Worked example: a small portfolio

A trader holds:

  • Long 5 of the reference calls (delta 0.52, gamma 0.046, theta -0.06, vega 0.11)
  • Short 200 shares of the same stock
Greek Calls: 5 x 100 x Greek Stock Position total
Delta 5 x 100 x 0.52 = +260 -200 +60 shares
Gamma 5 x 100 x 0.046 = +23 0 +23 per $1
Theta 5 x 100 x -0.06 = -$30 0 -$30 per day
Vega 5 x 100 x 0.11 = +$55 0 +$55 per vol point

Reading this: the position behaves like being long 60 shares right now. If the stock rises $1, the delta rises to about +83. The position loses about $30 a day if nothing happens, and gains about $55 for each 1-point rise in implied volatility. This is a long-gamma, long-vega position that pays theta for the privilege. Most broker platforms show position Greeks; checking them before and after each trade is a good habit.

Common mistakes

  • Treating delta as fixed. Delta changes with price, time and volatility. A position that is neutral now may not be after a move.
  • Ignoring theta on long options. A correct directional view can still lose if the move comes too late.
  • Forgetting vega around events. Buying options when implied volatility is high, then watching it collapse, is a common way to lose money on a correct call.
  • Underestimating short gamma. Selling options near expiry collects fast theta, but a sharp move can create large losses quickly.
  • Trusting model Greeks blindly. The Greeks come from models with assumptions. Large moves, gaps and volatility jumps can make them inaccurate exactly when they matter most.

FAQ

What is a good delta for buying options?

There is no single best delta. Higher-delta, in-the-money options behave more like stock and decay less in percentage terms, while lower-delta, out-of-the-money options are cheaper but less likely to finish in the money.

Why does theta increase near expiration?

Extrinsic value must reach zero at expiration. For at-the-money options, the remaining time value is lost over fewer and fewer days, so the daily decay grows larger as expiry approaches.

What does negative gamma mean?

Negative gamma, which comes from selling options, means your delta moves against you as the underlying moves: you get longer as the price falls and shorter as it rises. Losses can accelerate on large moves.

Which Greek matters most?

It depends on the strategy. Directional traders watch delta, short-dated traders watch gamma and theta, and traders around earnings or other events watch vega most closely.
