Implied Volatility

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Implied volatility (IV) is the level of future volatility that the market is pricing into an option, worked backward from the option's price using a pricing model. It is expressed as an annualized percentage and is the main reason two options with the same strike and expiry can cost very different amounts at different times. Because IV rises before uncertain events and falls after them, it often decides whether an option trade makes money as much as the direction of the underlying does.

Key takeaways

  • Implied volatility is forward-looking and comes from option prices; historical volatility is backward-looking and comes from past price moves.
  • IV rank and IV percentile show whether today's IV is high or low compared with its own past range.
  • IV tends to rise before scheduled events such as earnings and to collapse afterward, which is known as IV crush.
  • Different strikes trade at different IVs, creating a volatility skew or smile that reflects demand for protection and the shape of expected moves.
  • The expected move formula converts IV into a rough one-standard-deviation price range for a given period.

Historical vs implied volatility

Historical volatility (HV), also called realized volatility, measures how much the price actually moved over a past window. It is usually calculated as the standard deviation of daily returns, annualized.

Implied volatility is the volatility figure that, plugged into an option pricing model, gives the option's current market price. It is the market's consensus forecast, plus whatever premium sellers demand for taking on risk.

Worked example: annualizing historical volatility

Suppose a stock's daily returns over the past month have a standard deviation of 1.5 percent. There are roughly 252 trading days a year, and volatility scales with the square root of time.

  • Square root of 252 = about 15.87
  • Annualized HV = 1.5% x 15.87 = about 23.8 percent

If the stock's options are priced at an IV of 35 percent, the market expects larger moves ahead than the stock has recently delivered, perhaps because an event is coming. Historically, index option IV has tended to sit above subsequently realized volatility on average. This gap, called the volatility risk premium, is one reason option selling strategies have earned money on average over long periods, while occasionally suffering large losses.

Black-Scholes intuition

The Black-Scholes model, published by Fischer Black and Myron Scholes in 1973 with related work by Robert Merton, prices a European option from six inputs:

  1. Underlying price
  2. Strike price
  3. Time to expiration
  4. Interest rate
  5. Dividends
  6. Volatility

The first five are known. Volatility is not. So instead of using the model to find a price, traders take the market price and solve for the volatility that makes the model match it. That number is implied volatility.

The model assumes, among other things, that volatility is constant and that returns follow a smooth, bell-shaped distribution. Real markets have jumps, crashes and changing volatility, which is why options at different strikes end up with different IVs. IV is best thought of as a common unit for quoting option prices, not as a perfect forecast.

Higher IV means a higher option price, for both calls and puts. How much the price changes for each point of IV is measured by vega, one of the options Greeks.

IV rank and IV percentile

A stock with IV of 40 percent might be cheap or expensive depending on what is normal for that stock. Two common measures put IV in context, usually over the past year.

  • IV rank = (current IV - 52-week low IV) / (52-week high IV - 52-week low IV)
  • IV percentile = the percentage of trading days in the past year on which IV was below its current level

Worked example

A stock's IV has ranged from 20 percent to 60 percent over the past year, and it is now 30 percent.

  • IV rank = (30 - 20) / (60 - 20) = 10 / 40 = 25 percent.
  • If IV was below 30 percent on 160 of the last 252 trading days, IV percentile = 160 / 252 = about 63 percent.

The two can differ a lot. A single spike to 60 percent drags IV rank down for a year, while IV percentile is less affected by one outlier. Many traders prefer selling premium when these readings are high and buying it when they are low, though high IV is often high for a good reason.

The expected move formula

IV can be turned into a rough price range. The one-standard-deviation expected move over a period is:

Expected move = Price x IV x square root of (days to expiry / 365)

Calendar days and 365 are a common convention; some traders use trading days and 252 instead.

Worked example

A stock trades at $200 with an IV of 40 percent, and you want the expected move over the next 7 days.

  • Days fraction: 7 / 365 = 0.01918
  • Square root: about 0.1385
  • Expected move = $200 x 0.40 x 0.1385 = about $11.08

The market is implying that a move of about $11, or 5.5 percent, in either direction over the week is roughly a one-standard-deviation outcome. Under the model's bell-curve assumption, the stock would finish within that range about 68 percent of the time. Real return distributions have fatter tails, so larger moves happen more often than the bell curve suggests.

A quick market-based alternative is the price of the at-the-money straddle (a call plus a put at the same strike), which approximates the expected size of the move to expiration. See straddles and strangles and earnings trading.

IV crush

Implied volatility often climbs ahead of known events, such as earnings, product launches, drug trial results or central bank meetings, because a big move is possible. Once the news is out, that uncertainty disappears and IV can fall sharply within minutes. This drop is called IV crush.

Worked example: right direction, still a loss

A stock trades at $50 the day before earnings. A $50 call expiring later that week costs $2.10, almost all extrinsic value, because IV is elevated at 90 percent. After the report, the stock rises to $51.50. IV collapses to 35 percent.

  • The call now has $1.50 of intrinsic value plus, say, $0.25 of remaining extrinsic value: about $1.75.
  • Loss = $2.10 - $1.75 = $0.35 per share, or $35 per contract.

The trader correctly predicted the direction, but the move was smaller than the option price implied, and the drop in IV erased the gain. Buyers of options before events need the move to exceed what is already priced in. Sellers benefit from the crush but take the risk that the move is far larger than expected.

Volatility skew and smile

If Black-Scholes were exactly right, every strike on the same expiry would have the same IV. In practice, plotting IV against strike usually gives a curve.

Shape Description Where it is common
Put skew (smirk) Lower strikes have higher IV than higher strikes Stock indices and most large stocks
Smile Both far OTM puts and calls have higher IV than ATM Currencies, some single stocks, crypto at times
Call skew Higher strikes have higher IV Some commodities, and stocks with takeover or squeeze potential

Equity index put skew became pronounced after the October 1987 crash. It reflects steady demand from investors buying puts as portfolio insurance, and the tendency of markets to fall faster than they rise. As a result, OTM puts on indices usually cost more in IV terms than equally distant OTM calls.

IV also varies by expiry. The term structure of IV normally slopes upward in calm markets and can invert in a crisis, when near-term IV jumps above longer-term IV. The VIX index measures 30-day implied volatility on the S&P 500; see volatility and VIX.

Common mistakes

  • Buying options just before earnings without checking IV. The price may already reflect a large move.
  • Selling options only because IV is high. IV is often high because a big move is likely. High premium is compensation for real risk.
  • Comparing IV across different stocks without context. A 50 percent IV may be normal for a small biotech and extreme for a utility. Use IV rank or percentile.
  • Treating the expected move as a boundary. It is a one-standard-deviation estimate, and moves beyond it happen regularly.
  • Ignoring skew when choosing strikes. OTM index puts are relatively expensive; OTM calls relatively cheap.

FAQ

Is high implied volatility good or bad?

Neither in itself. High IV makes options more expensive, which tends to favor sellers and hurt buyers, but it usually signals that the market expects bigger moves.

What causes IV crush?

IV crush happens when an expected event, such as an earnings report, passes and the uncertainty it carried disappears. Option prices drop as IV falls, even if the stock moves.

How do you calculate the expected move from implied volatility?

Multiply the stock price by IV and by the square root of the number of days to expiry divided by 365. For a $200 stock with 40 percent IV, the 7-day expected move is about $11.

What is the difference between IV rank and IV percentile?

IV rank shows where current IV sits between its 52-week low and high. IV percentile shows the share of days in the past year when IV was lower than it is now.
