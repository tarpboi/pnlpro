Straddles and Strangles

6 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

A straddle is the purchase or sale of a call and a put with the same strike and expiry; a strangle does the same with an out-of-the-money call and an out-of-the-money put at different strikes. Long versions profit if the underlying moves a lot in either direction, and short versions profit if it stays quiet. Both are ways to trade the size of a move rather than its direction, which makes the price of volatility, not the direction of the stock, the key question.

Key takeaways

  • A long straddle or strangle profits from a move larger than the total premium paid, in either direction.
  • A short straddle or strangle collects premium and profits if the underlying stays within the breakevens, but faces large or unlimited losses on big moves.
  • The at-the-money straddle price is a good market estimate of the expected move to expiration.
  • Buying straddles before earnings works only if the actual move exceeds what was priced in, after accounting for the drop in implied volatility.
  • Short-volatility strategies tend to produce many small gains and occasional very large losses.

Long straddle

Worked example

A stock trades at $100. You buy the $100 call for $4.00 and the $100 put for $3.80, both expiring in 30 days. Each contract covers 100 shares.

  • Total cost: $4.00 + $3.80 = $7.80, or $780.
  • Upper breakeven: $100 + $7.80 = $107.80.
  • Lower breakeven: $100 - $7.80 = $92.20.
  • Maximum loss: $780, if the stock finishes exactly at $100.

The stock must move more than 7.8 percent in either direction by expiry just to break even. Before expiry, the position is also affected by time decay, which works steadily against it, and by changes in implied volatility.

Long strangle

Worked example

With the stock at $100, buy the $105 call for $2.00 and the $95 put for $1.80.

  • Total cost: $3.80, or $380.
  • Upper breakeven: $105 + $3.80 = $108.80.
  • Lower breakeven: $95 - $3.80 = $91.20.
  • Maximum loss: $380, anywhere between $95 and $105 at expiry.

The strangle costs less than half as much as the straddle, but its breakevens are further away: $91.20 and $108.80 versus $92.20 and $107.80. It loses its full premium across a wide $10 range rather than at a single point.

Breakeven vs implied move

The at-the-money straddle price is a quick, market-based estimate of how far the stock is expected to move by expiration. In the example above, the market is pricing a move of about $7.80, or 7.8 percent.

This leads to the central question for any straddle buyer: will the stock move more than the market expects? If the straddle costs $7.80 and the stock moves $6, the buyer loses even though the stock moved a lot. A long straddle is a bet that realized volatility will exceed implied volatility. A short straddle is the opposite bet. The relationship between implied volatility and the expected move is covered in implied volatility.

Earnings straddles

Straddles are widely used around earnings announcements, where a large overnight move is common.

Worked example

Before an earnings report, a $50 stock has a $50 straddle expiring that week priced at $4.00, implying a move of about 8 percent. Implied volatility is elevated.

  • If the stock jumps to $56 (12 percent): the call is worth at least $6.00 of intrinsic value. Even after IV drops, the straddle is worth a little over $6.00, a gain of about $2.00 per share, or $200.
  • If the stock moves to $53 (6 percent): the call has $3.00 of intrinsic value. After IV collapses, little extrinsic value remains, so the straddle is worth only slightly more than $3.00, a loss of nearly $1.00 per share.

Option prices already reflect the market's estimate of the move, and actual moves scatter widely around it. Some reports produce moves several times the implied move, which hurts sellers badly, while many produce much less, which hurts buyers. There is no reliable way to know in advance which earnings will produce outsized moves. See earnings trading.

Short straddles and strangles

Selling a straddle or strangle collects premium and profits if the stock stays between the breakevens. Using the earlier straddle numbers, a short $100 straddle collects $780 and keeps all of it only if the stock finishes exactly at $100. It profits anywhere between $92.20 and $107.80.

The tail risk

Suppose the stock gaps to $75 on unexpected news. The put is worth $25.00, so the short straddle loses $25.00 - $7.80 = $17.20 per share, or $1,720. That single loss wipes out more than two full cycles of maximum premium. A gap to $130 would cost $30.00 - $7.80 = $22.20, or $2,220, and on the upside there is no theoretical limit.

Short straddles and strangles have negative gamma and negative vega: losses accelerate as the stock moves, and rising implied volatility increases the cost of buying the position back. See options Greeks. Selling uncovered options also requires a high options approval level and substantial margin, and brokers can raise margin requirements suddenly when markets become volatile.

Several well-known blowups of individuals and funds came from selling volatility, which often looks like steady income until a sharp market move. The February 2018 collapse of some short-volatility products is one example; see volatility and VIX. Traders who want short-volatility exposure with capped risk often use iron condors and butterflies instead, which add protective wings.

Common mistakes

  • Buying straddles because "something big is coming". The market usually knows, and the price already reflects it.
  • Ignoring IV crush. After an event, the drop in implied volatility can wipe out gains from a moderate move.
  • Holding long straddles too long. Time decay accelerates near expiry, and a move that comes too late may not help.
  • Selling strangles without a loss plan. Undefined risk combined with leverage can cause losses many times the premium collected.
  • Sizing short volatility on typical days. Size for the rare gap move, not the average week.

FAQ

What is the difference between a straddle and a strangle?

A straddle uses a call and a put at the same strike, usually at the money. A strangle uses an out-of-the-money call and an out-of-the-money put at different strikes, so it is cheaper but needs a bigger move.

How do you calculate the breakeven on a straddle?

Add the total premium to the strike for the upper breakeven and subtract it from the strike for the lower breakeven. A $100 straddle bought for $7.80 breaks even at $92.20 and $107.80 at expiry.

Is a long straddle a good earnings strategy?

It profits only if the stock moves more than the straddle price implied, and IV typically falls sharply after the report. Many earnings straddles lose money even when the stock moves noticeably.

Why are short strangles risky?

They collect limited premium but face very large losses if the stock moves sharply, and the call side has theoretically unlimited risk. Losses can be many times the premium received.
