Covered Calls and Cash-Secured Puts

6 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

A covered call is a strategy in which you own 100 shares of a stock and sell a call option against them, collecting premium in exchange for agreeing to sell the shares at the strike price. A cash-secured put is the mirror strategy: you sell a put and set aside enough cash to buy 100 shares at the strike if assigned. Both are popular as "income" strategies, and both are among the first options strategies brokers approve, but the premium comes with real trade-offs that are easy to overlook.

Key takeaways

  • A covered call adds premium income to a stock position but caps the upside at the strike price, while leaving almost all of the downside.
  • A cash-secured put pays you to wait to buy a stock at a lower price, but you must buy it at the strike even if it has fallen far below.
  • At expiration, a covered call and a cash-secured put at the same strike and expiry have very similar payoff shapes.
  • The wheel strategy cycles between selling puts and selling calls on the same stock, and it works only as well as the stock you choose.
  • Neither strategy protects meaningfully against a large decline; the premium is a small cushion, not insurance.

How a covered call works

You own 100 shares. You sell one call, usually out of the money, and receive the premium. Three outcomes are possible at expiration:

  1. Stock below the strike: the call expires worthless. You keep the shares and the premium.
  2. Stock above the strike: the call is exercised. You sell your shares at the strike and keep the premium.
  3. Before expiry: you can buy the call back to close it, often to roll it to a later date or different strike.

Worked example: covered call

You buy 100 shares of a stock at $50.00, costing $5,000. You sell one 30-day call with a $55 strike for $1.20, receiving $120.

  • Maximum profit: (strike - stock cost) + premium = ($55 - $50) + $1.20 = $6.20 per share, or $620. This happens at any price of $55 or above at expiry.
  • Breakeven: stock cost - premium = $50.00 - $1.20 = $48.80.
  • Maximum loss: if the stock goes to zero, $48.80 per share, or $4,880.
Stock price at expiry Stock P&L Call P&L Total
$40 -$1,000 +$120 -$880
$48.80 -$120 +$120 $0
$50 $0 +$120 +$120
$55 +$500 +$120 +$620
$60 +$1,000 -$380 +$620
$70 +$2,000 -$1,380 +$620

At $60, the call is worth $5.00 at expiry, so the short call loses $500 - $120 = $380. At $70, it loses $1,500 - $120 = $1,380. Total profit stays at $620 no matter how high the stock goes. Without the call, the stock alone would have made $2,000 at $70. That forgone gain is the true cost of a covered call. At $40, the premium reduced the loss from $1,000 to $880, a modest cushion.

How a cash-secured put works

You sell one put and hold cash equal to the strike times 100 in your account. Possible outcomes:

  1. Stock above the strike at expiry: the put expires worthless and you keep the premium.
  2. Stock below the strike: you are assigned and buy 100 shares at the strike. Your effective cost is the strike minus the premium.

Worked example: cash-secured put

A stock trades at $50.00. You sell one 30-day put with a $45 strike for $1.00, receiving $100. You set aside $4,500 in cash.

  • Maximum profit: $100, if the stock is at or above $45 at expiry.
  • Return on collateral: $100 / $4,500 = about 2.2 percent for the month, if the put expires worthless.
  • Breakeven: strike - premium = $45.00 - $1.00 = $44.00.
  • Maximum loss: if the stock goes to zero, $44.00 per share, or $4,400.

If the stock falls to $38 and you are assigned, you buy 100 shares at $45. Your effective cost is $44, so you have an unrealized loss of ($44 - $38) x 100 = $600. You could have bought the stock at $38 if you had simply waited without selling the put. If the stock rallies to $65, you keep $100 while someone who bought shares at $50 made $1,500.

Covered calls and short puts are close cousins

At expiry, owning 100 shares and selling a call at a given strike has nearly the same payoff as selling a put at that same strike and expiry. Both make a fixed maximum above the strike and lose roughly dollar for dollar below it. This relationship comes from put-call parity. The differences in practice are dividends (the share owner receives them), the capital used, how assignment happens, and account permissions. See options basics.

The wheel strategy

The wheel combines the two:

  1. Sell cash-secured puts on a stock you are willing to own.
  2. If assigned, you now own 100 shares. Sell covered calls against them.
  3. If the shares are called away, you are back to cash. Return to step 1.

Each step collects premium. Supporters like its rules-based routine. Its weaknesses follow from the payoffs above:

  • In a strong rally, you keep making small premiums while the stock runs away, and you may be called away near the bottom of the move.
  • In a steep decline, you are assigned at a price well above the market and then face a choice: sell calls at strikes below your cost, which risks locking in a loss, or sell calls far above the current price for tiny premiums.
  • Stock selection matters most. The wheel on a weak stock is a slow way to own a falling asset. It works best, if at all, on stocks you would be content to hold for a long time.

Choosing strikes and expiries

Choice Effect
Strike closer to the current price More premium, higher chance of assignment, less room for upside (calls) or downside (puts)
Strike further away Less premium, lower chance of assignment
Short expiries (one to six weeks) Faster time decay per day; more decisions and transaction costs
Longer expiries More total premium, slower decay, longer commitment
Higher implied volatility Higher premium, but usually because larger moves are expected

Many traders use the option's delta as a rough guide; a 0.20 to 0.30 delta short option is often read as having roughly a 20 to 30 percent chance of finishing in the money. See options Greeks and implied volatility.

Assignment risk

Because US equity options are American-style, a short call or put can be assigned at any time before expiry, not just at expiration. Early assignment of a covered call is most likely when the call is deep in the money with little extrinsic value left, especially the day before the stock goes ex-dividend. If the shares are called away early, you lose the upcoming dividend. Assignment mechanics are covered in options expiration and assignment.

Common mistakes

  • Calling premium "free money". The premium pays you for giving up upside or for agreeing to buy into a decline.
  • Selling calls on a stock you can't bear to sell. If it rallies past the strike, the shares may be called away.
  • Selling puts on a stock you don't want to own. Choose based on the stock first and the premium second.
  • Chasing high premiums. Unusually rich premiums usually mean the market expects a big move, such as around earnings or a biotech trial result.
  • Selling calls below your cost basis after a decline without a plan. If the stock rebounds, you can lock in a realized loss.
  • Overusing margin. A "cash-secured" put sold on margin is no longer cash-secured, and losses scale up accordingly.

FAQ

Are covered calls a safe strategy?

Covered calls are lower-risk than owning the stock alone only by the amount of premium collected. You still carry nearly all the downside of the shares, and you give up gains above the strike.

What happens if my cash-secured put is assigned?

You buy 100 shares per contract at the strike price, using the cash you set aside. Your effective cost basis is the strike minus the premium received.

What is the wheel strategy in options?

The wheel is a cycle of selling cash-secured puts until assigned, then selling covered calls on the shares until they are called away, then starting again. Its results depend mainly on the stock and on how much the price moves.

Can I lose money with covered calls?

Yes. If the stock falls by more than the premium received, the position loses money, and the loss can be large if the stock falls sharply.
