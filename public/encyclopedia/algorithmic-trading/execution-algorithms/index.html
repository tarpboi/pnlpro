Execution Algorithms

Execution algorithms are programs that break a large order into smaller pieces and send them to the market over time, aiming to buy or sell at a good average price without moving the market too much. They answer the question "how should I trade?" rather than "what should I trade?". Even a strategy with a genuine edge can lose money through poor execution, which makes this an important topic for any systematic trader, large or small.

Key takeaways

  • Large orders move prices; splitting them over time reduces market impact but increases exposure to price drift.
  • TWAP spreads an order evenly over time, VWAP follows the typical volume pattern, and POV trades a fixed share of live volume.
  • Implementation shortfall algorithms balance impact against the risk of the price moving away while you wait.
  • Smart order routing sends each piece to the venue with the best available price and liquidity.
  • Measure execution quality against the price at the moment you decided to trade, not only against the day's average price.

Why execution matters

Every trade pays costs beyond commissions:

  • The spread: crossing from bid to ask costs half the bid-ask spread on each side, compared with the mid price.
  • Market impact: a large order consumes available liquidity and pushes the price. Some of that move is temporary, recovering once you stop trading; some is permanent, because your trading reveals information.
  • Timing risk: if you trade slowly to reduce impact, the price may move away from you for unrelated reasons.

Execution design is the trade-off between impact (trade slowly) and timing risk (trade quickly). The classic formal treatment is the Almgren-Chriss framework from around 2000, which finds the trading schedule that minimizes expected cost plus a penalty for cost uncertainty.

A widely cited empirical rule of thumb, the square-root law, says impact grows roughly with the square root of order size relative to daily volume:

Impact ≈ c × daily volatility × √(order size / average daily volume)

where c is a constant usually estimated to be around 1. Treat it as a rough guide, not a precise forecast.

The main algorithm types

Algorithm How it trades Strengths Weaknesses
TWAP (time-weighted average price) Equal slices at regular intervals over a set period Simple, predictable, easy to build Ignores volume patterns; regular slices can be detected
VWAP (volume-weighted average price) Slices sized to match the historical intraday volume curve Trades more when liquidity is high; matches a common benchmark Relies on historical volume; still fixed schedule
POV (percentage of volume) Trades a fixed share, such as 10%, of real-time market volume Adapts to actual liquidity Unknown finish time; can chase volume spikes
Implementation shortfall (arrival price) Trades faster at first, adjusting for urgency and risk Minimizes cost relative to the decision price More complex; more impact early
Liquidity-seeking Searches lit and hidden venues for size, often opportunistically Good for illiquid names Information leakage risk

TWAP

TWAP divides the order into equal pieces sent at fixed intervals, such as 1,000 shares every five minutes. Many crypto exchanges offer TWAP orders directly. Good implementations randomize slice sizes and timing slightly so other traders cannot easily detect the pattern.

import random

def twap_schedule(total_qty, start_ts, end_ts, n_slices, jitter=0.2):
    """Split total_qty into n_slices spread evenly between two timestamps (seconds)."""
    step = (end_ts - start_ts) / n_slices
    base, remainder = divmod(total_qty, n_slices)
    schedule = []
    for i in range(n_slices):
        qty = base + (1 if i < remainder else 0)
        offset = random.uniform(0.0, jitter) * step   # avoid a perfectly regular pattern
        schedule.append((start_ts + i * step + offset, qty))
    return schedule

VWAP

Trading volume in most stock markets follows a U-shape through the day, heavy at the open and close and lighter at midday. A VWAP algorithm uses historical volume profiles to trade more when the market is busiest. The volume-weighted average price itself, explained in VWAP, is also the most common benchmark for judging fills.

POV

A POV or participation algorithm watches live volume and trades a set fraction of it. If the market trades 50,000 shares in a minute and the participation rate is 10%, the algorithm aims to trade 5,000. It adapts to real conditions but cannot promise when the order will finish.

Implementation shortfall

The concept of implementation shortfall, introduced by André Perold in 1988, measures the difference between the return of a paper portfolio traded instantly at the decision price and the return actually achieved. Shortfall algorithms try to minimize that gap, usually by trading more aggressively early to reduce exposure to drift.

Smart order routing

Many markets are fragmented across several exchanges and alternative venues. A smart order router (SOR) checks prices and available size across venues and sends each child order to the best one, considering fees, rebates, fill probability and latency. In crypto, aggregators perform a similar role across exchanges or DEX liquidity pools. In the US, rules such as Regulation NMS require brokers to avoid trading through better displayed prices on other exchanges; other regions have their own best-execution rules.

Measuring slippage

You cannot improve execution you do not measure. Common benchmarks:

Benchmark Definition Notes
Arrival price Mid price when the order was sent Most honest measure of total execution cost
Decision price Price when the strategy generated the signal Captures delay between signal and order
Interval VWAP Market VWAP during the order's lifetime Popular, but can be "beaten" simply by trading in a favorable direction
Close Official closing price Relevant for strategies benchmarked to the close

Costs are usually expressed in basis points (bps), where 1 bp = 0.01%. For a buy order, slippage = (average fill price − benchmark) / benchmark. For a sell order, reverse the sign.

Worked example: implementation shortfall

A strategy decides to buy 10,000 shares when the price is 50.00 dollars. The paper portfolio value is 10,000 × 50.00 = 500,000 dollars.

  • The algorithm buys 8,000 shares at an average of 50.12 dollars. The remaining 2,000 shares are not filled before the price runs to 50.60 dollars, and the order is cancelled.
  • Execution cost: 8,000 × (50.12 − 50.00) = 960 dollars.
  • Opportunity cost on unfilled shares: 2,000 × (50.60 − 50.00) = 1,200 dollars.
  • Commissions: 40 dollars.
  • Total shortfall: 960 + 1,200 + 40 = 2,200 dollars, or 2,200 / 500,000 = 0.44% (44 bps).

The order looked well executed if judged only on the 8,000 shares filled, but missing the remaining shares cost more than the slippage itself.

Estimating impact

A stock has daily volatility of 2% and an average daily volume of 1,000,000 shares. An order for 10,000 shares is 1% of daily volume. The square-root rule with c = 1 suggests impact of about 2% × √0.01 = 2% × 0.1 = 0.2%, or 20 bps. Quadrupling the order to 40,000 shares only doubles the estimated impact, to about 40 bps, because √0.04 = 0.2.

Execution for retail and systematic traders

Retail orders are usually small relative to market volume, so impact is minor in liquid markets. Execution still matters:

  • Use limit orders where appropriate instead of market orders; order types explains the choices.
  • Avoid trading in the first and last minutes of a session and around major news, when spreads widen.
  • In thin markets such as small-cap stocks, altcoins or event contracts, a few thousand dollars can move the price; splitting orders helps.
  • Log signal time, order time, fill price and the mid price at each step, then compare live costs with backtest assumptions. See trading costs and fees.

Common mistakes

  • Measuring only against VWAP. It can hide the cost of delays and missed fills.
  • Ignoring unfilled orders. Missed trades on winners are a real, often large cost.
  • Predictable slicing. Identical slices at identical intervals can be detected and traded against.
  • Using market orders in thin books. One order can walk through several price levels.
  • Assuming backtest fills equal live fills. Always reconcile real execution against assumptions.

FAQ

What is the difference between TWAP and VWAP?

TWAP spreads an order evenly across time, while VWAP sizes each slice according to the typical volume at that time of day. VWAP usually trades more heavily near the open and close, when most stock market volume occurs.

What is implementation shortfall?

It is the difference between the result of an ideal trade executed instantly at the decision price and the actual result, including slippage, fees and the cost of any shares that were never filled. It is considered one of the most complete measures of trading cost.

Do retail traders need execution algorithms?

Most retail orders are small enough that a simple limit order works fine in liquid markets. Execution tactics such as splitting orders and avoiding volatile moments matter more in thin markets and for traders whose strategies trade often.

What is smart order routing?

Smart order routing is software that sends each order, or each piece of an order, to the trading venue offering the best combination of price, size, fees and likelihood of filling.
