High-Frequency Trading

6 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

High-frequency trading (HFT) is a form of algorithmic trading that uses very fast computers, specialized networks and servers located next to exchanges to trade in tiny time intervals, often holding positions for seconds or less. HFT firms supply a large share of the quotes in many electronic markets, mostly as market makers and arbitrageurs. Their speed advantage is measured in microseconds, which is why retail traders cannot compete with them on speed and should not try.

Key takeaways

  • HFT is defined by speed, high order volumes and very short holding periods, not by one particular strategy.
  • The main HFT activities are market making, latency arbitrage between venues and very short-term statistical strategies.
  • Firms pay for co-location, direct data feeds and fast networks because being first is often the whole edge.
  • Research generally links algorithmic and high-frequency trading to narrower spreads in normal conditions, but critics point to fragile liquidity and unequal access.
  • A retail trader's connection is thousands of times slower than a co-located server, so retail edges have to come from somewhere other than speed.

What counts as HFT

There is no single global legal definition. The EU's MiFID II framework, for example, describes a high-frequency algorithmic trading technique in terms of infrastructure built to minimize latency (such as co-location or proximity hosting), systems that decide on orders without human intervention, and high intraday message rates. In practice, HFT firms share several features:

  • Holding periods from microseconds to minutes, usually ending the day with little or no inventory.
  • Very high numbers of orders, most of which are cancelled before they fill.
  • Small profit per trade, multiplied by enormous volume.
  • Heavy investment in hardware, software and connectivity.

What HFT firms do

Electronic market making

The largest HFT activity is market making: posting bids and offers continuously across thousands of instruments. Speed matters because a market maker must update its quotes the instant the fair price changes, before faster traders trade against stale prices. Many firms that act as designated market makers or liquidity providers on exchanges are HFT firms.

Latency arbitrage

When the same or related instruments trade on several venues, prices update at slightly different times. A firm that sees a price move on one venue first can trade on another venue before its quotes adjust. Examples include trading a stock on several exchanges against each other, or trading stocks and ETFs against index futures in a different city. This is a fast version of the cross-venue and index trades in arbitrage strategies.

Short-term statistical strategies

Some firms predict price moves over the next few seconds using patterns in the order book, such as imbalances between bid and ask size, or trading flows in related instruments.

Event and news trading

Machine-readable news feeds and economic data releases can be parsed and traded in microseconds. Speed determines who trades at the old price.

Co-location and the latency arms race

Signals in optical fiber travel at about two thirds of the speed of light in a vacuum, roughly 200 kilometers per millisecond. Every kilometer of distance adds delay, so HFT firms pay exchanges to place their servers in the same data center as the matching engine. This is called co-location. Exchanges typically equalize cable lengths inside the data center so that co-located clients receive the same delay.

Between distant markets, firms compete on network routes. Around 2010, a dedicated fiber line between the Chicago futures markets and New Jersey data centers shaved milliseconds off the previous routes. Microwave and later millimeter-wave radio networks followed, because radio signals travel through air faster than light through glass and can take straighter paths. Inside their own systems, firms use specialized hardware such as FPGAs (programmable chips) to react in nanoseconds.

Economists Budish, Cramton and Shim, in a 2015 paper, argued that continuous trading itself creates this arms race, because tiny speed differences decide who captures each stale quote. They proposed frequent batch auctions, which would match orders at short fixed intervals instead of continuously, as an alternative market design.

Controversies

  • The 2010 Flash Crash. On May 6, 2010, US stock indices fell sharply and recovered within minutes. Official reports described a large automated sell order in index futures as a trigger, with liquidity withdrawing rapidly as HFT and other participants pulled back.
  • "Phantom" liquidity. Quotes can vanish as soon as large orders approach, so displayed depth may overstate what can actually be traded.
  • Unequal access. Critics argue that paying for speed lets some firms trade ahead of slower investors. Michael Lewis's 2014 book Flash Boys brought the debate to a wide audience. The exchange IEX, launched around that time, added a small intentional delay, often called a speed bump, to blunt certain latency strategies.
  • Manipulation. Spoofing, placing orders intended to be cancelled to mislead others, is illegal in major markets and has led to criminal prosecutions. It is not a normal HFT strategy, but the speed and volume of HFT make monitoring harder.
  • Benefits. Academic studies, such as Hendershott, Jones and Menkveld's 2011 work on algorithmic trading in US stocks, generally found that more automated trading coincided with narrower spreads and more informative prices in normal conditions. Retail investors in liquid stocks pay much smaller spreads than they did before electronic trading.

Regulators in many jurisdictions now require algorithmic traders to have pre-trade risk controls, kill switches, testing and record-keeping. Details differ by country and change over time.

Why retail traders cannot compete on speed

Factor Co-located HFT firm Typical retail trader
Distance to matching engine Meters Hundreds to thousands of kilometers
Reaction time Microseconds to nanoseconds Tens to hundreds of milliseconds, plus broker processing
Market data Direct exchange feeds Consolidated or broker feeds, sometimes delayed or throttled
Order routing Direct market access Through a broker's systems
Cost per trade Negotiated fees, rebates Retail fees and spreads

The practical conclusion is not that retail trading is pointless, but that retail strategies should not depend on being first. Holding periods of hours to months, niche markets and careful execution are realistic areas; reacting to headlines or arbitraging price gaps between major venues generally are not.

Worked example

Distance and delay

A trader in London sends orders to a US stock exchange's data center in New Jersey. The great-circle distance is roughly 5,570 kilometers.

  • Minimum one-way time in fiber ≈ 5,570 / 200 ≈ 28 milliseconds. Real cable routes are longer, and broker and internet processing add more.
  • A round trip (send order, receive confirmation) is therefore at least about 56 milliseconds.
  • A co-located server might react to the same market event in a few microseconds. One millisecond is 1,000 microseconds, so the retail order is thousands of times slower.

Small edges, large volume

Suppose an HFT market maker earns an average net profit of 0.1 cent (0.001 dollars) per share traded and trades 100 million shares a day across many stocks.

  • Daily profit = 0.001 × 100,000,000 = 100,000 dollars.
  • At 0.0005 dollars per share, profit halves to 50,000 dollars.

The numbers are illustrative, but they show why HFT firms fight over fractions of a cent and fractions of a microsecond. A business with that cost structure is not available to a trader paying retail fees.

Common mistakes

  • Trying to scalp news or arbitrage gaps between major venues from a home connection. By the time a retail order arrives, the opportunity has usually gone.
  • Paying for a "low-latency" VPS and expecting an edge. It can reduce delays and outages, but it does not make a retail setup competitive with co-location.
  • Blaming every loss on HFT. Most retail losses come from costs, leverage and poor strategies.
  • Trusting displayed depth. Quotes can be cancelled instantly; size your orders to the liquidity that actually trades. Data feed options are covered in market data.

FAQ

Is high-frequency trading legal?

Yes, HFT is legal in major markets, subject to rules on risk controls, testing and market conduct. Manipulative practices such as spoofing and layering are illegal whether or not they are carried out at high speed.

Does high-frequency trading hurt retail traders?

Evidence is mixed. Studies generally find that automated market making has narrowed spreads for ordinary investors in liquid markets, while critics point to vanishing liquidity during stress and an uneven playing field for speed-sensitive strategies.

How fast is high-frequency trading?

Leading firms measure their reaction times in microseconds, and the fastest hardware-based systems in nanoseconds. A typical retail order takes tens of milliseconds or more just to travel across the internet.

Can I do high-frequency trading from home?

Not in the professional sense. True HFT requires co-location, direct exchange feeds and specialized hardware that cost far more than any retail account could justify, so home traders should design strategies that do not depend on speed.
