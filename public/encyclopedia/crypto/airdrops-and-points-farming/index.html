Airdrops and Points Farming

An airdrop is a free distribution of a new token to wallets that meet certain criteria, usually past users of a protocol. Points programs are a newer variant in which projects award points for activity, then later convert them into tokens or other rewards. Some airdrops have been worth thousands of dollars per wallet, which created a whole activity, "farming," aimed at qualifying. Results are highly uneven, costs are often underestimated, and fake airdrops are one of the most common ways traders lose funds.

Key takeaways

  • Airdrops reward past or ongoing activity, and the criteria and amounts are usually unknown until the project announces them.
  • Points programs make rewards explicit but leave their conversion into tokens uncertain and at the project's discretion.
  • Projects use sybil filters to exclude people running many wallets, and farmers can spend significant fees and capital for nothing.
  • Newly airdropped tokens often face heavy selling at launch, so the value at announcement may not last.
  • In many countries, airdropped tokens are taxable when received, and any unsolicited "claim" link should be treated as a likely scam.

How airdrops work

A project takes a snapshot of blockchain activity at a chosen date, applies eligibility rules, and allocates tokens. Eligible users then claim through the project's official site, or receive tokens directly.

Typical criteria include:

  • Using the protocol before a cutoff date.
  • Trading volume, number of transactions or months of activity.
  • Providing liquidity or holding deposits for a period.
  • Bridging funds to a new network.
  • Holding a related token or NFT.
  • Community participation, such as governance votes.

One early, widely known example was Uniswap's UNI token in September 2020, which gave 400 UNI to every address that had used the protocol before a cutoff. Many later launches, including Arbitrum's ARB in March 2023, used tiered criteria to reward heavier users more.

The purpose, from the project's side, is to decentralize ownership, reward early users and attract attention. The airdrop is also part of the token's tokenomics, usually a modest share of total supply, with insiders and investors holding much more.

Points programs

From around 2023, many projects began awarding points for activity: deposits, trades, referrals or time spent holding positions. Points are typically shown on a dashboard and convert to tokens at a later "season" end.

Points give users visible progress, but:

  • The conversion rate from points to tokens is usually not fixed in advance.
  • Projects can change rules, add multipliers or reduce rewards at any time.
  • Points are not property you own and generally cannot be redeemed if the project never launches a token.
  • Several seasons may run, delaying the payoff.

Some points programs paid out generously; others converted to tokens that quickly fell, or produced disappointing allocations after users had committed capital for months.

Sybil filtering

A sybil attack, in this context, is one person using many wallets to claim multiple allocations. Projects actively try to detect and exclude this. Common methods include analyzing funding sources (wallets funded from the same address), identical transaction patterns and timing, minimum activity thresholds and on-chain clustering. Some projects have also asked users to report sybil clusters.

Projects' terms often prohibit multi-wallet farming, and farmers who are flagged may receive nothing after months of effort. Running many accounts can also breach exchange or platform terms of service. Focus on genuine use of products you would use anyway.

The real costs of farming

Airdrop farming is not free. Costs include:

  • Gas and transaction fees across many interactions.
  • Bridging fees and the risk of bridge exploits.
  • Opportunity cost of capital locked in a protocol instead of earning a safer yield.
  • Smart contract risk in new, often unaudited protocols.
  • Impermanent loss if farming involves providing liquidity. See DeFi trading.
  • Time.

Worked example: farming math

A user deposits $10,000 into a new protocol for six months to earn points, and spends $25 a month in transaction fees and $60 in bridging fees.

  • Fees: 6 × $25 + $60 = $210.
  • Opportunity cost: if the same $10,000 could have earned 4 percent a year elsewhere, six months forgoes about $200.
  • Total cost: about $410, plus the risk of losing some or all of the $10,000 to an exploit.

If the eventual airdrop is worth $1,500 at the moment the user can sell, the net gain is about $1,090. If it is worth $200, or the user is filtered out, it is a loss. The outcome depends on criteria the user cannot see in advance, and on the token's price at launch.

Selling pressure at launch

When an airdropped token starts trading, many recipients sell immediately. Meanwhile, the token often launches with a small circulating supply and a high fully diluted valuation, with large investor and team unlocks to come. The result, in many cases, is a peak at or soon after launch followed by a decline.

For traders:

  • The value shown at the claim moment may not be available by the time you sell.
  • Exchange listings at launch add liquidity but also give sellers an exit. See altcoin trading.
  • Some recipients choose to sell a portion immediately to lock in value and hold the rest, which is a personal risk decision rather than a rule.

Tax note

Tax treatment varies by country and is not settled everywhere. In the US, the IRS treats airdropped tokens as ordinary income, valued at fair market value when you gain control of them, with a later sale producing a capital gain or loss. Several other countries take a similar approach; others tax only on disposal. Points themselves are usually not taxed until converted, but this too depends on local rules. Keep records of dates and values when you receive tokens. See the trading taxes overview and consult a professional for your situation.

Scams posing as airdrops

Fake airdrops are among the most common crypto scams:

  • Unsolicited tokens appear in your wallet with a website in their name. Visiting it and trying to claim or sell triggers a wallet drainer.
  • Impersonation of real projects through hacked or lookalike social media accounts announcing "claims" that lead to phishing sites.
  • Search ads for "[project] airdrop claim" that lead to fake sites.
  • "Eligibility checkers" that ask you to sign approvals or enter your seed phrase.

Defenses:

  • Only claim through links from a project's verified official channels, and check them against several sources.
  • Never enter your seed phrase anywhere to claim anything.
  • Read every signature request. A claim should not need an approval to spend your existing tokens.
  • Use a separate wallet for claiming if you are unsure.

See wallet security for approvals, drainers and recovery steps.

Common mistakes

  • Treating farming as guaranteed income. Allocations are discretionary and often disappointing.
  • Ignoring costs and opportunity cost.
  • Locking large amounts in unaudited protocols for points.
  • Clicking claim links from social media replies or DMs.
  • Forgetting tax records for tokens received.

FAQ

Are crypto airdrops free money?

Not really. They reward activity that usually costs fees, capital and time, criteria are unknown in advance, and token prices often fall after launch. Some users have earned significant amounts, while many earn little or nothing.

How do points programs work in crypto?

Projects award points for activity such as deposits or trades and later convert them into tokens or other rewards. The conversion terms are usually decided by the project and can change.

Are airdrops taxable?

In many countries, yes. In the US, airdropped tokens are generally taxed as ordinary income at their market value when received, with later gains or losses taxed on sale. Rules differ by country.

How can I tell if an airdrop is a scam?

Unsolicited tokens, urgent claim links, requests for your seed phrase and approvals to spend your existing tokens are strong warning signs. Use only links from a project's verified official channels.
