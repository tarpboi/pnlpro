Tokenomics and Token Unlocks

Tokenomics is the design of a crypto token's supply and distribution: how many tokens exist, who holds them, how new tokens are created and when locked tokens become tradable. For traders, tokenomics often matters as much as the project itself, because predictable waves of new supply from emissions and unlocks can weigh on price for months or years. Reading a token's supply schedule before buying is one of the simplest ways to avoid a common trap.

Key takeaways

  • Market cap uses circulating supply; fully diluted valuation (FDV) uses maximum or total supply, and the gap between them shows how much supply is still to come.
  • Vesting schedules release tokens to teams and investors over time, often after a cliff, and unlock dates are usually published in advance.
  • Large unlocks relative to circulating supply and daily volume have tended to coincide with price weakness, though markets often price them in early.
  • Emissions to stakers, miners or liquidity providers are a continuous form of dilution.
  • Buybacks and burns reduce supply only if they exceed new issuance, and their real size is often small.

Supply terms

Term Meaning
Circulating supply Tokens currently available to trade
Total supply Tokens that exist now, including locked ones, minus any burned
Maximum supply The most that can ever exist, if capped (Bitcoin: 21 million). Some tokens have no cap
Market cap Price × circulating supply
FDV (fully diluted valuation) Price × maximum supply (or total supply where there is no cap)
Float Circulating supply as a share of total supply

Market cap, unlike for stocks (see valuation ratios), depends on which supply figure a data site counts. Different data aggregators sometimes show different circulating supplies for the same token.

Worked example: market cap vs FDV

A token trades at $0.50. Circulating supply is 200 million and maximum supply is 1 billion.

  • Market cap = $0.50 × 200 million = $100 million.
  • FDV = $0.50 × 1 billion = $500 million.
  • Float = 200 / 1,000 = 20 percent.

For today's price to hold once every token is circulating, the market must absorb 800 million more tokens, worth $400 million at $0.50, and support a market cap of $500 million instead of $100 million. If demand does not grow, price has to fall to clear it. See altcoin trading for why low float and high FDV became a well-known warning sign.

Allocations

A typical token launch splits supply among groups such as:

  • Team and advisors, often 15 to 25 percent.
  • Investors (seed, private and strategic rounds), often 10 to 25 percent, bought at prices far below the public price.
  • Treasury or foundation, for future development.
  • Ecosystem and community incentives, including airdrops, liquidity mining and grants.
  • Public sale, if any.

The figures vary widely. What matters to traders is how much is held by insiders with a low cost basis and when they can sell. An investor who paid $0.02 per token is in profit at almost any market price.

Vesting and cliffs

Insider tokens are usually locked under a vesting schedule:

  • A cliff is an initial period with no unlocks, commonly 6 to 12 months after launch.
  • At the cliff, a portion may unlock at once.
  • The rest then vests linearly, a fixed amount each day, week or month.

Worked example: a team vesting schedule

A project has 1 billion tokens. The team holds 20 percent, or 200 million. The schedule is a 12-month cliff with 25 percent released at the cliff, and the remaining 75 percent released monthly over 36 months.

  • At month 12: 200 million × 25% = 50 million tokens unlock at once.
  • Then: 150 million / 36 = about 4.17 million tokens per month for three years.

If circulating supply was 200 million just before the cliff, the cliff alone adds 50 million tokens, increasing circulating supply by 25 percent in one day. At a price of $0.50, that is $25 million of tokens. If the token's genuine daily volume is $10 million, the unlock equals two and a half days of trading. Investor tranches often unlock on the same date, making the cliff larger.

Not every unlocked token is sold. Teams may hold, stake or use tokens for operations. But insiders with a very low cost basis have a strong incentive to sell at least some.

Unlock calendars

Unlock dates are usually published in project documentation, and data services aggregate them into calendars. Traders compare an unlock's size with:

  • Circulating supply. An unlock of 1 percent is minor; 10 percent or more is significant.
  • Daily trading volume. How many days of volume the unlock represents.
  • Recipients. Investor and team unlocks tend to matter more than ecosystem funds that release slowly.

How unlocks affect price

Studies by research firms and crypto analysts have generally found that tokens tend to weaken around large unlocks, often in the weeks before the date, as traders anticipate supply and some hedge by shorting perpetual futures. Effects vary: some unlocks pass quietly, and some prices rise after because the event was already priced in. Treat unlock analysis as one input, not a guaranteed short signal. Shorting into a widely expected unlock is a crowded trade, and funding rates can make it costly.

Emissions and inflation

Many tokens continuously issue new supply:

  • Proof-of-stake rewards paid to validators and stakers.
  • Mining rewards, as with Bitcoin, which follows a fixed halving schedule described in crypto market cycles.
  • Liquidity mining and incentive programs in DeFi.

If a token has 10 percent annual emissions and you do not receive them (for example, because you do not stake), your share of supply shrinks by roughly 9 percent a year (1 − 1/1.10). Staking yield paid in newly issued tokens is largely dilution being redistributed, not new value.

Buybacks and burns

Some projects use revenue to buy back tokens, or burn tokens (send them to an address no one controls) to reduce supply. Ethereum, for example, burns part of each transaction fee, so its net supply can rise or fall depending on network activity.

Questions to ask:

  • Net effect. Does the burn exceed new emissions and unlocks? A $1 million monthly buyback is small against a $25 million unlock.
  • Source. Is it funded by real revenue, or by the treasury selling other tokens?
  • Discretion. Can the team stop it at any time?
  • Legal structure. Revenue-linked buybacks have raised regulatory questions in some jurisdictions.

Common mistakes

  • Judging a token by its unit price. A $0.001 token is not "cheap" if supply is in the trillions.
  • Looking only at market cap and ignoring FDV and upcoming unlocks.
  • Assuming unlocked tokens will all be sold, or that none will be.
  • Treating staking yield as profit without accounting for dilution and price.
  • Trusting announced burns without checking net supply on-chain.

FAQ

What is the difference between market cap and FDV?

Market cap multiplies the price by circulating supply. FDV multiplies the price by maximum or total supply, showing the valuation if every token were in circulation.

Do token unlocks make the price go down?

Large unlocks have often coincided with price weakness, frequently before the unlock date as traders anticipate selling. The effect is not guaranteed, because it depends on the unlock's size, the recipients and how much is already priced in.

What is a vesting cliff?

A cliff is an initial lock-up period during which no tokens vest. When it ends, a portion of tokens often unlocks at once, followed by gradual monthly or daily releases.

Where can I find a token's unlock schedule?

Projects usually publish vesting details in their documentation, and several data services compile unlock calendars across many tokens. Cross-check the figures, because sources sometimes differ.
