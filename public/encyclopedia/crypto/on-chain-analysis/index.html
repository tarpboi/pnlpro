On-Chain Analysis

On-chain analysis is the study of data recorded on public blockchains, such as transfers, wallet balances and the price at which coins last moved, to understand how holders are behaving. Because every Bitcoin and Ethereum transaction is public, traders can watch coins flowing to exchanges, track large wallets and estimate the average cost basis of the market. It offers a view no stock market has, but the data needs careful interpretation and rarely gives precise timing signals.

Key takeaways

  • Blockchains are public ledgers, so flows between wallets and exchanges can be measured directly, though wallet ownership must be inferred.
  • Exchange inflows are often read as potential selling, and outflows as accumulation, but internal exchange movements can distort both.
  • Realized price estimates the market's average cost basis; MVRV compares market value with that cost basis.
  • SOPR shows whether coins being spent are, on average, moving at a profit or loss.
  • On-chain metrics work best as context for cycle positioning, not as short-term trade triggers.

What on-chain data is

Every transaction on a public blockchain records sending addresses, receiving addresses, amounts and a timestamp. Analytics firms process this raw data and add wallet labels, which are guesses about who controls an address: an exchange, a fund, a miner, a bridge, a known hacker. Labels come from public disclosures, deposit tests, clustering heuristics (addresses that spend together are often controlled by the same entity) and investigative work.

On-chain analysis is a form of fundamental analysis specific to crypto. It complements price-based tools and derivatives data such as funding rates.

Exchange flows

Inflows are coins moving into wallets labeled as exchange deposit addresses. Outflows are coins leaving exchanges.

The common reading:

  • Large inflows of BTC or ETH can mean holders are preparing to sell.
  • Sustained outflows can mean holders are moving coins to long-term storage, reducing sell-side supply on exchanges.
  • Rising stablecoin balances on exchanges can mean buying power is arriving.

Caveats matter. Exchanges shuffle funds between hot and cold wallets, consolidate deposits and move coins to custodians. Spot ETF custody wallets, OTC desks and wrapped-token bridges also produce large flows that are not buying or selling. Coins sent to an exchange may be used as derivatives collateral rather than sold. A single big transfer is weak evidence; persistent trends across many wallets are more meaningful.

Whale tracking

"Whales" are holders of very large amounts. Traders watch whale wallets for large moves, and alert services publish big transfers in real time.

Useful practices:

  • Check the label. Many "whale" transfers are exchanges moving their own funds.
  • Look at the destination. A transfer from a long-dormant wallet to an exchange is more informative than one between two cold wallets.
  • Remember that sophisticated players know they are watched. They can split transfers, use OTC desks, or make visible moves to mislead.

For copying wallets in small tokens, see copy trading wallets, which covers how successful-looking wallets can be traps.

Holder distribution

Holder distribution shows how supply is spread across addresses. For Bitcoin, analysts group addresses by balance size or by how long coins have been held: long-term holders (often coins unmoved for 155 days or more, a convention used by some analytics providers) versus short-term holders.

For smaller tokens, concentration is a risk signal. If the top 10 non-exchange, non-contract wallets hold most of the supply, a few sellers can crash the price. Always exclude exchange wallets, liquidity pools, vesting contracts and burn addresses before judging concentration. See tokenomics and token unlocks.

Realized price, MVRV and SOPR

These three metrics come from the Bitcoin model where each coin (technically, each unspent transaction output) carries the price at which it last moved.

Realized cap and realized price

  • Market cap = current price × circulating supply.
  • Realized cap = the sum of each coin's value at the price when it last moved.
  • Realized price = realized cap / supply. It approximates the average cost basis of all holders.

MVRV

MVRV = market cap / realized cap.

  • Above 1: the average holder is in profit.
  • Below 1: the average holder is at a loss, which has historically occurred near major bear market lows.
  • Very high values have historically appeared near cycle tops, though peak levels have been lower in each successive cycle.

Worked example

Imagine a tiny blockchain with 4 coins:

Coins Price when last moved Realized value
1 $20,000 $20,000
2 $40,000 $80,000
1 $80,000 $80,000
4 $180,000
  • Realized cap = $180,000. Realized price = $180,000 / 4 = $45,000.
  • If the market price is $60,000, market cap = 4 × $60,000 = $240,000.
  • MVRV = $240,000 / $180,000 = 1.33. The average holder is up about 33 percent.

If the coin last moved at $80,000 is sold at $60,000, it realizes a loss. If one of the $40,000 coins is sold at $60,000, it realizes a profit.

SOPR

SOPR (spent output profit ratio) = value of coins when spent / value of those coins when they last moved.

  • A coin bought at $40,000 and sold at $50,000 has a SOPR of 50,000 / 40,000 = 1.25.
  • Daily SOPR averages all coins spent that day.
  • Above 1, coins are being sold at a profit on average; below 1, at a loss.

In bull markets, SOPR dipping toward 1 and bouncing has been read as holders refusing to sell at break-even. In bear markets, SOPR failing to hold above 1 suggests holders sell into rallies to get out at cost. Variants filter by holder age to reduce noise.

Stablecoin flows

Stablecoins act as the cash of crypto. Traders track:

  • Total stablecoin supply. Growth means new dollars entering crypto; shrinking supply can mean money leaving.
  • Minting and burning by issuers, which show large players moving fiat in or out.
  • Chain-level flows. Stablecoins bridging to a particular chain can signal where speculative activity is heading.
  • Exchange stablecoin reserves, a rough measure of buying power ready to deploy.

Limits of on-chain data

  • Attribution is probabilistic. Labels can be wrong or missing.
  • Off-chain activity is invisible. Trades inside centralized exchanges, ETF share trading and OTC deals do not appear on-chain. A large share of trading volume never touches the chain.
  • Lost coins distort supply. Coins lost years ago count in supply and realized cap.
  • Structural change. ETFs, custodians and layer-2 networks have changed what flows mean, so historical thresholds may not apply.
  • Different chains, different models. Metrics built for Bitcoin's output model do not map cleanly onto account-based chains like Ethereum or Solana.
  • Data vendors differ. Two providers can show different values for the "same" metric because of different labels and methods.
  • Look-ahead in charts. Indicators that "called every top" are often tuned with hindsight, a form of overfitting.

Common mistakes

  • Reacting to a single whale alert without checking labels and destinations.
  • Treating exchange inflows as guaranteed selling.
  • Using MVRV or SOPR thresholds as exact entry or exit points.
  • Ignoring off-chain activity, especially ETF flows, when judging demand.
  • Judging holder concentration without excluding exchange and contract wallets.

FAQ

What is on-chain analysis in crypto?

It is the analysis of public blockchain data, such as transactions, balances and exchange flows, to understand how holders and large players are behaving. Traders use it alongside price charts and derivatives data.

What is the MVRV ratio?

MVRV is market capitalization divided by realized capitalization, which values each coin at the price it last moved. A reading above 1 means the average holder is in profit; below 1 has historically been associated with deep bear markets.

What does SOPR mean?

SOPR, the spent output profit ratio, compares the value of coins when spent with their value when last moved. Above 1 means coins are being sold at a profit on average, and below 1 means at a loss.

Are exchange inflows bearish?

Large inflows can signal intended selling, but they also come from internal exchange transfers, collateral deposits and custodian moves. Persistent trends are more meaningful than single transfers.
