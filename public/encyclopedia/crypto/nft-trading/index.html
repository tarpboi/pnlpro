NFT Trading

An NFT (non-fungible token) is a unique token on a blockchain that represents ownership of a specific item, most often a digital image in a collection, but also game items, domain names, tickets and other assets. NFT trading means buying and selling these tokens on marketplaces, usually priced in a cryptocurrency such as ETH or SOL. NFTs went through a dramatic boom in 2021 and a deep bust from 2022, and they remain one of the least liquid and most speculative corners of crypto.

Key takeaways

  • Each NFT is unique, so prices are set item by item, and the "floor price" (cheapest listing) is the main reference for a collection.
  • Liquidity is thin: selling at the floor quickly is often possible only by accepting a lower bid, and many NFTs never sell at all.
  • Creator royalties, once standard, became optional or reduced on many marketplaces during 2022 and 2023.
  • Reported volume has been heavily inflated by wash trading, especially on marketplaces that paid token rewards for volume.
  • Most collections from the 2021 boom have lost the large majority of their value, and many trade at or near zero.

How NFT markets work

Marketplaces

NFTs trade on marketplaces that let owners list items for sale and buyers make offers. Well-known examples include OpenSea, Blur and Magic Eden, serving different chains and user types. Most use the same basic tools:

  • Listings: an owner offers an NFT at a fixed price, or in an auction.
  • Offers and bids: buyers offer a price on a specific item.
  • Collection bids: buyers bid on any item in a collection, which creates a floor of demand that sellers can hit instantly.

Trades settle on-chain. You need a self-custody wallet, and every listing or purchase involves signing messages or transactions. That makes wallet security essential, since NFT traders are frequent phishing targets.

Floor price

The floor price is the lowest current listing in a collection. Traders use it as the collection's headline price because individual items are hard to compare. Items with rarer traits usually trade above the floor.

The floor is not a fair-value measure. It can be moved by a handful of listings, and it says nothing about how much demand exists below it. The top collection bid is often a better indicator of what you could actually sell for right now.

Royalties

Many creators set a royalty, often 5 to 10 percent, meant to be paid on every secondary sale. Royalties are not enforced by most NFT token standards; they depend on marketplaces choosing to pay them. In 2022 and 2023, competition between marketplaces led many to make royalties optional or reduce them, which cut income for creators. Some newer standards and chains enforce royalties at the contract level.

Minting vs secondary trading

Minting is buying a newly created NFT directly from the project at a set price. Mints can be cheap compared with later prices, but:

  • Popular mints attract bots and can cost high gas fees, especially on congested networks.
  • Many mints never trade above their mint price after launch.
  • Fake mint sites are a common phishing route.

Secondary trading is buying from other holders on a marketplace. Prices reflect current demand, but costs include marketplace fees, royalties where paid, and gas.

Worked example: a flip

A trader buys an NFT at the floor for 1.00 ETH and later sells it for 1.20 ETH.

  • Sale price: 1.20 ETH.
  • Marketplace fee (assume 1 percent): 0.012 ETH.
  • Creator royalty (assume 5 percent): 0.060 ETH.
  • Seller receives: 1.20 − 0.012 − 0.060 = 1.128 ETH.
  • Gas for buying and listing (assume 0.01 ETH total): net profit = 1.128 − 1.00 − 0.01 = 0.118 ETH.

A 20 percent price rise produced an 11.8 percent profit. If the trader had instead needed to sell quickly by accepting the top collection bid at 0.90 ETH, the result after the same fees and gas would be 0.90 × 0.94 − 1.00 − 0.01 = −0.164 ETH, a loss of 16.4 percent. And all of this is in ETH; if ETH fell in dollar terms meanwhile, the dollar result is worse. Actual fees vary by marketplace and over time, so check them before trading.

Liquidity problems

NFT liquidity is far thinner than for fungible tokens:

  • Each item needs a specific buyer, not just any buyer of the asset.
  • Many collections have only a few sales per day, or none.
  • In downturns, bids disappear first and floors fall in steps as sellers undercut each other.
  • Rare items can take weeks or months to sell at a fair price.

Some platforms let traders borrow against NFTs or sell instantly into pools. These add liquidity but also add leverage and liquidation risk: if floor prices fall, borrowers lose the NFT. NFT lending liquidations contributed to sharp floor drops for some collections.

The altcoin trading article explains why exit liquidity, not entry price, should set position size. That applies even more strongly to NFTs.

Wash trading

Wash trading means trading an asset back and forth between wallets you control to create fake volume or a fake sale price. In NFTs it has been widespread:

  • In early 2022, a marketplace that rewarded traders with its token based on volume saw enormous reported volume, much of it from a small number of wallets selling NFTs between themselves to farm rewards.
  • Wash trades can make a collection look active, or set a high "last sale" price on an item to mislead buyers.

To spot it, look at the number of unique buyers and sellers, whether the same items move repeatedly between a few wallets, and whether volume collapses when incentives end. See volume analysis for how fake volume affects crypto data more broadly.

The 2021-22 boom and bust

NFTs had existed since at least 2017, when CryptoPunks and CryptoKitties launched on Ethereum. In 2021 the market exploded. A digital artwork by the artist Beeple sold at Christie's in March 2021 for about $69 million, profile-picture collections such as Bored Ape Yacht Club traded for hundreds of thousands of dollars per item, and celebrities and brands launched collections. Monthly marketplace volume reached billions of dollars around January 2022.

From 2022, the market reversed with the wider crypto bear market described in crypto market cycles. Trading volume fell by the vast majority from its peak, floor prices of most collections collapsed, and many projects stopped development. Several high-profile collections lost 90 percent or more of their value in ETH terms. Regulators in some countries also brought cases related to NFT offerings marketed as investments.

The market today

Described cautiously: as of late 2026, NFT trading is far smaller than at its peak. Activity continues around a small number of established collections, art, gaming items, Bitcoin-based inscriptions (which began in 2023) and utility uses such as tickets and memberships. Speculative attention that once went to NFT mints has largely moved to fungible tokens, including meme coins. New booms in specific niches remain possible, but most new collections lose value after launch.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the floor price as what you can sell for. The top bid is closer to the truth.
  • Ignoring fees, royalties and gas in profit calculations.
  • Buying collections with few holders and wash-traded volume.
  • Minting from links in social media replies or DMs.
  • Borrowing against NFTs without planning for a floor crash.
  • Forgetting that NFT prices in ETH or SOL add a second layer of currency risk.

FAQ

What does floor price mean for NFTs?

The floor price is the lowest price at which any item in a collection is currently listed. It is the most common reference for a collection's value, but it can be moved by a few listings and may be well above what buyers are bidding.

Is NFT trading still profitable?

Some traders still profit, but volumes and prices are far below their 2021-22 peak, liquidity is thin and most new collections lose value. Fees, royalties and gas also eat into small gains.

What are NFT royalties?

Royalties are a percentage of each secondary sale meant to go to the creator. Most NFT standards do not enforce them, and many marketplaces made them optional or reduced them during 2022 and 2023.

Why is NFT wash trading so common?

Fake trades can make a collection look active or inflate an item's last sale price, and some marketplaces paid token rewards based on volume, which encouraged traders to trade with themselves. Checking unique buyers and repeat transfers helps spot it.
