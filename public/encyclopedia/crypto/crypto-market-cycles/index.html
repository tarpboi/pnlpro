Crypto Market Cycles

Crypto markets have historically moved in large boom-and-bust cycles: long rallies that multiply prices several times, followed by crashes of 75 percent or more for Bitcoin and worse for most altcoins. Traders often link these cycles to Bitcoin's four-year halving schedule, to shifts between Bitcoin and altcoins, and to rotating narratives. Understanding the patterns helps with context and risk, but the sample is small and no cycle model has reliably timed tops or bottoms.

Key takeaways

  • Bitcoin's block reward halves roughly every four years, and past bull markets have peaked about 12 to 18 months after a halving.
  • Only four halvings have happened, so the "four-year cycle" rests on very few data points and may weaken as the market matures.
  • Bitcoin dominance tends to rise in fear and fall in speculative phases, when money rotates into altcoins.
  • Since US spot Bitcoin ETFs launched in January 2024, ETF flows and macro conditions such as interest rates have become larger drivers.
  • Cycles are useful for setting expectations about drawdowns, not for precise timing.

The halving cycle narrative

What a halving is

New bitcoins are created as a reward to miners for each block. Every 210,000 blocks, roughly every four years, that reward is cut in half. The reward started at 50 BTC per block in 2009.

Halving Date Block reward after
First November 2012 25 BTC
Second July 2016 12.5 BTC
Third May 2020 6.25 BTC
Fourth April 2024 3.125 BTC

The next halving is expected around 2028. The exact date depends on how fast blocks are found.

The story

The narrative goes like this: a halving cuts the flow of new supply, miners have fewer coins to sell, and if demand holds steady, price rises. Historically:

  • Bitcoin peaked near $20,000 in December 2017, about 17 months after the 2016 halving.
  • It peaked near $69,000 in November 2021, about 18 months after the 2020 halving.
  • After the April 2024 halving, it set a record above $126,000 in early October 2025, again roughly 18 months later.

Each peak was followed by a deep bear market. Bitcoin fell roughly 84 percent from its 2017 high to its December 2018 low, and roughly 77 percent from its 2021 high to its November 2022 low.

Why to be skeptical

  • Tiny sample. Four halvings is not enough to separate cause from coincidence. Each cycle also had its own catalysts, such as the ICO boom in 2017 and pandemic-era stimulus in 2020 and 2021.
  • Shrinking supply effect. Each halving cuts a smaller amount of new supply relative to the existing stock. With over 19.5 million of the 21 million coins already mined, the issuance change in 2024 was small compared with daily trading volume and ETF flows.
  • The pattern is known. When many traders expect a pattern, they may try to front-run it, which can change the timing. In March 2024, Bitcoin set an all-time high before the halving for the first time.
  • Diminishing returns. Each cycle's percentage gain from bottom to top has been smaller than the one before.

A reasonable view is that halvings form part of a self-reinforcing story that has coincided with past cycles, rather than a mechanical law.

Bitcoin dominance

Bitcoin dominance is Bitcoin's market value as a share of the total crypto market value.

Worked example

Suppose total crypto market capitalization is $3.0 trillion and Bitcoin's is $1.8 trillion.

Dominance = 1.8 / 3.0 = 60 percent.

If altcoins then rally 50 percent while Bitcoin is flat, altcoin value rises from $1.2 trillion to $1.8 trillion, the total becomes $3.6 trillion, and dominance falls to 1.8 / 3.6 = 50 percent.

How traders read it

  • Rising dominance often means money is flowing to the perceived safety of Bitcoin, as in early bull markets and during sell-offs, when altcoins usually fall harder.
  • Falling dominance with rising prices is often read as speculation spreading into altcoins.

Dominance has quirks. Stablecoins count in the total, so when traders sell coins into stablecoins, the stablecoin share rises and distorts the picture. Some traders use dominance figures that exclude stablecoins. New tokens with tiny circulating supply but high quoted prices can also inflate the total market value.

Altcoin seasons

An "altseason" is a period in which a broad range of altcoins outperforms Bitcoin, often sharply. The classic sequence described by traders is:

  1. Bitcoin rallies first and dominance rises.
  2. Ether and large-cap altcoins catch up.
  3. Money moves to mid and small caps, then to the most speculative tokens, including meme coins.
  4. The peak arrives with heavy retail participation, high funding rates and record leverage.

This sequence was clearest in early 2018 and in 2021. In later cycles, altcoin rallies have been narrower and shorter, focused on specific sectors rather than the whole market. One reason is the sheer number of tokens: supply of new tokens grew much faster than new buyers. Another is heavy token unlocks from projects launched with low float and high valuations.

Narratives and sector rotation

Within cycles, money rotates between themes. Past examples include ICOs (2017), DeFi (2020), NFTs and gaming (2021), layer-2 networks, AI-related tokens, real-world asset tokenization, and meme coin launchpads. A narrative can carry prices far beyond any measurable fundamentals, then fade quickly when attention moves on.

Traders who follow narratives watch where new liquidity is arriving, which tokens get new exchange listings, and on-chain data such as stablecoin flows into a chain. See altcoin trading and on-chain analysis. The risk is arriving late: by the time a narrative is obvious on social media, early holders are often selling.

Macro and ETF flows

Crypto no longer moves in isolation. Several outside forces now matter:

  • Interest rates and liquidity. Crypto rallied during the near-zero rates of 2020 and 2021 and fell as central banks raised rates in 2022. Lower rates and looser liquidity have tended to support speculative assets. See central banks and interest rates.
  • Risk sentiment. Bitcoin has at times traded closely with technology stocks, especially in sell-offs. Correlations change over time, as described in intermarket analysis.
  • ETF flows. The SEC approved US spot Bitcoin ETFs in January 2024, and spot Ether ETFs began trading in July 2024. Hong Kong listed spot Bitcoin and Ether ETFs in April 2024. Daily creations and redemptions in these funds are now watched as a measure of institutional demand.
  • Corporate treasuries. Several listed companies have bought Bitcoin and other tokens for their balance sheets, adding a new source of demand that can also become a source of selling.
  • Regulation. Events such as US stablecoin legislation in July 2025 and the EU's MiCA rules can change sentiment and access.

Using cycles responsibly

Cycle thinking is most useful for:

  • Setting expectations. If history is any guide, a crypto portfolio can fall 70 to 90 percent in a bear market. Size positions so you could survive that.
  • Avoiding extremes. Euphoria (record funding, celebrity endorsements, friends asking how to buy) has tended to appear near tops. Despair and low volume have tended to appear near bottoms.
  • Context for leverage. Leverage is especially dangerous late in a rally, when crowded longs set up liquidation cascades.

It is least useful for precise timing. Analysts who called exact cycle tops in advance are rare, and many charts of the "cycle" are drawn with hindsight.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the four-year cycle as certain and making large leveraged bets on its timing.
  • Expecting every altcoin to recover in the next cycle. Many tokens from past cycles never regained their highs.
  • Reading dominance without adjusting for stablecoins.
  • Ignoring macro conditions and assuming crypto follows only its own internal schedule.
  • Holding through a bear market by default without a plan for how much drawdown you can bear.

FAQ

When is the next Bitcoin halving?

The fifth halving is expected around 2028, when the block reward will fall from 3.125 to 1.5625 BTC. The exact date depends on block production speed, so estimates shift slightly over time.

Does the Bitcoin halving cause price increases?

Past bull markets peaked roughly 12 to 18 months after halvings, but with only four examples it is impossible to prove the halving was the cause. Each cycle also had other drivers, including liquidity conditions and new investor products.

What is Bitcoin dominance?

It is Bitcoin's market capitalization divided by the total crypto market capitalization. Traders use it to judge whether money is flowing toward Bitcoin or out into altcoins.

What is altcoin season?

Altcoin season is a period when many altcoins outperform Bitcoin, usually late in a bull market. Recent cycles have seen narrower, sector-specific rallies rather than broad altcoin seasons.
