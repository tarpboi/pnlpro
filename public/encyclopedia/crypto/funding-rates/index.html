Funding Rates

A funding rate is a periodic payment between traders holding long and short positions in a perpetual futures contract. When the perp trades above the spot index, longs pay shorts; when it trades below, shorts pay longs. Funding is what keeps a contract with no expiry tied to the spot price. For traders, it is both a cost (or income) on every held position and one of the most watched sentiment gauges in crypto.

Key takeaways

  • Funding payment = position notional × funding rate, exchanged at each funding interval.
  • Positive funding means longs pay shorts and usually signals bullish crowding; negative funding means shorts pay longs.
  • Intervals vary by venue and contract: eight hours is common, but four-hour, one-hour and continuous funding also exist.
  • A rate that looks tiny per interval can be large annualized: 0.01 percent every eight hours is about 11 percent a year.
  • Cash-and-carry funding trades can collect funding with little price exposure, but they carry fee, liquidation, exchange and rate-flip risks.

How funding works

Perpetual futures never settle, so something must stop their price drifting away from spot. Funding does that by making the crowded side pay.

If many traders want to be long, buying pushes the perp above the index price. Positive funding makes longs pay shorts, which discourages new longs and rewards shorts, nudging the perp back down. The reverse happens when shorts crowd in.

On most centralized exchanges, funding goes directly from one group of traders to the other. The exchange does not keep it, though it does charge normal trading fees.

Intervals

  • Eight hours is the most common schedule on large exchanges, often at 00:00, 08:00 and 16:00 UTC.
  • Four hours or one hour applies to some contracts, and some exchanges shorten the interval during extreme moves.
  • Hourly or continuous funding is common on perp DEXs.

On interval-based venues, you pay or receive funding only if you hold a position at the funding timestamp. Opening a minute after and closing a minute before means no funding changes hands. Check each contract's details page; intervals and caps differ by exchange and can change.

Calculation intuition

Exact formulas vary, but a common structure is:

Funding rate = premium index + clamp(interest rate − premium index, a small band)

  • The premium index measures how far the perp has traded above or below the spot index over the interval.
  • The interest rate component is often fixed at a small value, commonly 0.01 percent per eight hours on many large exchanges, reflecting the cost difference between the quote and base currencies.
  • The clamp means that when the premium is small, funding settles at the default rate. Many contracts therefore show exactly 0.0100 percent most of the time in calm markets.
  • Exchanges also cap funding at a maximum per interval.

The payment itself is simple:

Funding payment = position notional × funding rate

Notional is usually based on the mark price at the funding time, so it changes as the price moves.

Worked examples

Paying positive funding

A trader is long $50,000 notional of ETH perps. Funding is +0.03 percent per eight hours.

  • Per interval: $50,000 × 0.0003 = $15 paid to shorts.
  • Per day (three intervals): $45.
  • Over 10 days: $450.

Annualized, that rate is 0.03% × 3 × 365 = 32.85 percent of notional. If the trader used 5x leverage, margin is $10,000, so $450 over 10 days is 4.5 percent of the margin, just from funding. Holding a leveraged long in a hot market can cost far more than most traders expect. See trading costs and fees for how carrying costs add up.

Receiving negative funding

A trader is short $20,000 notional of BTC perps. Funding is −0.02 percent per eight hours. Negative funding means shorts pay longs, so the short pays $20,000 × 0.0002 = $4 per interval, $12 per day. Being on the popular side in either direction costs money.

Annualizing the default rate

0.01% × 3 × 365 = 10.95 percent a year. That is why some traders describe "baseline" perp funding as roughly 11 percent annualized, paid by longs to shorts, on venues using that default.

Reading funding as sentiment

Funding shows which side is paying to hold exposure, so it is used as a sentiment indicator.

Funding reading Usual interpretation Caveat
Near default, steady Balanced positioning No signal
Strongly positive and rising Crowded, leveraged longs; often late in rallies Can stay high for weeks in strong trends
Strongly negative Crowded shorts; fear or hedging Sets up potential short squeezes
Positive while price falls Longs adding into weakness Risk of long liquidation cascades

Extreme funding often coincides with fragile positioning. When many leveraged longs are paying high funding and price starts to drop, their liquidations can cascade, as explained in liquidations. Many traders combine funding with open interest: rising open interest plus rising positive funding suggests new leveraged longs entering.

Funding is a contrarian signal at extremes, not a timing tool. Markets can rise for a long time with high funding.

Funding arbitrage (cash-and-carry)

Because funding has historically been positive more often than negative, some traders hold a market-neutral position to collect it:

  1. Buy the coin on the spot market.
  2. Short the same amount on the perp.

Price moves roughly cancel, and the trader receives funding while it stays positive. This is a version of the basis or cash-and-carry trade covered in crypto arbitrage.

Worked example

  • Buy 1 BTC spot at $60,000.
  • Short 1 BTC perp at $60,000, posting $12,000 margin (5x).
  • Total capital: $72,000.
  • Average funding: +0.01 percent per eight hours, so $6 per interval, $18 per day.
  • Over 30 days: $540 received.
  • Fees: spot taker 0.10 percent in and out = $120; perp taker 0.05 percent in and out = $60. Total $180.
  • Net: $540 − $180 = $360, or 0.5 percent on $72,000 for the month.

Risks

  • Funding flips. If funding turns negative, the short pays instead of receiving.
  • Liquidation of the short leg. If BTC rallies sharply, the short's margin shrinks even though the spot leg gains. Without enough margin, or if the spot is on a different venue, the short can be liquidated, leaving an unhedged long.
  • Fees. The trade needs several days of normal funding just to cover entry and exit costs.
  • Exchange risk. Capital sits on centralized exchanges or in DeFi protocols. A failure can wipe out months of small gains.
  • Crowding. When large funds and yield products run this trade, funding compresses toward the default, reducing returns.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting funding is charged on notional, not margin. At high leverage, funding eats margin quickly.
  • Comparing per-interval rates across venues with different intervals. 0.01 percent per hour is eight times 0.01 percent per eight hours.
  • Treating high funding as an automatic short signal.
  • Running cash-and-carry without a liquidation buffer on the short leg.
  • Ignoring that predicted funding shown on screen can change before the timestamp.

FAQ

What does a positive funding rate mean?

It means the perpetual contract is trading above the spot index and longs pay shorts at each funding interval. It usually indicates more demand for leveraged long exposure.

How often is funding paid?

Many large exchanges pay funding every eight hours, but some contracts use four-hour or one-hour intervals, and many perp DEXs fund hourly or continuously. Check the contract specification on your exchange.

How do I calculate a funding payment?

Multiply your position's notional value by the funding rate. A $10,000 position with a 0.01 percent rate pays or receives $1 per interval.

Is funding rate arbitrage risk-free?

No. It removes most price exposure but still carries risks from funding turning negative, liquidation of the short leg, trading fees and the failure of the exchange or protocol holding your capital.
