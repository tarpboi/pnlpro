Stablecoins

A stablecoin is a crypto token designed to hold a steady value, almost always $1. Stablecoins are the cash of the crypto market: most trading pairs are quoted against them, they serve as collateral on derivatives exchanges and DeFi protocols, and they let traders move dollars between venues around the clock. Their stability depends entirely on how they are backed, and history includes both brief depegs and total collapses.

Key takeaways

  • Fiat-backed stablecoins such as USDT and USDC hold cash and short-term government debt and redeem tokens for dollars, though usually only for approved customers.
  • Crypto-backed stablecoins hold more crypto collateral than the tokens issued; algorithmic designs that relied on market incentives alone have failed.
  • TerraUSD (UST) collapsed in May 2022, and USDC briefly fell below $0.90 in March 2023 when a bank holding part of its reserves failed.
  • A depeg changes the value of your collateral and can trigger liquidations, so the choice of stablecoin is a risk decision.
  • Regulation tightened from 2024: the EU's MiCA stablecoin rules applied from June 30, 2024, and the US enacted the GENIUS Act on July 18, 2025.

Why traders use stablecoins

  • Quote currency. Pairs such as BTC/USDT and ETH/USDC are priced in stablecoins.
  • Parking cash. Traders sell into a stablecoin to step out of the market without withdrawing to a bank.
  • Collateral. Linear perpetual futures are margined in stablecoins, and DeFi lending uses them heavily.
  • Transfers. Stablecoins move between exchanges and wallets 24/7, often faster than bank transfers.
  • Market signal. Stablecoin supply and exchange balances are watched as a measure of buying power. See on-chain analysis.

Types of stablecoins

Type How it holds $1 Examples Main risks
Fiat-backed Issuer holds cash, bank deposits and short-term government bills; redeems tokens for dollars USDT (Tether), USDC (Circle) Reserve quality, bank failure, issuer freeze, regulatory action
Crypto-backed Users lock more crypto than the stablecoins they mint; positions are liquidated if collateral falls DAI and USDS (Sky, formerly MakerDAO) Collateral crash, oracle failure, smart contract risk
Synthetic or delta-neutral Holds crypto and offsets price risk with short derivatives positions Ethena's USDe Negative funding, exchange counterparty risk, liquidity in stress
Algorithmic Relies on incentives and a linked token, with little or no outside collateral TerraUSD (UST), collapsed 2022 Death spiral when confidence breaks

Fiat-backed

The issuer takes in dollars and mints tokens. Large holders that pass the issuer's checks can redeem tokens for dollars, which anchors the price: if a token trades at $0.99, an approved firm can buy it and redeem it for $1.00. Most retail users cannot redeem directly, so they rely on these firms and on market liquidity.

Tether's USDT, launched in 2014, has long been the largest stablecoin by market value and dominates trading on many offshore exchanges. Tether has faced questions over reserve disclosure throughout its history. Circle's USDC is widely used in the US and in DeFi, and Circle publishes regular reserve attestations.

Fiat-backed issuers can freeze tokens at specific addresses, usually at law enforcement request. That makes them closer to a bank deposit than to Bitcoin in terms of control.

Crypto-backed

Users deposit volatile crypto, such as ETH, into a smart contract and mint stablecoins against it, with collateral worth well over $1 per token. If collateral value falls toward the minimum ratio, the position is liquidated. DAI, issued by MakerDAO since 2017, is the best-known example; the protocol rebranded as Sky in 2024 and introduced a successor token, USDS. Over time DAI also took in large amounts of USDC and tokenized government debt as collateral, which reduced its independence from the traditional system.

Algorithmic: the UST collapse

TerraUSD (UST) aimed to hold $1 through a mint-and-burn link with the LUNA token: 1 UST could always be swapped for $1 worth of newly minted LUNA. A protocol called Anchor offered deposit yields of close to 20 percent on UST, which drew in large deposits.

In May 2022, large withdrawals and sales pushed UST below $1. Arbitrageurs burned UST to mint LUNA and sold the LUNA, which crashed its price, which required minting ever more LUNA per UST. Within days UST was worth a few cents and LUNA was nearly worthless, wiping out tens of billions of dollars in market value. The collapse triggered failures at crypto lenders and funds later that year. See leverage risk and black swans.

The lesson: a stablecoin backed by its own system's token is only as stable as confidence in that token.

Depeg events

USDC, March 2023

On March 10, 2023, US regulators closed Silicon Valley Bank. Circle disclosed that about $3.3 billion of USDC's reserves were held there. Over the weekend, USDC traded as low as roughly $0.87 to $0.88 on some venues. DAI, which held a large amount of USDC as collateral, fell with it. After US authorities announced on March 12 that all SVB depositors would be protected, USDC returned to about $1.

Venue-specific depegs

During the October 10 and 11, 2025 crash, USDe traded far below $1 on at least one large exchange's order book while holding closer to its peg elsewhere. Exchanges that valued collateral at their own order book price saw some positions liquidated as a result. A stablecoin's price can differ by venue during stress, and your exchange's pricing method matters.

Worked example: a depeg and your margin

A trader holds $50,000 of a stablecoin as cross-margin collateral and has a leveraged long that requires $20,000 of maintenance margin. If the stablecoin falls to $0.90 on that exchange's valuation, collateral value drops to $45,000. If the trader's position is also down $26,000 in the same crash, equity falls to $19,000, below maintenance, and the position is liquidated even though the stablecoin later recovers. Without the depeg, equity would have been $24,000, safely above the $20,000 requirement.

Holding stablecoins is not the same as holding risk-free cash, especially on leveraged accounts.

Choosing and managing stablecoins

  • Know the backing. Read the issuer's reserve reports and how often they are published.
  • Spread exposure. Large balances can be split across issuers.
  • Check redemption. Who can redeem, how fast and with what minimums.
  • Watch the peg in stress. Small, persistent discounts can be early warning signs.
  • Mind the network. The same stablecoin exists on several blockchains. Sending on the wrong network is a common, sometimes unrecoverable, mistake.

Regulation

Stablecoin rules have tightened quickly. As of late 2026:

  • European Union. Under MiCA, the rules for stablecoins (e-money tokens and asset-referenced tokens) applied from June 30, 2024. Issuers need authorization and must meet reserve and redemption requirements. Several exchanges restricted non-compliant stablecoins for customers in the European Economic Area.
  • United States. The GENIUS Act, signed July 18, 2025, created a federal framework for payment stablecoins, with requirements for reserves, redemption and supervision. It takes effect on the earlier of 18 months after enactment or 120 days after final implementing regulations are issued, so details were still being phased in.
  • Elsewhere. Hong Kong, Singapore, Japan, the UK and others have introduced or proposed their own stablecoin regimes.

Rules continue to change, so check the current position in your country. For regulators and investor protection in general, see the regulation overview.

Common mistakes

  • Treating every stablecoin as equal. Backing, transparency and liquidity differ.
  • Chasing high stablecoin yields without asking where the yield comes from. Anchor's UST yield was the bait in 2022.
  • Holding all collateral in one stablecoin on a leveraged account.
  • Sending tokens on the wrong network.
  • Assuming you can redeem directly with the issuer at $1.

FAQ

What is the difference between USDT and USDC?

Both are fiat-backed dollar stablecoins issued by private companies: USDT by Tether and USDC by Circle. They differ in reserve disclosures, regulatory status in different regions, and which exchanges and blockchains favor them.

Can a stablecoin lose its peg?

Yes. Fiat-backed coins have briefly traded below $1 during stress, such as USDC in March 2023, and algorithmic designs such as TerraUSD have collapsed entirely. Prices can also differ temporarily between exchanges.

Are stablecoins safe?

They carry issuer, reserve, smart contract and regulatory risks, and they are generally not covered by deposit insurance. Well-backed, transparent stablecoins have held their pegs most of the time, but they are not risk-free cash.

What happened to TerraUSD (UST)?

In May 2022, selling pushed UST below $1 and its mint-and-burn link to LUNA created a spiral that crashed both tokens. UST lost nearly all of its value within days.
