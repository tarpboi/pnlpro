Altcoin Trading

6 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Altcoins are all cryptocurrencies other than Bitcoin, from large smart-contract platforms to small tokens with a few thousand holders. Trading them offers bigger moves than Bitcoin in both directions, but liquidity is thinner, supply schedules matter more, and reported volume is less trustworthy. Most altcoins have underperformed Bitcoin over full market cycles, and many have gone to near zero.

Key takeaways

  • Altcoins fall into liquidity tiers, and the right position size and order type depend heavily on the tier.
  • Most altcoins behave like leveraged Bitcoin: they tend to rise more in rallies and fall more in sell-offs.
  • Exchange listings can cause short spikes that often fade, especially for tokens with low float and high fully diluted valuation.
  • Volume figures can be inflated by wash trading, so check order book depth directly.
  • Delisting, regulatory action and token unlocks are risks rarely seen in large-cap assets.

Liquidity tiers

Liquidity, meaning how much you can trade without moving the price, varies enormously across altcoins.

Tier Typical examples Characteristics Practical implications
Large caps Ether, Solana and a few others Deep books on many exchanges, liquid perps and often ETFs Similar trading conditions to Bitcoin, with higher volatility
Mid caps Established DeFi, infrastructure and exchange tokens Decent depth on major exchanges, perps available Spreads widen during stress; size moderately
Small caps Newer or niche tokens Thin books, few venues, large spreads Limit orders essential; exits can be hard
Micro caps and new launches Recently launched tokens, many DEX-only Very shallow liquidity, high manipulation risk Treat as lottery-like; see meme coins explained

The bid-ask spread and liquidity article explains how to measure the true round-trip cost.

Worked example: exit liquidity

A trader holds $50,000 of a small-cap token. The order book shows $20,000 of bids within 2 percent of the current price, and another $40,000 between 2 and 8 percent below it.

Selling everything at market would clear the first $20,000 of bids and then eat $30,000 into the next band. If bids are spread evenly, the first $20,000 fills at an average discount of about 1 percent ($200), and the next $30,000 eats about three-quarters of the second band, filling between 2 and about 6.5 percent below the starting price, an average of about 4.25 percent ($1,275). That is roughly $1,475, or about 3 percent of the position, lost to price impact before fees. If other holders sell at the same moment, as they often do on bad news, those bids may vanish. Position size in thin altcoins should be set by how much you can exit, not by how much you can buy.

Beta to Bitcoin

Beta measures how much an asset tends to move when the market moves. Most altcoins have a beta above 1 relative to Bitcoin.

Suppose an altcoin has an estimated beta of 2 to Bitcoin. If Bitcoin falls 10 percent, the altcoin falls about 20 percent on average, all else equal. A $10,000 position in that altcoin carries market risk similar to $20,000 of Bitcoin.

Practical consequences:

  • Holding several altcoins is not real diversification. In sell-offs, correlations rise and they fall together. See correlation and portfolio risk.
  • Beta estimates change over time and can break down for tokens with their own news.
  • Some traders hedge altcoin longs with a Bitcoin short to isolate the token's specific performance, though this adds cost and complexity.

The listing effect

When a token is listed on a large exchange, it becomes accessible to millions of new buyers. Prices have often jumped on listing announcements, a pattern sometimes called the "listing pump."

The pattern has weakened and often reversed:

  • Traders who bought earlier on DEXs or smaller exchanges sell into the new liquidity.
  • Airdrop recipients and early investors take profits. See airdrops and points farming.
  • New tokens launched directly on major exchanges have frequently fallen in the weeks after listing as supply comes to market.

Listing announcements also attract scams and leaks. Insider trading on listing news has been the subject of enforcement actions. A rumored listing that does not happen can leave late buyers holding a falling token.

Low float, high FDV

Float is the share of total supply that is circulating and tradable. FDV (fully diluted valuation) is the price times the maximum supply.

Many tokens launch with a small float, perhaps 10 to 20 percent of supply, while the rest is locked for teams, investors and ecosystem funds. The small float lets a modest amount of buying push the price high, producing a large FDV. As locked tokens unlock over the following months and years, new supply hits the market. If demand does not grow at the same pace, the price tends to fall.

Before trading a token, check:

  • Circulating supply versus total supply.
  • The unlock schedule, especially large cliffs in the coming months.
  • Who holds the locked tokens and their likely cost basis.

The tokenomics and token unlocks article covers the math in detail.

Narratives

Altcoins trade on stories more than on measurable fundamentals. A sector can rally on a single headline or a popular founder's post, then fade as attention moves on. Narratives in past cycles included DeFi, NFTs and gaming, layer-2 networks, AI-related tokens and real-world asset tokenization. Within a sector, the largest and most liquid token often moves first, followed by smaller copies.

Narrative trading means competing for attention against fast, well-informed participants, including insiders. If you first hear about a narrative from a trending social post, early holders may already be selling. See crypto market cycles for how rotation tends to unfold.

Delisting and regulatory risk

Exchanges remove tokens for low volume, project failure, security issues or regulatory reasons. Past examples include:

  • US exchanges suspending trading in tokens after US regulators alleged they were unregistered securities, such as XRP after the SEC lawsuit in December 2020.
  • Some exchanges restricting privacy coins in certain jurisdictions.
  • EU-facing exchanges restricting stablecoins that did not comply with MiCA rules, which applied to stablecoins from June 30, 2024.

A delisting announcement often triggers an immediate sharp fall, and liquidity can disappear before the deadline. Smaller exchanges may also simply stop processing withdrawals for a token.

Wash trading and fake volume

Wash trading means buying and selling with yourself, or with a colluding party, to create the appearance of activity. In 2019 the asset manager Bitwise presented research to the SEC concluding that most reported Bitcoin volume at the time came from exchanges reporting fake or non-economic volume. The worst examples were concentrated in smaller, unregulated exchanges. Practices have improved at major venues, but wash trading remains common in small tokens and on some DEXs, where bots trade back and forth to climb trending lists.

To judge real liquidity:

  • Look at order book depth within 1 to 2 percent of the price, not 24-hour volume.
  • Compare volume across several reputable venues.
  • Be suspicious of high volume with very few holders or flat, unnatural trade patterns.

See volume analysis for more on wash trading in crypto.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a basket of altcoins as diversified.
  • Buying the listing announcement after the price has already spiked.
  • Ignoring circulating supply and unlocks and judging a token only by its price.
  • Using market orders in thin books.
  • Trusting 24-hour volume as a measure of liquidity.
  • Holding through the bear market on the assumption every altcoin recovers.

FAQ

Are altcoins riskier than Bitcoin?

Generally yes. Altcoins tend to have higher volatility, thinner liquidity and more supply and project risk, and many have lost nearly all their value over past cycles.

Why do altcoins drop more than Bitcoin?

Most altcoins have a beta above 1 to Bitcoin, so they amplify market moves. In sell-offs, traders also sell riskier assets first, and thin liquidity makes the price impact larger.

What does low float, high FDV mean?

It describes a token with only a small share of its total supply in circulation but a high valuation when all supply is counted. Future unlocks can add large amounts of supply and weigh on the price.

Does an exchange listing make a token go up?

Listings have often caused short-term spikes, but the effect frequently fades or reverses as early holders sell into the new liquidity. It is not a reliable signal on its own.
