Crypto Arbitrage

Crypto arbitrage is the practice of profiting from price differences for the same or equivalent assets, either across venues or between related instruments. Because crypto trades on hundreds of fragmented exchanges and blockchains, price gaps appear more often than in mature markets. But most visible gaps are smaller than the costs of capturing them, and the ones that remain are contested by fast, well-capitalized firms. Real arbitrage also carries risks that "risk-free" marketing ignores.

Key takeaways

  • Cross-exchange, triangular and CEX-DEX arbitrage exploit price gaps between venues or trading pairs, and usually need automation to compete.
  • Fees, transfer times, withdrawal limits and slippage often exceed the apparent profit.
  • Funding and basis (cash-and-carry) trades earn a spread over time rather than instantly, and are the most accessible form for non-specialists.
  • Counterparty risk is the biggest hidden cost, because arbitrage requires capital spread across several exchanges.
  • No arbitrage in crypto is truly risk-free once execution, custody and liquidation risks are counted.

Why price gaps exist

  • Fragmentation. The same coin trades on many exchanges with separate order books.
  • Capital controls and access. Some local markets trade at a persistent premium or discount because money cannot move freely. South Korea's "kimchi premium" is the best-known example.
  • Transfer friction. Moving coins between venues takes time and costs fees.
  • Speed of information. Prices update at different speeds on different venues, especially on blockchains with slower block times.
  • Stress. During crashes and exchange outages, gaps widen sharply as liquidity disappears.

The general theory of arbitrage, including statistical arbitrage, is covered in arbitrage strategies.

Cross-exchange arbitrage

Buy on the cheaper exchange, sell on the more expensive one.

Worked example

BTC trades at $60,000 on Exchange A and $60,150 on Exchange B, a spread of 0.25 percent. A trader buys 1 BTC on A and sells 1 BTC on B.

  • Gross profit: $150.
  • Taker fees at 0.10 percent on each side: $60 + $60.15 = about $120.
  • Net: about $30, before any transfer costs.

If the trader has to move the BTC from A to B, add a withdrawal fee and the time for the blockchain to confirm. During that time the gap can close, or reverse. That is why serious cross-exchange arbitrageurs keep balances on both exchanges and trade both legs at once, rebalancing inventory later. This requires double the capital and leaves it exposed on two venues.

Triangular arbitrage

Triangular arbitrage uses three trading pairs on the same exchange whose prices are out of line.

Worked example

On one exchange:

  • BTC/USDT = 60,000
  • ETH/USDT = 3,000
  • ETH/BTC = 0.0505

The implied ETH/BTC price is 3,000 / 60,000 = 0.0500, so ETH is slightly overpriced in BTC terms. A trader with 10,000 USDT:

  1. Buys ETH: 10,000 / 3,000 = 3.3333 ETH.
  2. Sells ETH for BTC: 3.3333 × 0.0505 = 0.168333 BTC.
  3. Sells BTC for USDT: 0.168333 × 60,000 = 10,100 USDT.

Gross profit: $100, or 1 percent. With a 0.10 percent fee on each of the three trades, the result is 10,100 × 0.999³ = about 10,069.73 USDT, a net profit of about $69.73.

Going the other way around the triangle would lose money: 10,000 USDT to BTC to ETH to USDT returns about 9,900.99 USDT before fees.

In practice, gaps of this size on a major exchange are rare and disappear within milliseconds, because many bots monitor every triangle. Gaps that last long enough for manual trading usually sit in illiquid pairs, where the order book cannot absorb meaningful size.

CEX-DEX arbitrage

Prices on decentralized exchanges only change when someone trades. When a centralized exchange moves first, the AMM pool's price is stale until an arbitrageur trades against it. This is one of the largest sources of on-chain MEV.

Competing here requires fast infrastructure, inventory on both sides, and the ability to pay block builders or validators for priority. Gas costs and failed transactions eat into profits. For most individuals, this space is dominated by specialized firms. See CEX vs DEX.

Funding and basis arbitrage

These trades capture a spread that builds up over time.

Funding rate arbitrage

Buy the coin spot and short the same amount on a perpetual future. Price moves roughly cancel, and the trader collects funding while it is positive. The funding rates article includes a worked example with fees.

Cash-and-carry with dated futures

Dated futures often trade above spot when markets are bullish (contango). A trader buys spot and sells the future, locking in the difference, which converges to zero at expiry.

Example: BTC spot is $60,000. A futures contract expiring in 90 days trades at $61,500.

  • Basis: $1,500, or 2.5 percent.
  • Annualized: 2.5% × 365 / 90 = about 10.1 percent.

If the trader holds to expiry, the future settles at the spot price, and the $1,500 is earned regardless of where BTC goes, minus fees and the cost of margin. The trader compares that annualized return with what the capital could earn elsewhere, such as on cash or short-term government bills.

Regulated exchanges such as CME list Bitcoin and Ether futures, and the spot leg can be held through ETFs, which is how many institutions run this trade. For the mechanics of term structure, see contango, backwardation and rollover.

The costs and risks that erase edges

Risk What goes wrong
Fees Maker/taker fees on every leg, withdrawal fees and gas can exceed thin spreads
Transfer delays Blockchain confirmations and exchange processing take minutes to hours; prices move meanwhile
Withdrawal limits and suspensions Exchanges cap or pause withdrawals, often exactly when gaps are largest
Slippage The quoted price is only good for the size at the top of the book
Counterparty risk Capital spread across exchanges is exposed to each one's failure; many trading firms had funds trapped when FTX collapsed in 2022
Liquidation The short leg of a hedged trade can be liquidated in a sharp rally if margin is too thin
Execution risk One leg fills and the other does not, leaving an unhedged position
Wallet and network errors Wrong network, missing memo or tag, or a congested chain can strand funds
Regulatory Moving money to capture regional premiums can break capital control laws

Persistent gaps usually exist for a reason. A coin trading cheaper on one exchange may be cheap because withdrawals there are suspended, or because the market doubts the exchange's solvency. In early 2014, after Mt. Gox halted Bitcoin withdrawals, Bitcoin on Mt. Gox traded far below the price on other exchanges. The "arbitrage" was really a bet on getting money out, and the exchange collapsed weeks later.

Who wins

The main winners in crypto arbitrage are firms with low fees (high-volume tiers or market-maker rebates), fast infrastructure, capital on many venues, and risk systems to manage counterparty exposure. Retail traders can access funding and basis trades with reasonable effort, but should treat them as carrying real risk rather than as a savings account. For related strategies in prediction markets, see prediction market arbitrage and the guide to BTC up/down arbitrage.

Common mistakes

  • Counting only the price gap and forgetting fees on every leg.
  • Transferring coins between exchanges mid-trade and assuming the gap will still be there.
  • Chasing gaps on unknown exchanges, where the gap reflects withdrawal problems.
  • Running a hedged trade with thin margin on the derivatives leg.
  • Falling for "arbitrage bot" schemes promising guaranteed daily returns, a common scam. See trading scams.

FAQ

Is crypto arbitrage still profitable?

Simple cross-exchange and triangular gaps on major exchanges are mostly captured by automated firms within seconds. Funding and basis trades remain accessible but return modest amounts and carry exchange and liquidation risk.

What is triangular arbitrage in crypto?

It is a sequence of three trades on one exchange, such as USDT to ETH to BTC to USDT, that profits when the three exchange rates are inconsistent. Gaps are usually tiny and short-lived.

What is the cash-and-carry trade in crypto?

It means buying a coin on the spot market and selling a futures or perpetual contract against it to earn the premium or funding. Price exposure is largely hedged, but fees, margin and counterparty risk remain.

Is crypto arbitrage risk-free?

No. Price gaps can close before both legs fill, transfers can be delayed, exchanges can freeze withdrawals or fail, and hedged positions can still be liquidated.
