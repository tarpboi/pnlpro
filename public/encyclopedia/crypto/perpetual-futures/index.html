Perpetual Futures

A perpetual future, or "perp", is a futures contract with no expiry date that tracks the price of an underlying asset such as Bitcoin. Instead of converging to spot at expiry, a perp is pulled toward the spot price by regular funding payments between long and short traders. Perps are the most traded instrument in crypto, and their mechanics (mark price, margin, liquidation and funding) shape much of the market's short-term price action.

Key takeaways

  • A perp never expires; funding payments keep its price near the underlying index.
  • Exchanges use a mark price, built from an index of spot prices, to calculate unrealized profit and liquidations, so a brief spike on one venue does not liquidate everyone.
  • Linear contracts are margined and settled in a stablecoin; inverse contracts are margined and settled in the coin itself, which adds an extra layer of exposure.
  • When liquidations cannot be filled at a good price, an insurance fund absorbs losses, and if it runs short, auto-deleveraging can close profitable traders' positions.
  • Perps are high-leverage products, and most retail traders who use them lose money.

Why perps exist

Traditional futures, covered in futures basics, expire on a set date. Holders must roll to the next contract, and prices can differ from spot by a basis that changes over time. The idea of a futures contract without expiry was proposed by economist Robert Shiller in the early 1990s for illiquid assets. The crypto exchange BitMEX launched a Bitcoin perpetual swap in 2016, and the design spread to nearly every crypto derivatives venue.

Perps give traders a simple product: one contract per asset, no rolling, and the ability to go long or short with leverage.

Three prices: last, index and mark

A perp screen usually shows three prices, and confusing them is a common mistake.

Price What it is Used for
Last price The most recent trade on this exchange's perp order book What you see on the chart; fills of your orders
Index price A weighted average of spot prices from several major exchanges The reference the perp is anchored to; funding calculations
Mark price A fair-value estimate based on the index, plus a smoothed premium Unrealized PnL, margin checks and liquidations

Because liquidations use the mark price, a sudden wick in the last price caused by one large order does not, by itself, trigger liquidations. A sustained move in spot markets does. Exchanges document their exact formulas, which differ in detail.

Funding

If a perp trades above the index, longs pay shorts at each funding interval. If it trades below, shorts pay longs. This makes it expensive to hold the side that is pushing the price away from spot, which pulls the perp back toward fair value.

Funding is usually exchanged directly between traders, not collected by the exchange. Common intervals are every eight hours, though some venues use four hours, one hour or continuous accrual. The funding rates article covers the calculation and how traders read funding as sentiment.

Margin, leverage and liquidation

To open a perp you post initial margin, a fraction of the position's notional value. At 10x leverage, initial margin is 10 percent. You must keep at least the maintenance margin, a smaller percentage, or the position is liquidated. Exchanges set maximum leverage per contract, often lowering it as position size grows (tiered margin).

Positions can use isolated margin, where only the margin assigned to that position is at risk, or cross margin, where the whole account balance backs all positions. The liquidations article works through liquidation-price calculations.

Linear vs inverse contracts

Linear contracts

Linear perps are quoted, margined and settled in a stablecoin such as USDT or USDC. Profit and loss is straightforward: position size in coins times the price change.

Inverse contracts

Inverse perps are margined and settled in the underlying coin. A BTC/USD inverse perp is quoted in dollars, but you post BTC as margin and profits and losses are paid in BTC. Contracts are often sized in dollars (for example, one contract equals $1).

Worked example

A trader goes long Bitcoin at $60,000 and closes at $63,000, a 5 percent rise.

Linear: long 0.5 BTC (notional $30,000) with $6,000 margin (5x). PnL = 0.5 × ($63,000 − $60,000) = $1,500. Return on margin = $1,500 / $6,000 = 25 percent.

Inverse: long 30,000 contracts of $1 each. PnL in BTC = 30,000 × (1/60,000 − 1/63,000) = 0.5 − 0.476190 = 0.023810 BTC. At $63,000, that is worth 0.023810 × 63,000 = $1,500.

The dollar result is the same here, but the inverse trader holds margin in BTC, so the value of their collateral also rose. On a falling market the reverse happens: the position loses BTC while each BTC is worth less, so losses in dollar terms compound. That makes inverse longs more dangerous than linear longs at the same leverage. An inverse short at 1x, by contrast, roughly locks in the dollar value of the BTC posted as margin, which is why some holders use it as a hedge.

Insurance funds and auto-deleveraging

When a position is liquidated, the exchange tries to close it at better than the bankruptcy price (the price at which the trader's margin hits zero). If it succeeds, leftover margin often goes into an insurance fund. If the market gaps and the position closes worse than bankruptcy, the insurance fund covers the shortfall.

If the insurance fund cannot cover losses, many exchanges use auto-deleveraging (ADL): they forcibly close part of the positions of profitable traders on the opposite side, ranked by profit and leverage, at the bankruptcy price of the liquidated trader. ADL indicators on trading screens show where you sit in the queue. Some venues instead socialize losses across all traders or use a protocol-owned liquidity pool as backstop. ADL events have occurred during extreme moves, including the October 2025 crash, so a profitable hedged position can still be closed unexpectedly.

Perp DEXs

Decentralized perpetual exchanges let traders use perps from a self-custody wallet. Designs include:

  • On-chain or app-chain order books that match orders much like a centralized exchange. Hyperliquid and dYdX are well-known examples.
  • Pool-based models in which traders take positions against a liquidity pool that acts as counterparty, priced by oracles. GMX is a well-known example.

Perp DEXs grew rapidly from 2023 onward. They remove the risk of a centralized exchange misusing deposits, but add oracle risk, smart contract risk, and in some designs, risk that liquidity providers or a protocol vault take large losses. Transparency is a double-edged sword: positions and liquidation levels are publicly visible, which other traders can target. See CEX vs DEX.

Regulation and access

Most perp volume has historically traded on offshore exchanges. Many regulators restrict or ban retail access to high-leverage crypto derivatives, and rules differ widely: some countries allow perps through licensed venues, others limit leverage, and others ban them for retail. Some regulated venues have begun offering perpetual-style futures in recent years. Check the current rules where you live. For a comparison with exchange-traded futures, CFDs and stocks, see futures vs other instruments.

Common mistakes

  • Watching the last price instead of the mark price when judging liquidation risk.
  • Ignoring funding on positions held for days or weeks.
  • Using inverse contracts without realizing the margin itself is volatile.
  • Using maximum leverage because it is available. Exchange limits are not recommendations.
  • Assuming a hedged position is safe from ADL or exchange failure.
  • Holding large balances on a derivatives exchange that are not needed as margin.

FAQ

What is the difference between perpetual futures and regular futures?

Regular futures have an expiry date and converge to the spot price at settlement. Perpetual futures never expire and use periodic funding payments to stay close to spot.

Why is the mark price different from the last price?

The last price is the latest trade on one exchange's order book, which can spike briefly. The mark price is based on an index of spot prices across exchanges and is used for liquidations so that a single wick does not trigger unfair liquidations.

What happens if the insurance fund runs out?

The exchange typically uses auto-deleveraging, closing part of the positions of profitable traders on the other side at the liquidated trader's bankruptcy price. Some venues use other loss-sharing methods described in their rules.

Are perpetual futures legal?

It depends on the country. Many jurisdictions restrict retail access to crypto derivatives or cap leverage, and rules have been changing, so check what applies where you live and whether the exchange is licensed there.
