Crypto Liquidations

A liquidation is the forced closure of a leveraged position when the trader's margin falls below the level the exchange requires. In crypto, where perpetual futures are traded at high leverage around the clock, liquidations happen constantly, and in sharp moves they can trigger chains of forced selling or buying that drive prices much further than fundamentals would. Knowing exactly where your liquidation price sits, and why, is basic survival for anyone using leverage.

Key takeaways

  • Liquidation happens when your equity in a position falls to the maintenance margin, not when it reaches zero.
  • At 10x leverage, a move of roughly 9.5 percent against you can liquidate an isolated position; at 50x, roughly 1.5 percent.
  • Cross margin pushes the liquidation price further away but puts the whole account at risk.
  • Liquidation cascades happen when forced orders push price into the next cluster of liquidation levels.
  • The most reliable way to avoid liquidation is lower leverage plus a stop-loss well before the liquidation price.

How liquidation works

When you open a leveraged position on a perpetual future, you post initial margin. The exchange also sets a maintenance margin rate (MMR), a smaller percentage of the position's value you must keep as equity. Equity is your margin plus unrealized profit or loss, measured using the mark price.

When equity falls to the maintenance requirement, the exchange's liquidation engine takes over the position and closes it. Depending on the venue, you lose all of the position's margin or what remains after a liquidation fee. Some exchanges first try a partial liquidation to bring the position back above maintenance.

The general mechanics of leverage and margin apply across markets. What makes crypto distinctive is the leverage available (often 50x to 100x or more on some contracts), the 24/7 market, and the speed at which liquidations feed into price.

Liquidation price math

The following examples use a linear (USDT-margined) contract with isolated margin, a maintenance margin rate of 0.5 percent, and ignore fees and funding. Real exchanges use tiered MMRs and may deduct fees, which move the liquidation price slightly closer to entry.

Long position

A trader buys 1 BTC on a perp at $60,000 with 10x leverage.

  • Notional: $60,000. Initial margin: $6,000.
  • At price P, equity = $6,000 + (P − $60,000).
  • Maintenance requirement = 0.005 × P.

Liquidation occurs when equity equals the requirement:

6,000 + P − 60,000 = 0.005P 0.995P = 54,000 P = $54,271.36

Check: at $54,271.36, equity = 6,000 − 5,728.64 = $271.36, and 0.5 percent of $54,271.36 is $271.36. The position is liquidated after a fall of about 9.55 percent, not the 10 percent that "10x" suggests.

Short position

The same trader instead shorts 1 BTC at $60,000 with 10x leverage.

  • At price P, equity = $6,000 + ($60,000 − P).

6,000 + 60,000 − P = 0.005P 66,000 = 1.005P P = $65,671.64

Check: equity at $65,671.64 = 6,000 − 5,671.64 = $328.36, and 0.5 percent of $65,671.64 is $328.36. The short is liquidated after a rise of about 9.45 percent.

Shortcut formula

For an isolated linear position, the liquidation price is approximately:

  • Long: entry × (1 − 1/leverage + MMR)
  • Short: entry × (1 + 1/leverage − MMR)

For the long above: 60,000 × (1 − 0.1 + 0.005) = $54,300. The approximation is close to the exact $54,271.36 and is fine for a quick check, but your exchange's displayed liquidation price is the one that counts.

How leverage changes distance to liquidation

Using the same 0.5 percent MMR for a long:

Leverage Approximate move to liquidation
2x about 50 percent
5x about 20 percent
10x about 9.5 percent
20x about 4.5 percent
50x about 1.5 percent
100x about 0.5 percent

At 20x, the exact long liquidation price for a $60,000 entry is (60,000 − 3,000) / 0.995 = $57,286.43. Bitcoin regularly moves 4.5 percent in a day, and smaller coins can move that in minutes. At 100x, ordinary noise liquidates positions. Higher maintenance rates on large positions shrink these distances further.

Cross vs isolated margin

With isolated margin, only the margin assigned to a position can be lost. With cross margin, the entire account balance supports all open positions.

Suppose the trader above holds $20,000 in a cross-margin account and opens the same 1 BTC long at $60,000.

20,000 + P − 60,000 = 0.005P 0.995P = 40,000 P = $40,201.01

The liquidation price is much further away, but if it is hit, the trader loses close to the whole $20,000 instead of $6,000. Cross margin also links positions: a loss on one trade can liquidate another. Isolated margin gives a clear, capped loss per trade, which many traders find easier to manage.

Liquidation cascades

Liquidations are market orders. A liquidated long becomes a forced sell; a liquidated short becomes a forced buy. When many positions share similar liquidation levels, forced selling pushes price down into the next cluster, which triggers more forced selling. This is a long liquidation cascade. The upward version is a short squeeze.

Cascades are more likely when:

  • Funding rates are high, showing crowded leveraged positioning.
  • Open interest has risen quickly.
  • Liquidity is thin, such as on weekends or overnight in the main trading regions.
  • A macro shock arrives when markets are already stretched.

On October 10 and 11, 2025, after a US announcement of new tariffs on Chinese goods, crypto markets saw roughly $19 billion of liquidations in 24 hours according to data aggregator Coinglass, mostly long positions and the largest single-day total on record. Some altcoins briefly traded far below their prior prices on individual venues, and some exchanges reported auto-deleveraging. Earlier large cascades occurred in March 2020 and May 2021. See leverage risk and black swans for other tail events.

Open interest and liquidation heatmaps

Open interest (OI) is the total number or value of outstanding derivative contracts. Rising OI means new positions are being opened; falling OI means positions are closing, including through liquidation. A sharp drop in OI during a price crash is a typical sign of a cascade.

Liquidation heatmaps are charts from data providers that estimate where clusters of liquidation levels sit, based on OI changes, typical leverage and price history. Some traders treat these clusters as "magnets" price may hunt. Keep in mind:

  • Heatmaps are estimates. Exchanges do not publish individual traders' liquidation prices, except on transparent perp DEXs.
  • Clusters move as traders open, close and adjust positions.
  • Data can be incomplete; some exchanges report liquidations in throttled form.

Liquidation statistics are also undercounted for the same reason, so treat daily liquidation totals as approximate.

How to avoid being liquidated

  1. Use low leverage. At 2x or 3x, normal volatility rarely reaches the liquidation price.
  2. Size by risk, not by margin. Decide how much you are willing to lose, then size the position so your stop-loss caps that loss. See position sizing.
  3. Place a stop-loss well before the liquidation price. A stop at a planned level loses less than liquidation and avoids liquidation fees. Note that stops can slip in fast markets.
  4. Watch the mark price, not just the last price.
  5. Account for funding and fees, which slowly reduce margin on long-held positions.
  6. Avoid adding margin to a losing position by reflex. It moves the liquidation price but increases the amount at risk.
  7. Keep only trading capital on derivatives venues.

Most retail traders who use high leverage on crypto perps lose money over time. High-leverage trading can also become compulsive. If trading starts to feel like gambling you can't stop, help is available from problem gambling services in many countries.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming 10x means a 10 percent buffer. Maintenance margin and fees make it smaller.
  • Using cross margin without realizing the whole balance is exposed.
  • Placing stops beyond the liquidation price, where they never trigger.
  • Trading around major news or weekends at high leverage.
  • Believing liquidation heatmaps are precise.

FAQ

How is liquidation price calculated?

For an isolated linear long, it is roughly entry × (1 − 1/leverage + maintenance margin rate). For a short, it is entry × (1 + 1/leverage − maintenance margin rate). Exchanges also factor in fees and tiered margin rates, so use the exchange's displayed figure.

What happens to my money when I get liquidated?

You lose the margin backing the position, or nearly all of it, after any liquidation fee. With isolated margin that is limited to the position's margin; with cross margin it can be most of your account balance.

Is cross margin or isolated margin better?

Isolated margin caps the loss on each position, which makes risk easier to control. Cross margin moves the liquidation price further away but exposes the whole account, and one bad trade can affect all open positions.

What causes a liquidation cascade?

A price move triggers forced liquidations, which are executed as market orders that push price further and trigger more liquidations. Cascades are most likely when leverage, funding and open interest are high and liquidity is thin.
