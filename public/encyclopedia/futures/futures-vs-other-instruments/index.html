Futures vs Stocks, CFDs and Perpetuals

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Futures Trading

  • Index Futures (ES, NQ)
  • Commodity Futures
  • Contango, Backwardation & Rollover
  • Futures vs Stocks, CFDs & Perps

Futures, stocks, CFDs and crypto perpetual futures can all give exposure to the same market, but they differ in leverage, costs, who stands behind the trade, how they are regulated and how they are taxed. For a trader who wants exposure to the S&P 500, for example, an index ETF, an index CFD, an E-mini future and a perpetual on a crypto exchange can behave very differently over the same holding period. Choosing the right instrument is a question of fit, not of which is "best".

Key takeaways

  • Exchange-traded futures are centrally cleared, which removes most of the counterparty risk found in CFDs and offshore crypto derivatives.
  • Futures leverage is built into the contract, has no daily financing charge and is usually far higher than stock margin allows.
  • In the US, regulated futures are generally taxed under Section 1256's 60/40 rule and are not subject to the stock pattern day trader rule.
  • CFDs are flexible and widely available outside the US, but they carry financing costs and dealer counterparty risk, and are not offered to US retail traders.
  • Crypto perpetuals never expire, but holders pay or receive funding, and the venue's own rules on liquidation and insurance matter.

Leverage and margin

With stocks, leverage comes from borrowing. In a US margin account, Regulation T generally limits initial borrowing to 50 percent of a stock purchase, which is about 2 to 1 leverage overnight. Intraday buying power can be higher for qualifying accounts.

With futures, the contract itself is leveraged. You post a performance bond that is a small fraction of notional value, and you pay no interest on the remainder. The financing cost is still there, but it is built into the futures price as the basis, as explained in index futures.

CFDs offer leverage set by the broker within local rules. In the European Union, regulators cap retail CFD leverage, for example at 30 to 1 on major currency pairs and 20 to 1 on major stock indices, and require brokers to disclose the share of retail accounts that lose money. Those disclosures typically show that most retail CFD accounts lose.

Crypto perpetuals can offer very high leverage on some venues. High leverage shrinks the distance to liquidation to a small price move.

Costs

Worked example: holding $50,000 of exposure for 30 days

Assume a trader wants about $50,000 of long exposure to a stock index for 30 days. The figures below are illustrative; actual rates vary by broker, venue and market conditions.

  • Futures: two MES contracts at an index level of 5,000 give 2 x 5,000 x $5 = $50,000 of exposure. Costs are a round-trip commission and exchange fees per contract, often a few dollars, plus the spread. The financing cost is embedded in the basis rather than charged as a separate line.
  • CFD: suppose the broker charges a benchmark rate plus 2.5 percent, for 7 percent a year. Financing for 30 days is $50,000 x 7% x 30 / 365 = about $287.67, plus the spread.
  • Crypto perpetual: suppose funding averages 0.01 percent per 8-hour interval, paid by longs. That is $50,000 x 0.0001 = $5 per interval, three times a day, so $15 a day and $450 over 30 days, plus trading fees. Funding changes constantly and can turn negative, so this figure can be much higher or lower. See funding rates.

The key difference is that CFD financing and perp funding are explicit, recurring charges, while futures fold the cost of carry into the price you trade at. Over short holding periods, spreads and commissions usually matter most. Over long ones, financing dominates. See trading costs and fees.

Clearing and counterparty risk

When you trade a regulated future, the clearinghouse guarantees performance. It collects margin from every member firm, marks positions to market daily and holds default funds. Customer funds at regulated US futures brokers must be held in segregated accounts.

With a CFD, your counterparty is the broker. If the broker fails, your recovery depends on local client money rules and any compensation scheme. With a crypto exchange, you rely on the exchange's solvency and honesty, as the 2022 FTX collapse showed. Perpetual venues may also use auto-deleveraging, which can close profitable positions to cover losses that the insurance fund cannot absorb.

The US pattern day trader rule

In the US, FINRA's pattern day trader (PDT) rule required margin accounts that made four or more day trades within five business days to keep at least $25,000 in equity. FINRA replaced it with an intraday margin standard effective June 4, 2026, and brokers may phase the change in until October 2027, so check your broker's current rules. Either way, it is a securities rule. It does not apply to futures accounts, which is one reason US day traders with smaller accounts turn to micro index futures. See day trading stocks.

Taxation in the US: Section 1256

Most regulated futures traded by US taxpayers are "Section 1256 contracts" under the Internal Revenue Code. Described in general terms:

  • 60/40 treatment: gains and losses are treated as 60 percent long-term and 40 percent short-term capital gains, no matter how long the position was held.
  • Year-end mark-to-market: open positions are treated as if sold at fair market value on the last business day of the year.

Worked example

A US trader makes a net $10,000 from futures day trades held for minutes at a time. Under Section 1256, $6,000 is treated as long-term and $4,000 as short-term. As an illustration using 20 percent and 37 percent as top federal long-term and ordinary rates, the blended top rate would be 0.6 x 20% + 0.4 x 37% = 12% + 14.8% = 26.8 percent, compared with 37 percent if the full amount were short-term. Actual rates depend on income, filing status, state taxes and current law.

Stocks held a year or less are taxed as short-term gains. Tax treatment of CFDs, spread bets and crypto derivatives depends on the country and changes over time. Spread betting in the UK, for example, has historically been free of capital gains tax for most individuals. This section is general information, not tax advice; see trading taxes overview and consult a qualified professional.

Which instrument fits which use

Goal Instruments often used Why
Long-term investing Stocks and ETFs No leverage needed, no roll or financing costs
Intraday index trading Index futures, CFDs outside the US Deep liquidity and leverage; futures add central clearing
Commodity exposure Futures, ETFs, CFDs Futures are the primary market; ETFs and CFDs are wrappers
Small, flexible position sizes CFDs, micro futures CFDs allow fractional sizing; micros allow small contract sizes
Crypto exposure around the clock Spot crypto, perpetuals, CME crypto futures Perps trade 24/7; CME contracts trade on futures hours with clearing

Common mistakes

  • Comparing margin instead of notional. Lower margin means higher leverage, not lower risk.
  • Ignoring financing on CFDs and perps. Daily charges add up over weeks and months.
  • Assuming all "futures" are the same. A regulated CME contract and an offshore perpetual differ in clearing, oversight and customer protections.
  • Relying on tax rules from memory. Rules differ by country and change; check current law before planning around them.
  • Choosing an instrument for leverage alone. Most retail traders using high leverage lose money; leverage magnifies whatever edge, or lack of edge, a trader has.

FAQ

Are futures better than CFDs?

Neither is better in general. Futures offer central clearing, transparent exchange pricing and no explicit financing charge, while CFDs offer smaller sizes and access to many markets in one account, but carry broker counterparty risk and daily financing.

Does the pattern day trader rule apply to futures?

No. The US PDT rule applies to securities margin accounts, not to futures accounts regulated by the CFTC and NFA.

What is the 60/40 rule for futures?

Under US Section 1256, gains and losses on regulated futures are treated as 60 percent long-term and 40 percent short-term capital gains regardless of holding period. Tax rules change, so check current law and seek professional advice.

What is the difference between futures and perpetual futures?

Traditional futures have an expiry date and converge to spot at expiry. Perpetual futures never expire and use periodic funding payments between longs and shorts to keep their price close to spot.
