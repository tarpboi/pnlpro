Index Futures

8 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Futures Trading
  • Futures Basics
  • Index Futures (ES, NQ)
  • Commodity Futures
  • Contango, Backwardation & Rollover
  • Futures vs Stocks, CFDs & Perps

Stock index futures are futures contracts whose value tracks a stock market index such as the S&P 500, Nasdaq-100, Dow Jones Industrial Average, DAX or Nikkei 225. They let traders take a view on an entire market in one trade, nearly 24 hours a day, with central clearing and high leverage. The CME's E-mini and Micro E-mini contracts are among the most actively traded futures in the world and a common choice for day traders.

Key takeaways

  • Index futures are cash-settled and track an index through a fixed dollar multiplier, such as $50 per point for the E-mini S&P 500.
  • Micro E-mini contracts are one-tenth the size of the E-minis, which makes position sizing much easier for smaller accounts.
  • The futures price normally sits above or below the cash index by an amount set by interest rates and expected dividends, called fair value.
  • Trading runs almost around the clock on weekdays, so major news outside US stock hours is priced into futures first.
  • The leverage that attracts day traders is the same thing that makes losses fast and large.

The main US index futures

The CME Group lists the most widely traded US contracts. "E-mini" refers to the electronically traded contracts that replaced older, larger floor-traded versions. "Micro E-mini" contracts, launched by CME in 2019, are one-tenth the size of the E-minis.

Contract Symbol Multiplier Tick size Tick value
E-mini S&P 500 ES $50 x index 0.25 point $12.50
Micro E-mini S&P 500 MES $5 x index 0.25 point $1.25
E-mini Nasdaq-100 NQ $20 x index 0.25 point $5.00
Micro E-mini Nasdaq-100 MNQ $2 x index 0.25 point $0.50
E-mini Dow ($5) YM $5 x index 1 point $5.00
Micro E-mini Dow MYM $0.50 x index 1 point $0.50
E-mini Russell 2000 RTY $50 x index 0.10 point $5.00
Micro E-mini Russell 2000 M2K $5 x index 0.10 point $0.50

All of these list quarterly expiries in March (H), June (M), September (U) and December (Z), and settle in cash. Contract specs can change, so confirm them on the exchange's site before trading. For general contract mechanics, see futures basics.

Notional value

Notional value is the index level times the multiplier. It is the real size of the position, regardless of how little margin you post.

Contract Example index level Notional value
ES 5,000 $250,000
MES 5,000 $25,000
NQ 18,000 $360,000
MNQ 18,000 $36,000

A trader holding one NQ contract has more index exposure than one holding one ES contract at these levels, even though the NQ multiplier is smaller, because the Nasdaq-100 index number is much higher. The Nasdaq-100 also tends to move more in percentage terms, so NQ positions usually swing more dollars per day.

International index futures

Index futures exist on most major markets.

  • DAX (Germany): Eurex lists the full-size DAX future at €25 per index point and a Mini-DAX at €5 per index point.
  • Euro Stoxx 50 (Eurozone): Eurex's Euro Stoxx 50 future is €10 per index point with a one-point tick, and is one of the most traded equity index futures in Europe.
  • Nikkei 225 (Japan): the Osaka Exchange lists yen-denominated Nikkei 225 futures in several sizes, and CME lists Nikkei contracts in both yen and US dollars, including a dollar-denominated contract at $5 times the index.
  • Others: FTSE 100 futures trade on ICE Futures Europe, Hang Seng futures on Hong Kong Exchanges, and many other national indices have their own contracts.

These contracts are often used alongside the cash index for hedging, and they help price the market before the local stock exchange opens. Index construction and weighting methods are covered in stock index trading.

Trading hours and the overnight session

CME equity index futures trade on the Globex electronic platform from Sunday evening to Friday afternoon US time, with a daily one-hour break starting at 5:00 p.m. Eastern Time. The period that matches US stock exchange hours (9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. ET) is often called regular trading hours (RTH). The rest is called the overnight or electronic trading hours (ETH) session.

The overnight session matters for three reasons:

  1. News reacts in futures first. Earnings released after the close, central bank decisions abroad and geopolitical events are priced into ES and NQ hours before US stocks open.
  2. Liquidity is thinner. Spreads are usually still one tick in ES, but the order book has fewer contracts, so large orders can move price more. See bid-ask spread and liquidity.
  3. Gaps happen in the cash market, not always in futures. A stock index can "gap" at the open while the futures traded through the move gradually overnight.

Eurex and Osaka have their own sessions, including extended evening hours. Session details change, so check the exchange's current schedule, and see market hours and sessions for an overview.

Fair value vs the cash index

The futures price is not the same as the index level. The gap between them is called the basis. In theory, the futures should trade at fair value, which reflects the cost of buying all the stocks in the index today and holding them until expiry:

  • You pay interest to finance the stocks, which pushes fair value above the index.
  • You collect dividends while you hold them, which pushes fair value below the index.

A simplified formula is:

Fair value = Index x (1 + (interest rate - dividend yield) x days to expiry / 365)

Worked example

Suppose the S&P 500 cash index is at 5,000, the short-term interest rate is 4.5 percent a year, the index dividend yield is 1.3 percent, and there are 90 days to expiry.

  • Net carry rate: 4.5% - 1.3% = 3.2%
  • Time fraction: 90 / 365 = 0.2466
  • Carry: 3.2% x 0.2466 = 0.789%
  • Fair value: 5,000 x 1.00789 = about 5,039.45

So the futures "should" trade near 5,039.45, about 39.45 points above the cash index. If futures traded well above fair value, arbitrageurs could sell futures and buy the stocks; if well below, the reverse. This index arbitrage keeps the basis close to fair value most of the time. The basis shrinks toward zero as expiry approaches, which is why the next quarterly contract trades at a different price from the front month. Rolling between them is covered in contango, backwardation and rollover.

Pre-market TV coverage that says "futures are up 20 points" sometimes means the raw futures price and sometimes means futures relative to fair value. Those two numbers can differ by dozens of points when the expiry is months away.

Expiry and settlement

US equity index futures expire quarterly and are cash-settled. For the E-mini S&P 500, the final settlement is based on a special opening quotation of the index calculated from the opening prices of the index's stocks on the third Friday of the contract month. Most traders roll to the next contract about a week before expiry, when volume shifts to the new front month.

Why day traders favor index futures

Feature What it means in practice
Deep liquidity ES and NQ usually trade with a one-tick spread during US hours
Nearly 24-hour access Traders in Europe and Asia can trade US indices in their own daytime
No pattern day trader rule The US PDT rule applies to stock margin accounts, not futures accounts
Central clearing No dependence on a dealer's own balance sheet for contract performance
Scalable size Micro contracts allow sizing in steps of $5 per S&P point
Tax treatment in the US Gains are generally taxed under Section 1256's 60/40 rule; see futures vs other instruments

The same features have a cost. A single ES contract represents a quarter of a million dollars of index exposure at the levels above, and day-trading margin at some brokers is far below exchange initial margin. A 20-point adverse move in ES, which is ordinary on many days, is a $1,000 loss per contract. Research on retail day traders in several countries has consistently found that the large majority lose money over time, and leverage makes that outcome arrive faster.

Worked example: sizing with MES instead of ES

A trader with a $10,000 account wants to risk no more than 1 percent, or $100, per trade. Their stop is 8 points from entry.

  • With ES: 8 points x $50 = $400 risk per contract. Even one contract is four times the risk limit.
  • With MES: 8 points x $5 = $40 risk per contract. Two contracts risk $80, within the limit.

This is the main reason micro contracts exist. See position sizing for the general method.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing points and ticks. One ES point is four ticks. A "10-tick stop" in ES is only 2.5 points, or $125.
  • Treating NQ like ES. NQ's higher index level and volatility mean larger dollar swings per contract.
  • Trading the expiring contract. Liquidity drains out of the old contract after the roll, so spreads and slippage worsen.
  • Ignoring scheduled news. CPI, jobs reports and central bank decisions can move index futures dozens of points in seconds. See news trading.
  • Holding over the weekend without a plan. The market is closed from Friday afternoon to Sunday evening, and price can open far from Friday's close.

FAQ

What is the difference between ES and MES?

Both track the S&P 500 and have the same 0.25-point tick, but ES is $50 per index point and MES is $5 per index point. One MES is one-tenth of an ES in size, risk and margin.

How much is one point on NQ worth?

One point on the E-mini Nasdaq-100 (NQ) is worth $20, and each 0.25-point tick is $5. On the Micro E-mini Nasdaq-100 (MNQ), one point is $2 and one tick is $0.50.

Why is the futures price different from the S&P 500 index?

The futures price includes the cost of financing the index stocks until expiry minus the dividends they pay. That difference, called fair value or the basis, shrinks to zero at expiry.

When do E-mini futures trade?

CME equity index futures trade nearly 24 hours a day from Sunday evening to Friday afternoon US time, with a daily one-hour break starting at 5:00 p.m. Eastern Time. Check the exchange for holiday schedules.
