Contango, Backwardation and Rollover

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Futures Trading

  • Index Futures (ES, NQ)
  • Commodity Futures
  • Contango, Backwardation & Rollover
  • Futures vs Stocks, CFDs & Perps

Contango and backwardation describe the shape of the futures curve, the line connecting the prices of the same commodity or index for different expiry months. In contango, later contracts cost more than nearer ones; in backwardation, they cost less. The shape matters because anyone holding futures for longer than one contract life must roll from month to month, and the curve decides whether each roll quietly adds to or subtracts from returns.

Key takeaways

  • The term structure is the set of futures prices across expiry months; contango slopes up and backwardation slopes down.
  • Futures prices converge to the spot price at expiry, so a long position in contango tends to lose value relative to spot if the curve stays the same, and gains in backwardation.
  • Rolling means closing the expiring contract and opening a later one, usually as a single calendar-spread order around a predictable roll date.
  • Commodity ETFs that hold futures can trail the spot price badly for long periods when markets are in steep contango.
  • In April 2020, steep contango and full storage pushed the expiring WTI crude contract below zero.

The futures curve

For most futures, several expiry months trade at once. Plotting their prices against time to expiry gives the futures curve, also called the term structure or forward curve.

Contract month Contango example (price) Backwardation example (price)
Front month $70.00 $70.00
2nd month $70.80 $69.30
3rd month $71.50 $68.70
6th month $73.20 $67.10
12th month $75.60 $65.00

The same idea applies to index futures, interest rate futures and VIX futures, though the drivers differ.

Why curves slope the way they do

Contango

For storable commodities, holding the physical good costs money: storage, insurance and the interest on the cash tied up. Together these are called the cost of carry. If later futures were cheaper than spot plus carry, a merchant could buy the commodity, store it and sell a future for a riskless profit. That arbitrage tends to push longer-dated futures above spot, so contango is the "normal" state when supply is ample.

Equity index futures usually trade in mild contango when interest rates exceed dividend yields, because of the financing cost described in index futures.

Backwardation

Backwardation tends to appear when the commodity is scarce right now. Users who need the physical good immediately will pay a premium for nearby delivery. Holders of inventory earn what economists call a convenience yield: the benefit of having the good on hand during a shortage. Crude oil spent long stretches in backwardation in tight markets, such as during parts of 2022.

The steepness carries information

A steep contango often signals oversupply and filling storage. A steep backwardation often signals tight supply. Traders watch spreads between months, such as the price of the first month minus the second, as a real-time gauge of physical market conditions. See oil and energy trading.

Convergence and roll yield

A futures contract must converge to the spot price at expiry, because at that point it becomes a contract for immediate delivery (or cash settlement at spot). That convergence is the source of roll yield.

Worked example: rolling a long crude position in contango

Suppose spot crude and the front-month contract are both at $70.00, and the next month trades at $71.00. You want to keep one long CL contract (1,000 barrels) for two months.

  1. At the first roll, you sell the expiring contract at $70.00 and buy the next month at $71.00.
  2. A month later, spot crude is still $70.00 and the curve has the same shape. The contract you bought at $71.00 is now the front month and has converged to $70.00.
  3. Your loss on that contract is $1.00 per barrel x 1,000 barrels = $1,000.

The spot price never moved, yet the position lost $1,000. That is negative roll yield. In a backwardated market the effect reverses: if you bought the next month at $69.00 and it converged up to an unchanged spot of $70.00, you would gain $1,000.

Real markets rarely hold still, so roll yield mixes with spot moves. Over long holding periods, though, it can dominate. A curve in 1 percent monthly contango, held unchanged, costs about 12 percent a year before compounding.

How rolling works in practice

The mechanics

To roll, you close the position in the expiring month and open the same position in a later month. Most platforms let you do this as one calendar spread order: for a long, sell the near month and buy the far month at a single net price. This avoids being exposed between the two legs and often gets a tighter price than two separate orders.

When to roll

  • Equity index futures: quarterly contracts expire on the third Friday of March, June, September and December. Volume typically shifts to the next contract about a week before expiry. CME commonly refers to the Thursday eight days before expiry as the roll date.
  • Physically delivered commodities: you must roll before the first notice day or last trading day, whichever comes first, to avoid delivery. Many retail brokers set their own earlier cutoff. See commodity futures.
  • Watch volume and open interest: the contract with the most volume is the one with the best liquidity. Most charting platforms offer a continuous contract that switches automatically, but check how it adjusts past prices.

Continuous contract charts

Because each contract expires, long-term charts splice contracts together. A back-adjusted chart shifts old prices to remove the jump at each roll, which keeps indicators smooth but means old prices on the chart are not prices that actually traded. An unadjusted chart shows real prices but has artificial gaps at each roll. Backtesting on the wrong kind of series is a common source of false results.

Impact on commodity ETFs and ETPs

Many commodity exchange-traded products do not hold the physical commodity. They hold futures and roll them on a schedule. In contango, every roll sells cheaper near contracts and buys pricier far ones, so the fund can trail the spot price for years even when spot ends up roughly flat.

Oil funds that held front-month futures were badly hurt in 2020, and some changed their roll strategy to spread holdings across several months. Volatility products based on VIX futures face the same effect because the VIX futures curve is usually in contango; see volatility and VIX. For ETF structures in general, see ETFs and index trading.

Gold ETFs that hold physical metal are a different design and do not have futures roll costs, though they charge management fees.

April 2020: oil below zero

In March and April 2020, pandemic lockdowns cut oil demand faster than producers could reduce supply. Storage filled quickly, especially at Cushing, Oklahoma, the delivery point for WTI futures. The futures curve went into extreme contango.

On April 20, 2020, the day before the May 2020 WTI contract expired, it settled at minus $37.63 per barrel. Holders of long positions who could not take delivery, because they had no access to storage, had to sell at any price. Buyers effectively demanded payment to take on the obligation to receive oil with nowhere to put it. Later-dated contracts stayed well above zero, which shows the event was mainly about the expiring contract and storage, not about oil being worthless.

Lessons from the episode:

  • Know your contract's delivery terms. Physically delivered contracts carry obligations beyond price risk.
  • Roll early. The worst damage hit those still in the front month in its final days.
  • Prices can go negative. Some trading systems, models and broker risk tools had never been built to handle a price below zero.
  • ETFs are not the commodity. Funds tracking front-month oil futures suffered heavy losses from rolling in deep contango.

The broader story of tail events like this is covered in leverage risk and black swans.

Common mistakes

  • Buying a commodity ETF as a long-term proxy for spot. In persistent contango, roll costs can erase much of the spot gain.
  • Forgetting to roll. Leaving it until the last day means thin liquidity and possibly forced liquidation.
  • Rolling with two separate market orders. Legging in and out exposes you to price moves and double spreads. A calendar spread order is usually cleaner.
  • Misreading continuous charts. Back-adjusted prices are not real historical prices.
  • Assuming backwardation means a free return. The curve can flip quickly, and spot moves usually matter more over short horizons.

FAQ

What is the difference between contango and backwardation?

In contango, futures for later delivery are priced higher than nearer contracts or spot. In backwardation, later contracts are priced lower, usually because the commodity is scarce now.

Is contango bullish or bearish?

Neither directly. Steep contango usually indicates ample current supply, while steep backwardation usually indicates tight supply, but the curve says more about physical conditions than about where prices will go next.

What does rolling a futures contract mean?

Rolling means closing a position in the contract that is about to expire and opening the same position in a later month. It is usually done with a calendar spread order around the roll date.

Why did oil prices go negative in April 2020?

The May 2020 WTI contract was about to expire while storage at the Cushing delivery point was nearly full. Holders who could not take physical delivery had to pay others to take the contracts, and the contract settled at minus $37.63 per barrel on April 20, 2020.
