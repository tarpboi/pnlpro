Futures Trading Basics

9 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Futures Trading

A futures contract is a standardized, exchange-traded agreement to buy or sell a fixed quantity of an asset at a set price on a future date. Futures exist on stock indices, interest rates, currencies, energy, metals, grains, livestock and crypto, and they are among the most heavily traded instruments in the world. Because a small deposit controls a large position, futures can magnify both gains and losses, which makes understanding the contract mechanics essential before placing a trade.

Key takeaways

  • A futures contract fixes the quantity, quality, expiry and settlement method, so every contract on an exchange is interchangeable with every other.
  • Each contract has a tick size and tick value, and those two numbers tell you exactly how much money a price move is worth.
  • Gains and losses are settled in cash every day through mark-to-market, so a losing position can trigger a margin call long before expiry.
  • Most contracts are closed or rolled before expiry; some settle in cash and some require physical delivery.
  • Futures margin is a performance bond, not a loan, and the leverage it provides is large enough to wipe out an account quickly.

What a futures contract is

At its core, a futures contract is a promise. The buyer (the "long") agrees to take the asset at the agreed price on the expiry date, and the seller (the "short") agrees to deliver it. Neither side pays the full price up front. Instead, both post margin with their broker, and the exchange's clearinghouse stands between them.

That clearinghouse is a big part of why futures work. Once a trade is matched, the clearinghouse becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer. You do not need to trust the person on the other side of your trade, only the clearinghouse and your broker. This is called central clearing, and it is one of the main differences between futures and over-the-counter products such as forwards or many CFDs.

Futures evolved from forward contracts used by farmers and merchants. A forward is a private, customized deal. A future is the same idea made standard and tradable on an exchange, which lets anyone enter or exit at any time during trading hours.

The major futures exchanges

Exchange group Location Well-known contracts

Others Worldwide Singapore Exchange, Hong Kong Exchanges, B3 in Brazil, NSE in India

Most retail futures traders access these markets through a futures broker, sometimes called a futures commission merchant (FCM) in the US, or through an introducing broker that clears through one.

Contract specifications

Every futures contract has a published specification sheet. The fields that matter most to a trader are:

  • Underlying and size: what the contract represents. One CME WTI crude oil contract (CL) is 1,000 barrels. One COMEX gold contract (GC) is 100 troy ounces.
  • Multiplier: for index futures, the dollar value of one index point. The E-mini S&P 500 (ES) is $50 times the index.
  • Tick size: the smallest allowed price change.
  • Tick value: the profit or loss from a one-tick move on one contract. Tick value equals tick size multiplied by contract size or multiplier.
  • Contract months and expiry: which months are listed and when trading stops.
  • Settlement method: cash or physical delivery.
  • Trading hours: many CME contracts trade nearly 24 hours a day, five days a week, with a short daily maintenance break. See market hours and sessions.

Expiry months and month codes

Futures are listed for specific delivery months. Traders and data feeds identify each month with a single letter, combined with the root symbol and the year. For example, ESZ6 is the E-mini S&P 500 expiring in December 2026, and CLF7 is WTI crude for January 2027 delivery.

Month Code Month Code
January F July N
February G August Q
March H September U
April J October V
May K November X
June M December Z

Equity index futures list quarterly months (March, June, September, December). Crude oil lists every month. Grains follow crop-related cycles, such as March, May, July, September and December for corn. The contract with the most volume, usually the nearest one, is called the front month. Moving a position from one month to the next is called rolling, covered in contango, backwardation and rollover.

Daily mark-to-market

Futures profits and losses are not left to build up until you close. At the end of each trading day the exchange sets a settlement price, and every open position is revalued at that price. Winners have cash added to their account, and losers have cash taken out. This is called mark-to-market, or variation margin.

Worked example: one ES contract over three days

Suppose you buy one ES contract at 5,000.00. The contract's notional value is 5,000 x $50 = $250,000.

Day Settlement price Change (points) Daily P&L Running P&L
Entry 5,000.00
Day 1 5,012.50 +12.50 +$625 +$625
Day 2 4,980.00 -32.50 -$1,625 -$1,000
Day 3 4,995.25 +15.25 +$762.50 -$237.50

Each point is worth $50, so a 12.50-point gain is 12.50 x $50 = $625. The 32.50-point fall costs 32.50 x $50 = $1,625. On Day 3, 15.25 points is 61 ticks, and 61 x $12.50 = $762.50. If you had traded one MES contract instead, every figure would be one-tenth as large: the running loss after Day 3 would be $23.75.

Margin: a performance bond

Futures margin is a good-faith deposit that shows you can cover daily losses. It is not a down payment, and you do not borrow the rest of the contract value or pay interest on it.

  • Initial margin is what you must have to open a position.
  • Maintenance margin is the lower level your account must stay above. If losses push the account below maintenance, you get a margin call and must add funds or reduce the position, or the broker may close it for you.
  • Day-trading margin is a lower amount some brokers allow for positions closed before the session ends. It is set by the broker, not the exchange, and it means even more leverage.

Exchanges set and change margin requirements based on volatility, and they often raise them sharply during turbulent markets. Brokers can require more than the exchange minimum.

How much leverage that is

Suppose, purely as an illustration, that initial margin on one ES contract were $15,000 against $250,000 of notional value. That is about 16.7 to 1 leverage. A 1 percent fall in the index (50 points) would cost $2,500, about one-sixth of the margin. A 6 percent fall would cost $15,000 and erase the entire deposit. Real margin levels vary over time, so check your broker's current figures. The general mechanics of leverage are covered in leverage and margin.

Cash settlement vs physical delivery

When a contract expires, it settles in one of two ways.

  • Cash-settled contracts pay or collect the difference between your trade price and a final settlement price. Equity index futures such as ES and NQ settle in cash. No shares change hands.
  • Physically delivered contracts require the short to deliver, and the long to accept, the actual asset at an approved location. WTI crude oil, gold, corn and many Treasury futures are physically delivered.

Retail traders almost never want delivery. Most brokers require customers to close or roll physically delivered contracts before the first notice day, the first date on which a short can announce intent to deliver. Some brokers will liquidate positions automatically ahead of that date. The risks of holding into delivery are covered in commodity futures.

Hedgers vs speculators

Futures markets bring together two broad groups.

Hedgers use futures to reduce a risk they already have. A corn farmer expecting to harvest 50,000 bushels can sell 10 corn contracts in spring to lock in a price. If corn falls by harvest, the futures gain offsets the lower price received for the crop. An airline can buy crude oil or heating oil futures to protect against rising fuel costs. A pension fund can sell index futures to reduce stock exposure quickly without selling individual shares.

Speculators take on price risk in the hope of profit. They include individual traders, proprietary trading firms, hedge funds and commodity trading advisors (CTAs) that run trend-following systems. Speculators supply liquidity that hedgers need, and they bear risk that hedgers want to shed.

The US Commodity Futures Trading Commission publishes a weekly Commitments of Traders report that breaks down open positions by trader type. Some traders use it as a sentiment gauge, described in market sentiment indicators.

Common mistakes

  • Not knowing the tick value. Trading NQ when you meant MNQ makes every move ten times larger. Check the contract and tick value before every order.
  • Sizing on margin rather than notional. The margin deposit says nothing about risk. A single ES contract moves $250,000 worth of index exposure at a price of 5,000.
  • Forgetting expiry and first notice day. Holding a physically delivered contract too long can lead to forced liquidation or delivery obligations.
  • Ignoring overnight sessions. Nearly 24-hour trading means prices can move a long way while you sleep. Stops can be filled far from their trigger price during fast markets or at the Sunday open.
  • Assuming margin is fixed. Exchanges and brokers can raise margin with little notice, often right when the market is moving against you.

Futures are a professional-grade product. Evidence from many markets shows that most retail traders who use high leverage at short horizons lose money, and futures leverage is high. Starting with micro contracts and strict position sizing keeps mistakes affordable while you learn.

FAQ

How much money do you need to trade futures?

It depends on the contract and broker. Micro contracts such as MES have margin requirements a fraction of the full-size E-mini, so they can be traded with a few thousand dollars, but having enough capital to survive normal swings matters more than meeting the minimum.

Do futures expire?

Yes. Every futures contract has a last trading day. Traders who want to keep their exposure close the expiring contract and open the next month, which is called rolling.

Can you lose more than you deposit trading futures?

Yes. Because losses are settled daily and positions are leveraged, a large or gapping move can create a negative account balance, and you are liable to your broker for the deficit.

What is the difference between futures and options?

A futures contract obligates both sides to transact at the agreed price. An option gives the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell, in exchange for paying a premium. See options basics.
