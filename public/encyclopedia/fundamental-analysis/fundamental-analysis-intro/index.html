Fundamental Analysis

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Fundamental analysis is the study of the economic forces behind an asset's price: earnings and cash flows for a company, interest rates and growth for a currency, supply and demand for a commodity. Its central idea is that an asset has a value that can be estimated from these drivers, and that the market price will tend to move toward that value over time. It matters to traders because the biggest and most persistent price moves, over weeks to years, usually trace back to a change in fundamentals or in what the market expects them to be.

Key takeaways

  • Fundamental analysis estimates what an asset should be worth from its underlying drivers, then compares that estimate with the market price.
  • Prices move on the gap between new information and what was already expected, not on whether news is good or bad in absolute terms.
  • The relevant fundamentals differ by asset: cash flows for stocks, interest rates for currencies, inventories for commodities, and adoption and token supply for crypto.
  • Valuation estimates are very sensitive to assumptions, so a small change in the discount rate or growth rate can move "fair value" a lot.
  • Fundamentals are a poor timing tool on their own, which is why many traders pair them with technical analysis or strict risk rules.

Intrinsic value versus price

The price of an asset is whatever the last buyer and seller agreed on. The intrinsic value is an estimate of what the asset is worth based on what it will produce or how scarce it is. The two can differ for long periods. A fundamental analyst looks for cases where the gap is large and has a reason to close.

For a business, value comes from the cash it will generate for its owners in the future, discounted back to today. A dollar received in ten years is worth less than a dollar today because money now can earn interest and because the future is uncertain. The rate used to shrink future cash flows is the discount rate, and it rises when interest rates rise or when the cash flows look riskier.

Worked example: how sensitive "fair value" is

A simple version of a discounted cash flow model is the growing perpetuity formula:

Value = next year's free cash flow per share / (discount rate − growth rate)

Suppose a company is expected to generate $5.00 of free cash flow per share next year, growing 4% a year forever, and an investor requires a 9% return.

Scenario Discount rate Growth rate Value per share
Base case 9% 4% $5.00 / 0.05 = $100.00
Rates rise one point 10% 4% $5.00 / 0.06 = $83.33
Growth one point higher 9% 5% $5.00 / 0.04 = $125.00

A one-point change in a single assumption moves the estimate by about 17% down or 25% up. This is why fundamental estimates come as ranges, why growth stocks with distant cash flows fall hard when interest rates rise, and why two careful analysts can disagree by a wide margin.

Top-down and bottom-up approaches

There are two broad ways to organize fundamental work.

Top-down analysis starts with the global economy and narrows. An analyst first asks where growth, inflation and interest rates are heading, then which countries and currencies benefit, then which sectors, and only last which individual assets. A top-down trader might conclude that falling inflation in Europe will lead the European Central Bank to cut rates, then express that view through bonds, the euro or rate-sensitive stocks.

Bottom-up analysis starts with a single company or asset and works outward. A bottom-up stock picker studies a company's financial statements, competitive position and management, and may hold it whatever the economic backdrop if the business looks undervalued.

Top-down Bottom-up
Starting point Global macro: growth, inflation, rates A single company or asset
Main tools Economic indicators, central bank policy, capital flows Financial statements, valuation ratios, industry analysis
Typical users Macro funds, FX and rates traders, asset allocators Stock pickers, value and growth investors
Main risk Getting the macro call right but picking the wrong instrument Owning a good company in a falling market

Most practitioners blend the two. Even a pure stock picker needs to know when interest rates are moving enough to reprice the whole market.

How fundamentals differ by asset class

Stocks

Equity fundamentals are the most developed. Analysts study revenue, profit margins, cash flow, debt and growth, and compare the share price with those figures using ratios such as price-to-earnings. Quarterly or semiannual earnings reports, depending on the country, are the main scheduled events. Over long horizons, stock returns have historically tracked earnings growth plus dividends, with valuation changes adding large swings in between.

Currencies

A currency has no earnings, so its fundamentals are macroeconomic. The most important is the interest rate set by its central bank relative to other central banks, along with inflation, growth, trade and capital flows, and safe-haven demand during crises. The forex market tends to move most when data or central bank decisions change expectations about future rate paths. See what moves currencies for the full list.

Commodities

Commodity fundamentals are about physical supply and demand: production, weather, inventories, storage costs and the shape of the futures curve. Scheduled reports such as weekly US oil inventory data or monthly crop estimates can move prices sharply. The commodities trading article covers these drivers in detail.

Bonds

Bond prices depend on interest rates, inflation expectations and the credit quality of the issuer. Government bonds of large economies respond mainly to central bank policy and inflation data. See bonds and interest rate trading.

Crypto

Most crypto assets do not produce cash flows, so classic valuation does not apply cleanly. Analysts instead look at network usage, fees, token supply and unlock schedules, holder behavior visible on the blockchain, and liquidity conditions. Tokenomics and on-chain analysis are the closest equivalents to fundamental analysis in crypto. Global liquidity and interest rates have also become important drivers since crypto entered mainstream portfolios.

Expectations matter more than the news itself

Markets are forward-looking. By the time a piece of news is published, traders have usually formed a view of what it will say, and prices already reflect that view. What moves the price is the surprise: the difference between the actual figure and the expectation.

This is why a company can report record profits and see its shares fall, or why a currency can rise after a weak jobs report if the report was less weak than feared. A fundamental trader has to ask two questions: what will happen, and what does the market already believe will happen? An edge exists only where the answer to the second question is wrong.

Combining fundamentals with technical analysis

Fundamental analysis says what might be mispriced. It rarely says when the mispricing will correct, and a market can stay "wrong" longer than a leveraged trader can stay solvent. Technical analysis studies price and volume to judge timing and trend.

A common combined approach:

  1. Use fundamentals to choose a direction or a shortlist. For example, a currency whose central bank is likely to raise rates faster than markets expect.
  2. Use price action or trend tools to time the entry, waiting for the market to start confirming the view.
  3. Set a stop based on where the trade idea is clearly wrong, and size the position so that a loss is affordable.

Some traders work the other way round: they trade technical setups but check the economic calendar to avoid holding positions through major releases.

What the evidence says

The academic record is mixed in an informative way. Large, liquid markets are hard to beat consistently, and most active fund managers have historically underperformed low-cost index funds over long periods after fees. At the same time, researchers have documented long-lived return patterns linked to fundamentals, such as the tendency of cheaper stocks (value) and more profitable companies to outperform over very long horizons, although these effects come with long dry spells. Short-term trading on fundamentals is harder still, because professional desks process scheduled news within milliseconds.

For retail traders the practical lesson is to use fundamentals mainly to understand why markets move and to avoid being on the wrong side of a major shift, rather than expecting to out-analyze institutions on every data point.

Common mistakes

  • Treating fair value as a price target. Estimates rest on assumptions that can be wrong by a wide margin, as the example above shows.
  • Ignoring expectations. Trading the headline ("good news, so buy") without checking what was already priced in.
  • Using fundamentals for short-term timing. Cheap assets can get cheaper for years.
  • Forcing stock-style valuation onto assets without cash flows, such as many crypto tokens.
  • Anchoring on one story. A thesis built on a single variable, such as interest rates, can be overwhelmed by another, such as a recession or a credit event.

FAQ

Is fundamental analysis better than technical analysis?

Neither is universally better. Fundamental analysis is more useful for longer horizons and for understanding why prices move, while technical analysis is more often used for timing and risk control, and many traders combine both.

Can fundamental analysis be used for day trading?

Only in a limited way. Day traders mainly use the economic and earnings calendar to know when volatility is likely, but the long-term value of an asset rarely matters within a single session.

What is the difference between intrinsic value and market price?

Market price is the latest traded price, set by supply and demand. Intrinsic value is an analyst's estimate of what the asset is worth based on its future cash flows or other drivers, and it is only as reliable as the assumptions behind it.

Does fundamental analysis work for crypto?

Partly. Most tokens have no cash flows, so traditional valuation does not apply, but supply schedules, network usage, liquidity and macro conditions are real drivers that analysts study instead.
