Intermarket Analysis

6 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Intermarket analysis studies how different asset classes, such as currencies, government bonds, stocks, commodities and crypto, influence each other. A move in one market often carries information about others: rising bond yields can pressure growth stocks, a strong US dollar can weigh on commodities and emerging markets, and a rush into safe havens can signal stress before it shows up in stock prices. The technical analyst John Murphy popularized the approach in the early 1990s. Its key lesson for modern traders is that these relationships are real but unstable, and they shift with the inflation and policy regime.

Key takeaways

  • Interest rates and the US dollar sit at the center of most cross-asset relationships, because they set the cost of money and the value of the world's main reserve currency.
  • "Risk-on" and "risk-off" describe broad swings in appetite for risky assets versus safe havens.
  • Correlations between assets change over time; the stock-bond relationship, for example, flipped in 2022.
  • Correlated positions in different markets can amount to one large bet, which matters for risk control.
  • Intermarket signals are best used as context and confirmation, not as standalone trade triggers.

The core relationships

The table summarizes widely observed tendencies. Each comes with conditions, discussed below.

Relationship Typical tendency Main reason
Bond yields and growth stocks Rising yields tend to pressure growth and tech stocks Higher discount rates reduce the present value of distant earnings
US dollar and commodities A stronger dollar tends to weigh on dollar-priced commodities Commodities become more expensive in other currencies
Real yields and gold Rising inflation-adjusted yields have often weighed on gold Gold pays no income, so holding it costs more when real yields rise
US dollar and emerging markets A stronger dollar tends to hurt emerging-market stocks, bonds and currencies Many borrowers there owe dollars, and capital flows toward higher US returns
Oil and inflation expectations Higher oil tends to lift inflation expectations and bond yields Energy feeds directly into consumer prices
Stocks and crypto Correlation has tended to be positive since around 2020, strongest with tech stocks Both respond to liquidity and risk appetite
Commodity prices and commodity currencies Higher metals and energy prices tend to support AUD, CAD, NOK and others Exports rise in value

Interest rates and the dollar at the center

The policy rates set by central banks anchor bond yields, and bond yields set the discount rate for every other asset. The US dollar plays a special role because it is the main currency for global trade, commodity pricing and international borrowing. When US yields rise relative to other countries, money tends to flow into dollars, which tightens financial conditions worldwide. The most widely quoted dollar gauge, the US Dollar Index (DXY), is weighted heavily toward the euro (about 58%), followed by the yen, pound, Canadian dollar, Swedish krona and Swiss franc, so it says little about the dollar against emerging-market or Asian currencies other than the yen.

Gold and real yields

For much of the past two decades, gold moved inversely to US real yields, the yield on inflation-protected Treasury bonds. That relationship weakened markedly from 2022 onward, when gold rose despite sharply higher real yields, a shift widely attributed to heavy buying by central banks, especially in emerging economies, and to geopolitical demand. It is a good example of a "reliable" relationship changing when a new type of buyer arrives. The gold and precious metals article covers these drivers.

Copper, gold and growth

Copper is used across construction, power grids and manufacturing, so its price is often read as a gauge of industrial demand. Some analysts track the copper-to-gold ratio as a growth-versus-fear indicator: a rising ratio suggests growth optimism and often coincides with rising bond yields. Supply disruptions at large mines can distort the signal.

Risk-on and risk-off

Risk-on describes periods when investors favor assets that do well in growth and optimism: stocks, especially cyclical and tech, high-yield credit, emerging markets, commodity currencies such as the Australian dollar, and crypto. Risk-off describes flights to safety: government bonds of major economies, the US dollar, the Japanese yen, the Swiss franc and gold.

Traders use simple barometers to read the mood:

  • AUD/JPY: the Australian dollar is tied to commodities and growth, the yen to safety and carry unwinds. A falling AUD/JPY often signals rising risk aversion. See what moves currencies.
  • Credit spreads: the extra yield on corporate bonds over government bonds widens in stress.
  • The VIX: rises when equity investors seek protection. See volatility and VIX.
  • Small caps versus large caps and cyclicals versus defensive sectors.

Risk-off episodes are not uniform. In a stress event centered on the US, such as a debt-ceiling standoff, the dollar may not behave as a safe haven. And the yen's safe-haven role has varied, depending on Japan's interest rates relative to the rest of the world.

Why correlations change

The stock-bond flip

From roughly the late 1990s to 2021, US stocks and government bonds tended to move in opposite directions on most days: when growth fears hit stocks, bonds rallied. This made balanced portfolios such as the classic 60% stocks, 40% bonds mix unusually stable. In 2022, with inflation at multi-decade highs and central banks raising rates aggressively, both fell together.

Worked example: a balanced portfolio in 2022

Using round, approximate figures for 2022, US stocks fell about 18% and a broad US bond index fell about 13%. A 60/40 portfolio:

  • Actual: 0.6 × (−18%) + 0.4 × (−13%) = −10.8% − 5.2% = −16.0%.
  • If bonds had behaved as in earlier stock sell-offs, say +5%: 0.6 × (−18%) + 0.4 × (+5%) = −10.8% + 2.0% = −8.8%.

The bond "hedge" that investors relied on for two decades turned into a second source of loss. The reason was regime: when inflation is the main risk, rising rates hit both stocks and bonds. When growth is the main risk, bonds tend to hedge stocks.

Other drivers of change

  • Policy regime: QE periods compressed volatility and lifted many assets together.
  • New participants: crypto's correlation with equities rose as institutions and ETFs brought it into mainstream portfolios.
  • Crises: in sharp sell-offs, correlations between risky assets tend to jump toward one, as traders sell whatever they can. See correlation and portfolio risk.
  • Specific shocks: an oil supply disruption can push oil up and stocks down at the same time, the opposite of a demand-driven oil rally.

Using intermarket analysis in practice

  1. Confirmation. A breakout in stocks is more convincing when credit spreads are tightening and cyclical sectors lead. A rally that safe havens refuse to confirm deserves more caution.
  2. Explaining moves. If gold, bonds and the yen rally while stocks drop, the driver is probably risk aversion, not something specific to one market.
  3. Avoiding hidden concentration. Long Nasdaq futures, long bitcoin and short the yen may look like three trades but behave like one risk-on bet. Size them together.
  4. Watching the regime. Track the rolling correlation between key pairs, such as stocks and bonds, rather than assuming it.

Common mistakes

  • Treating relationships as fixed laws. Almost every cross-asset correlation has broken down for months or years at some point.
  • Confusing correlation with causation. Two markets may both respond to a third driver, such as liquidity.
  • Using short windows. A few weeks of correlation can be noise.
  • Stacking correlated positions and thinking they are diversified.
  • Ignoring the inflation regime, which changes how bonds, stocks and gold relate.

FAQ

What does risk-on and risk-off mean?

Risk-on means investors are buying assets that benefit from growth and optimism, such as stocks and commodity currencies. Risk-off means they are moving into perceived safe havens such as government bonds, the US dollar, the yen, the Swiss franc and gold.

Why does gold go down when the dollar goes up?

Gold is priced in dollars, so a stronger dollar makes it more expensive for buyers using other currencies, which can reduce demand. The relationship is a tendency, and both can rise together during periods of fear.

Why did stocks and bonds fall together in 2022?

High inflation forced central banks to raise interest rates quickly, which pushed bond prices down and also reduced the value of future corporate earnings. When inflation is the main risk, stocks and bonds tend to be positively correlated.

Is bitcoin correlated with the stock market?

Since around 2020 bitcoin has often shown a positive correlation with stocks, especially tech, as both respond to liquidity and risk appetite. The correlation varies over time and can break down around crypto-specific events.
