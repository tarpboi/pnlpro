Economic Indicators

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Fundamental & Macro Analysis

Economic indicators are regularly published statistics that measure the health of an economy: inflation, employment, output, business activity and consumer spending. Traders watch them because they shape what central banks do with interest rates, and interest rate expectations drive currencies, bonds, stocks, gold and increasingly crypto. The market reaction depends less on whether a number is good or bad and more on how far it differs from what economists expected.

Key takeaways

  • Inflation and labor market data usually move markets most, because central banks set policy mainly to control inflation and support employment.
  • Markets react to the surprise, the gap between the actual figure and the consensus forecast, not to the level of the number.
  • Revisions to earlier months, and the details inside a report, can matter as much as the headline.
  • The same indicator can move markets in opposite directions at different times, depending on what central banks are focused on.
  • Every major economy publishes its own version of these reports, so traders of non-US assets need to follow local calendars as well.

Why indicators move markets

Most indicators matter through one channel: what they imply for interest rates. If inflation runs hotter than expected, traders price in higher rates or fewer cuts. Bond yields tend to rise, the currency tends to strengthen (see what moves currencies), and rate-sensitive stocks often fall. A weaker-than-expected jobs report tends to do the opposite. The central banks article explains this transmission in detail.

A second channel is growth. Strong output and spending data can lift stocks on better earnings prospects, even if they also push yields up. Which channel dominates depends on the moment. When inflation is the main worry, good growth news can be bad for stocks. When recession is the main worry, good growth news is usually welcomed.

Inflation: CPI and PCE

The Consumer Price Index (CPI) measures the change in prices of a fixed basket of goods and services bought by households. In the United States it is published monthly by the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Traders look at headline CPI and at core CPI, which excludes food and energy because those prices are volatile. The month-over-month change in core CPI is often watched more closely than the year-over-year rate, because it shows the current pace.

The Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price index, published by the Bureau of Economic Analysis, is the Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge, and its 2% target is defined in PCE terms. PCE covers a broader range of spending and adjusts weights as consumers switch between products. Because CPI and producer price data arrive first, economists can usually forecast PCE closely, so it tends to surprise less.

Other economies publish their own equivalents:

Economy Main inflation report Publisher
Eurozone Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices (HICP), with a flash estimate Eurostat
United Kingdom CPI Office for National Statistics
Japan National CPI, with Tokyo CPI as an earlier read Statistics Bureau of Japan

Employment: payrolls and unemployment

The US Employment Situation report, usually released on the first Friday of the month, is one of the most market-moving releases in the world. It contains:

  • Non-farm payrolls (NFP): the monthly change in jobs, from a survey of employers.
  • The unemployment rate: from a separate survey of households.
  • Average hourly earnings: wage growth, which feeds into services inflation.
  • Revisions to the two previous months, which are sometimes large.

Weekly initial jobless claims give a faster but noisier read on layoffs. Elsewhere, traders follow the UK's labor market report, Canada's Labour Force Survey (often released the same morning as US payrolls), Australia's monthly labor force data and the eurozone unemployment rate.

Output: GDP

Gross domestic product (GDP) measures the total value of goods and services an economy produces. Most major economies report it quarterly. The US publishes an advance estimate about a month after the quarter ends, followed by revised estimates, and quotes growth at an annualized rate. Many other countries, including the eurozone and the UK, quote the plain quarter-over-quarter change, so the same underlying growth looks smaller in their headlines. Always check which convention a figure uses.

GDP is comprehensive but backward-looking. By the time it is published, monthly data have usually told traders most of what it will say, so its market impact is often smaller than its importance suggests.

Business surveys: PMIs

Purchasing Managers' Indexes (PMIs) survey company managers about orders, output, employment, prices and supplier delays. A reading above 50 signals expansion compared with the previous month, below 50 signals contraction. PMIs are timely, often published within days of the month ending, and the "flash" estimates come out before the month is even over.

In the US, the Institute for Supply Management (ISM) publishes manufacturing and services PMIs. S&P Global publishes PMIs for dozens of economies, including flash readings for the eurozone, the UK, Japan, Australia and India. China's National Bureau of Statistics publishes an official PMI, and private surveys provide a second view. Other closely watched surveys include Germany's Ifo and ZEW indexes and Japan's quarterly Tankan survey from the Bank of Japan.

Consumers: retail sales and confidence

Retail sales measure monthly spending at stores and online, a direct read on consumption, which is the largest part of most developed economies. Traders often focus on the "control group" or core measure that excludes volatile items such as autos and fuel.

Consumer confidence surveys, such as the University of Michigan survey and the Conference Board index in the US or the GfK survey in Germany and the UK, ask households how they feel about the economy. The Michigan survey also asks about inflation expectations, which central banks watch closely.

Consensus versus actual

Before each release, news services poll economists and publish a consensus forecast, usually the median. The economic calendar shows the previous figure, the consensus and, once released, the actual.

Worked example: a payrolls surprise

Suppose the consensus for US non-farm payrolls is +150,000 jobs and the unemployment rate is expected at 4.2%.

Item Consensus Actual Surprise
Payrolls +150,000 +250,000 +100,000
Unemployment rate 4.2% 4.0% 0.2 points lower
Prior month revision none -60,000 weaker past data

The headline is a large upside surprise, so a typical first reaction would be a stronger dollar and higher short-term Treasury yields as traders price fewer rate cuts. But the 60,000 downward revision means that, over the two months combined, the labor market added only 40,000 more jobs than previously thought (+100,000 surprise minus 60,000 revision). Traders who read the details may fade part of the initial move. This is common: the first move reflects the headline, and the next few minutes reflect the details.

Note also that even a correct forecast does not guarantee a profitable trade, a point covered in fundamental analysis. If the market had a "whisper" expectation above the published consensus, a figure that beats consensus can still disappoint.

Typical reactions, in general terms

Surprise Bonds Currency Stocks Gold
Inflation higher than expected Yields up, prices down Tends to strengthen Often down Mixed, often down short term
Jobs stronger than expected Yields up Tends to strengthen Mixed Often down
Growth weaker than expected Yields down Tends to weaken Mixed: weaker earnings vs lower rates Often up

These are tendencies, not rules. In a recession scare, bad data can hit stocks hard. When a central bank is near the end of a hiking cycle, the same data can produce a different reaction.

Common mistakes

  • Reading only the headline. Revisions, core measures and sub-components often change the meaning.
  • Ignoring the consensus. A "strong" number that was fully expected can produce no move or a reversal.
  • Comparing figures across countries without checking the method, such as annualized versus quarterly GDP.
  • Holding leveraged positions through top-tier releases without a plan. Spreads widen and prices can gap past stops. See news trading.
  • Assuming the relationship is fixed. Whether good data is good for stocks depends on the policy backdrop.

FAQ

Which economic indicator moves markets the most?

In recent years US inflation (CPI) and the monthly jobs report have usually produced the largest moves, followed by central bank decisions. Their importance shifts with what the Federal Reserve is focused on.

What is the difference between CPI and PCE?

Both measure consumer inflation in the US. CPI is published first and uses a fixed basket of household purchases, while PCE covers a broader range of spending, updates its weights more often, and is the measure behind the Federal Reserve's 2% target.

What does a PMI above 50 mean?

It means more surveyed companies reported improvement than deterioration compared with the previous month, which signals expansion. Below 50 signals contraction.

Why do markets fall on good economic news?

Because strong data can push up expectations for interest rates, which lowers the value of future earnings and tightens financial conditions. The reaction depends on whether markets are more worried about inflation or about growth.
