Market Sentiment Indicators

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Fundamental & Macro Analysis

Sentiment indicators measure how optimistic or fearful market participants are, and how they are positioned. They draw on futures positioning reports, options activity, volatility, surveys and, in crypto, derivatives data such as funding rates. Traders use them mostly in a contrarian way: when almost everyone is already bullish and fully invested, there are fewer buyers left, and the market becomes vulnerable to bad news. Sentiment is a context tool, not a timing signal, because crowds can stay one-sided for a long time.

Key takeaways

  • Sentiment indicators show how positioned and emotional the market is, which helps judge how much fuel is left for a move.
  • Most sentiment signals are contrarian at extremes and trend-confirming in the middle of their range.
  • Extremes can persist for weeks or months, so sentiment works best combined with price confirmation.
  • Positioning data such as the COT report is delayed, and survey data reflects what people say, not what they do.
  • In crypto, funding rates and open interest give a near real-time view of leveraged positioning.

Why sentiment matters

Prices move when buyers or sellers become more urgent than the other side. If nearly everyone who wants to own an asset already owns it, buying pressure is mostly spent, and any disappointment triggers selling. The reverse holds at moments of panic: forced and fearful sellers have sold, and small amounts of buying can lift prices sharply.

This is the logic of contrarian investing, but it has a catch. Sentiment tends to follow price. In a strong uptrend, sentiment stays bullish for good reasons. Fading every bullish reading in a bull market is a reliable way to lose money. Most practitioners only act on extreme readings, and only once price starts to turn. The psychology behind herd behavior is covered in cognitive biases in trading.

Positioning: the Commitments of Traders report

The US Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) publishes the Commitments of Traders (COT) report weekly, usually on Friday afternoon, showing positions as of the previous Tuesday. It breaks down open interest in US futures markets by trader type.

Report version Main categories Used for
Legacy Commercials (hedgers), non-commercials (large speculators), non-reportable (small traders) Long history, simple view
Disaggregated Producers and merchants, swap dealers, managed money, other reportables Commodities such as oil, gold, grains
Traders in Financial Futures Dealers, asset managers, leveraged funds, other reportables Currencies, stock indices, interest rates

Traders often watch the net position of speculators (longs minus shorts), such as managed money in crude oil or leveraged funds in yen futures. When speculators hold a record net long, the market may be crowded. Commercial hedgers are often on the other side, since producers sell forward when prices are high.

Worked example: a COT index

A common way to normalize COT data is to express the current net position as a percentage of its range over a lookback period, often three years.

  • Managed money in crude oil: 300,000 contracts long, 50,000 short, so net long = 250,000.
  • Over the past three years, the net position ranged from 80,000 to 270,000.
  • COT index = (250,000 − 80,000) / (270,000 − 80,000) = 170,000 / 190,000 = about 89%.

A reading near 90% says speculators are close to their most bullish of the past three years. That does not mean oil will fall, but it means the market has less room for new speculative buying and is more exposed to a long liquidation if the news turns.

Limits: the data is three days old when published, the categories are imperfect, and positions may be hedges against other holdings.

Options-based measures

Put/call ratio

The put/call ratio divides the volume (or open interest) of put options by that of call options. Puts are used to bet on or protect against declines, calls to bet on rises. In the US, Cboe publishes ratios for equity options, index options and the total.

A high ratio, say 1.2 (120 puts for every 100 calls), suggests fear and heavy hedging. A low ratio, say 0.6, suggests complacency or speculative call buying. Contrarians treat very high readings as potentially bullish and very low readings as potentially bearish. Index options are dominated by institutional hedging, so their ratio is normally higher than the equity-only ratio, and the two should not be compared directly. See options basics for how puts and calls work.

VIX and other volatility indexes

The VIX, calculated by Cboe from S&P 500 option prices, measures the market's expectation of volatility over the next 30 days. It tends to rise sharply when stocks fall, which earned it the nickname "fear gauge." Spikes in the VIX have historically coincided with periods of panic selling, and very low readings with calm, complacent markets. Other markets have equivalents, such as the VSTOXX for the Euro Stoxx 50 and the Nikkei Volatility Index for Japan. The volatility and VIX article covers how the index works and how it is traded.

Surveys and composite indexes

  • AAII Sentiment Survey: the American Association of Individual Investors asks members each week whether they are bullish, bearish or neutral on US stocks over the next six months. Readings far from the long-run average have sometimes marked turning points, but the relationship is noisy.
  • Investors Intelligence and similar newsletter surveys track the views of market commentators.
  • Fund manager surveys, published by some large banks, show how professional investors are positioned and which trades they consider most crowded.
  • Europe's Sentix and other regional surveys provide non-US views.
  • Fear and greed indexes combine several measures, such as momentum, breadth, volatility, put/call ratios and safe-haven demand, into a single score. CNN publishes one for US stocks, and several crypto data sites publish crypto versions. They are convenient summaries, but the weighting is arbitrary, so treat them as a rough mood reading.

Crypto sentiment: funding rates and open interest

Crypto offers sentiment data in near real time because so much trading happens in perpetual futures.

Funding rates are periodic payments between long and short holders of perpetual contracts that keep the contract price close to the spot price. Positive funding means longs pay shorts, usually because there is more demand to be long with leverage. Persistently high positive funding signals crowded, leveraged bullishness. Negative funding signals the opposite. See funding rates for the mechanics.

Worked example: what funding costs a crowded long

A trader holds a $10,000 long in a BTC perpetual where funding is +0.03% every eight hours, three payments a day.

  • Cost per payment: $10,000 × 0.03% = $3.00.
  • Cost per day: $3.00 × 3 = $9.00.
  • Annualized rate: 0.03% × 3 × 365 = 32.85% a year.

Readings like this suggest longs are paying heavily for leverage, which has historically often coincided with overheated conditions.

Open interest is the total number of outstanding contracts. Rising open interest alongside rising prices means new leveraged positions are being opened. Very high open interest with high funding often precedes sharp moves when price turns, because liquidations force positions closed. The liquidations article explains the cascade effect.

Social and retail positioning

Some brokers publish the share of their retail clients who are long or short each instrument. Because retail traders often trade against trends, some traders read heavy retail positioning on one side as a contrarian signal. Social media sentiment tools count bullish and bearish mentions, but they are easy to distort through bots and coordinated promotion, especially in small crypto tokens.

Using sentiment well

  1. Look for extremes, not ordinary readings.
  2. Use more than one source; a survey extreme combined with record speculator positioning is stronger evidence than either alone.
  3. Wait for price to confirm, for example a break of a trend line or a key level, before acting.
  4. Treat sentiment as a risk filter: when the crowd is all on one side, reduce leverage on trades that agree with it.

Common mistakes

  • Fading the trend on every bullish or bearish reading. Sentiment stays one-sided during strong trends.
  • Ignoring data delays, such as the three-day lag in the COT report.
  • Comparing indicators without understanding their construction, such as equity-only and index put/call ratios.
  • Trusting social sentiment that can be manufactured.
  • Using a single composite index as a trading signal.

FAQ

What is the best market sentiment indicator?

No single indicator is best. Traders often combine positioning data such as the COT report, options measures such as the VIX or put/call ratio, and surveys, and look for agreement at extremes.

Is the fear and greed index reliable?

It is a useful summary of market mood but not a reliable timing tool. Extreme readings have sometimes marked turning points, but markets can stay at extremes for long periods.

How do you read the COT report?

Focus on the net position of speculators, such as managed money or leveraged funds, and compare it with its own history. Record net long or net short positions suggest a crowded market that may be vulnerable to a reversal.

What does a high put/call ratio mean?

It means more puts are trading relative to calls, which signals fear or heavy hedging. Contrarian traders sometimes see very high readings as a sign that selling is close to exhausted.
