Earnings and Financial Statements

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Fundamental & Macro Analysis

A company's financial statements are its official record of what it earned, what it owns and owes, and how cash moved through the business. Earnings reports present these figures each quarter or half-year, along with management's outlook, and they are the most important scheduled events for individual stocks. Traders who can read the three core statements understand why a stock moves on results, and why "record profits" can still send shares lower.

Key takeaways

  • The income statement shows profit over a period, the balance sheet shows what a company owns and owes at a point in time, and the cash flow statement shows actual cash in and out.
  • Earnings per share (EPS) is net income divided by shares outstanding, and it is the headline figure analysts forecast and the basis of the P/E ratio (see valuation ratios).
  • Stocks react to results versus expectations, including unofficial "whisper" numbers, and often react more to guidance than to the past quarter.
  • Profit and cash are not the same thing; a company can report profits while burning cash.
  • Reporting rules and frequency differ by country, so check what standard and schedule a company follows.

The three core statements

Income statement

The income statement, also called the profit and loss statement, covers a period such as a quarter or a year. It starts with revenue and subtracts costs layer by layer:

  • Revenue (sales): money earned from selling goods or services.
  • Cost of goods sold: the direct cost of producing what was sold.
  • Gross profit: revenue minus cost of goods sold. Gross margin is gross profit divided by revenue.
  • Operating expenses: research, sales, marketing and administration.
  • Operating income: gross profit minus operating expenses. This shows how profitable the core business is.
  • Interest and taxes: subtracted to reach net income.
  • Net income: the "bottom line," the profit attributable to shareholders.

Balance sheet

The balance sheet is a snapshot at a single date. It follows one identity:

Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' equity

Assets include cash, receivables, inventory, property and intangible items such as acquired brands. Liabilities include debt, payables and other obligations. Equity is what would be left for shareholders if all assets were sold at book value and all liabilities paid. Traders use the balance sheet to judge financial strength: how much debt a company carries, whether it has enough cash to survive a downturn, and whether it may need to raise money by issuing new shares, which dilutes existing holders.

Cash flow statement

The cash flow statement reconciles profit with actual cash. It has three sections:

  • Operating cash flow: cash generated by the business, starting from net income and adjusting for non-cash items such as depreciation and for changes in working capital.
  • Investing cash flow: spending on equipment and acquisitions (capital expenditure, or capex) and proceeds from asset sales.
  • Financing cash flow: borrowing, repaying debt, issuing shares, buybacks and dividends.

Free cash flow is usually defined as operating cash flow minus capex. It is the cash available to pay down debt, pay dividends or buy back shares, and many analysts consider it harder to manipulate than net income.

Worked example: from revenue to free cash flow

A company reports these annual figures, in millions of dollars, with 100 million shares outstanding:

Line Amount
Revenue 1,000.0
Cost of goods sold (600.0)
Gross profit 400.0 (40% gross margin)
Operating expenses (250.0)
Operating income 150.0 (15% operating margin)
Interest expense (10.0)
Pre-tax income 140.0
Tax at 21% (29.4)
Net income 110.6

EPS = 110.6 / 100 = $1.106, reported as $1.11.

Now the cash flow side:

Line Amount
Net income 110.6
Add back depreciation (non-cash) 40.0
Increase in working capital (cash tied up in inventory and receivables) (30.0)
Operating cash flow 120.6
Capital expenditure (50.0)
Free cash flow 70.6

The company earned $110.6 million of accounting profit but generated $70.6 million of free cash. That gap is normal for a growing company that is building inventory and investing, but if it widens year after year, analysts start to ask whether profits are turning into cash.

Earnings per share and its variants

Basic EPS divides net income by the average number of shares outstanding. Diluted EPS also counts shares that could be created from stock options, convertible bonds and similar instruments, so it is lower or equal. Many companies also report adjusted or non-GAAP EPS, which excludes items management considers one-off, such as restructuring costs or stock-based compensation. Adjusted figures can be useful but are not standardized, and companies choose what to exclude, so compare them with the official figure.

US companies report under US GAAP (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles). Most other major markets, including the EU, the UK, Australia, Hong Kong and many others, use IFRS (International Financial Reporting Standards). The two are similar in spirit but differ in details such as lease accounting and some revenue rules.

Earnings season and reporting schedules

In the United States, listed companies report quarterly, and the busiest weeks, known as earnings season, start a couple of weeks after each quarter ends, typically opened by the large banks. Most companies release results before the market opens or after it closes, then hold a conference call with analysts.

Schedules differ elsewhere. Many European and UK companies publish full results twice a year, sometimes with shorter quarterly trading updates, because quarterly reporting is not mandatory there. Many Japanese companies have fiscal years ending in March. Check each company's investor relations calendar, and remember that results released outside regular hours can produce large gaps at the next open; see pre-market and after-hours trading. See earnings trading for how traders approach these events.

Beats, misses, guidance and whisper numbers

Before each report, analysts publish forecasts for revenue and EPS, and data providers compile them into a consensus. A company beats when results exceed consensus and misses when they fall short.

Several factors complicate the simple beat-or-miss story:

  • Guidance. Many companies give a forecast for the next quarter or year. A beat on the past quarter combined with weak guidance often sends the stock down, because the market prices the future.
  • Whisper numbers. Investors often expect a beat, since companies tend to guide conservatively, and options prices imply how big a move traders expect (see implied volatility). The unofficial expectation, the whisper number, can sit above consensus, so a small beat may disappoint.
  • Quality of the beat. A beat driven by a lower tax rate or a one-time gain is viewed differently from one driven by revenue growth and wider margins.
  • Key metrics. For some industries, specific figures matter more than EPS: subscriber numbers for streaming companies, same-store sales for retailers, net interest margin for banks, or data-center revenue for chip makers.

In the worked example above, suppose consensus EPS was $1.05. The company beat by about 6 cents, or roughly 6%. If management also cut its revenue forecast for next year below what analysts expected, the stock could still fall sharply when the market opens.

Red flags in financial statements

  • Profits rising while operating cash flow stagnates or falls.
  • Receivables growing much faster than revenue, which can mean customers are slow to pay or sales are being pulled forward.
  • Frequent "one-time" charges that recur every year.
  • Rising debt used to fund dividends or buybacks.
  • Repeated share issuance that dilutes holders, common in small caps; see penny stocks and small caps.
  • Changes of auditor or delayed filings.

Common mistakes

  • Reading only the headline EPS and ignoring revenue, margins, cash flow and guidance.
  • Treating adjusted earnings as equal to official earnings without checking what was excluded.
  • Assuming a beat means the stock will rise. The reaction depends on expectations and guidance.
  • Ignoring the balance sheet, which shows whether a company can survive a bad year.
  • Holding a large position through an earnings release without sizing for a possible gap.

FAQ

What is the most important financial statement?

Each answers a different question, so analysts use all three. The income statement shows profitability, the balance sheet shows financial strength, and the cash flow statement shows whether profits turn into cash, which many investors consider the most revealing.

What does EPS tell you?

Earnings per share is net income divided by shares outstanding, showing how much profit each share represents. It is the basis for the price-to-earnings ratio and the main figure analysts forecast.

Why does a stock fall after beating earnings?

Usually because expectations were higher than the published consensus, the company gave weak guidance, or the beat came from one-off items rather than core growth. Markets price the future, not the past quarter.

What is the difference between GAAP and non-GAAP earnings?

GAAP earnings follow official accounting standards. Non-GAAP or adjusted earnings exclude items management considers unusual, which can clarify underlying performance but can also flatter results.
