News Trading and the Economic Calendar

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Fundamental & Macro Analysis

News trading means taking positions around scheduled releases, such as inflation data or central bank decisions, or around unscheduled headlines, such as geopolitical events. The economic calendar lists the scheduled events with their time, expected impact, consensus forecast and previous value. News moments offer some of the largest short-term moves of any day, but they are also when spreads are widest, fills are worst and professional speed matters most, which makes them one of the hardest environments for retail traders.

Key takeaways

  • The economic calendar tells you when volatility is likely; it does not tell you which way prices will move.
  • At top-tier releases, spreads can widen several times over and stop orders can fill well away from their price.
  • Automated systems react to headlines within milliseconds, so a retail trader cannot win a pure speed race.
  • Most retail news trading falls into three approaches: positioning beforehand, trading the first move, or waiting and trading the reaction after the dust settles.
  • For many traders the most valuable use of the calendar is defensive: knowing when to reduce size, widen stops or stay flat.

Reading an economic calendar

A typical calendar entry shows:

Field What it means
Time Release time, usually shown in your local time zone; check that daylight saving changes are handled
Currency or country Which market is most affected
Event For example "CPI m/m" or "Interest Rate Decision"
Impact Often low, medium or high, based on how much the release has historically moved prices
Previous The last reading, sometimes with a revision
Consensus or forecast The median economist forecast
Actual Filled in at release

High-impact events usually include inflation reports, the US jobs report, central bank decisions and press conferences, GDP, retail sales and PMIs for the largest economies. See economic indicators for what each measures and central banks and interest rates for how policy decisions move markets.

Impact ratings are a rough guide. A "medium" release can become important when it bears directly on the next central bank decision, and a "high" release can be a non-event when the outcome is already obvious.

What happens to the market at a release

In the seconds around a major release, the market changes character:

  • Liquidity thins. Market makers and banks pull or reduce their quotes just before the number, because they do not want to be caught on the wrong side. See bid-ask spread and liquidity.
  • Spreads widen. A forex pair that normally trades at a spread under one pip can show several pips. CFD and retail forex brokers often widen spreads automatically around releases.
  • Slippage grows. Market and stop orders fill at the next available price, which can be far from the order price when the book is thin.
  • Prices can gap. On a large surprise, price may jump past many levels with almost no trading in between, so stop losses can fill well beyond where they were set. See stop-loss strategies.
  • Reversals are common. The first move often reflects the headline number, then partly reverses as traders digest revisions and details.

Headline algorithms

Many large trading firms use systems that read machine-readable news feeds and trade within milliseconds of a release. Some services sell data releases in a format designed for computers, delivered over low-latency connections. By the time a release appears on a retail news site or chart, the first part of the move has often already happened.

This means a retail trader trying to react to a number by clicking a button is competing for the scraps of a move that faster participants have mostly captured. Approaches that do not depend on speed are generally more realistic.

Common approaches

Positioning before the release

The trader forms a view, for example that inflation will come in hotter than the consensus, and takes a position before the number. This is a bet on both the outcome and the market's reaction, and it carries full gap risk. Traders using this approach often size smaller than usual and accept that a stop may not protect them.

The straddle (bracket) approach

The trader places a buy stop above the current price and a sell stop below it, hoping a large move in either direction triggers one side. In practice this suffers from slippage on entry and from whipsaws, when price spikes one way, triggers the order, then reverses and triggers the other side.

Fading the first move

Some traders wait for the initial spike and trade against it if they believe the market has overreacted, for example when the headline was strong but revisions were weak. Fading needs a clear reason and a firm stop, because sometimes the first move is the start of a trend.

Waiting for the reaction

Others wait 15 to 60 minutes for spreads to normalize, then trade the new trend or range using their usual technical method. They give up the first part of the move in exchange for normal costs and clearer information.

Worked example: the cost of trading the spike

A trader uses a straddle on EUR/USD before a US jobs report, with one standard lot (100,000 euros), where one pip (0.0001) is worth $10. EUR/USD is at 1.0850. The trader places a buy stop at 1.0870 and a sell stop at 1.0830, each with a 20-pip stop loss. The normal spread is 0.8 pips; at release it widens to about 6 pips.

Case 1: a clean move. The number beats expectations and price jumps. The buy stop at 1.0870 fills at 1.0874 because of slippage. Price peaks at 1.0895 on the chart, but by the time the trader exits a few minutes later the bid is 1.0880.

  • Profit: 1.0880 − 1.0874 = 6 pips = $60.
  • The chart showed a 25-pip move from the entry level to the high, worth $250, but slippage and the exit spread consumed most of it.

Case 2: a whipsaw. Price spikes up, filling the buy stop at 1.0874, then reverses down as traders read a weak revision.

  • The buy leg's stop at 1.0850 fills at 1.0847: loss of 27 pips = $270.
  • On the way down, the sell stop at 1.0830 fills at 1.0826. Price then rebounds and the sell leg's stop at 1.0850 fills at 1.0852: loss of 26 pips = $260.
  • Total: −$530 on a release where price ended almost where it started.

The arithmetic shows why news strategies that look good on a chart often fail in live trading. Costs that are trivial at normal times become a large share of the move.

Geopolitical and unscheduled news

Wars, elections, sanctions, trade disputes, sovereign downgrades and sudden policy announcements do not appear on any calendar. Typical patterns include flows into perceived safe havens such as the US dollar, Japanese yen, Swiss franc, gold and government bonds, and spikes in oil when supply routes are threatened. These patterns are not reliable. The yen, for example, has not always rallied in crises.

Unscheduled news also tends to arrive over weekends or outside the main session, when many markets are closed. Positions can open on Monday at a very different price from Friday's close. Traders who hold leveraged positions over weekends or through elections should size for that gap risk.

Practical safeguards

  • Check the calendar at the start of each day and each week.
  • Decide in advance whether to trade a release, stay flat or reduce size.
  • Use limit orders where possible and avoid market orders in the seconds after a release.
  • Know your broker's policies: some brokers raise margin requirements or restrict orders around major events, and many prop firm evaluations restrict trading near high-impact news.
  • Account for the fact that a stop loss is an instruction, not a guarantee of price, unless it is a guaranteed stop.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the calendar as a signal. It shows timing, not direction.
  • Underestimating costs. Spreads, slippage and requotes can turn a winning idea into a loss.
  • Trading the headline only, ignoring revisions and details that often reverse the first move.
  • Using too much leverage at the moment price is most likely to gap.
  • Chasing the spike after the move has already happened.

FAQ

Is news trading profitable?

It can be for firms with fast data and execution, but for most retail traders the costs at release time, wide spreads, slippage and whipsaws, make consistent profits hard. Many traders use the calendar mainly to avoid unwanted volatility.

Why do spreads widen during news?

Liquidity providers pull or widen their quotes just before a release to avoid being caught by a sudden move. With fewer orders in the book, the gap between the best bid and ask grows until normal quoting resumes.

What is the best time to trade after a news release?

There is no single best time, but many traders wait at least several minutes, and often 15 to 60, for spreads to return to normal and for the market to show whether the first move is holding.

Do stop losses work during news events?

A regular stop loss becomes a market order once triggered, so in a fast market it can fill well beyond its price. Only a guaranteed stop, offered by some brokers for a fee, fixes the exit price.
