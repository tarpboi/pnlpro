Central Banks and Interest Rates

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026
More in Fundamental & Macro Analysis

A central bank manages a country's or currency area's money: it sets a short-term policy interest rate, controls the size of its balance sheet and acts as lender of last resort to banks. Because interest rates set the price of money for the whole economy, central bank decisions, and expectations about future decisions, are among the most powerful forces in financial markets. Currencies, government bonds, stock indices, gold and crypto all reprice when the expected path of rates changes.

Key takeaways

  • Most major central banks aim for low, stable inflation, usually around 2%, and some also have an explicit employment goal.
  • Markets move on changes in the expected path of rates, so a decision that matches expectations often has little effect while a change in guidance can move prices sharply.
  • Quantitative easing and tightening change the amount of long-term bonds a central bank holds and influence longer-term yields and liquidity.
  • Higher expected rates tend to strengthen a currency and pressure bonds, growth stocks, gold and crypto, though the relationships are not fixed.
  • Rate expectations can be read from futures and swap markets, which price the probability of each outcome.

What central banks do

The core tool is the policy rate, the short-term interest rate at which banks borrow or deposit reserves. Changes in the policy rate pass through to loan rates, mortgage rates, deposit rates and bond yields. Raising rates (tightening or being hawkish) slows borrowing and spending to cool inflation. Cutting rates (easing or being dovish) supports activity.

Rate decisions are usually made by a committee at scheduled meetings, published on a calendar well in advance. The decision is followed by a statement and often a press conference, and minutes are released a few weeks later.

The major central banks

Central bank Currency Decision body and frequency Main mandate Market notes
Federal Reserve (Fed) US dollar FOMC, eight scheduled meetings a year Maximum employment and stable prices (2% PCE inflation) The dollar's global role makes Fed decisions matter worldwide
European Central Bank (ECB) Euro Governing Council, policy meetings about every six weeks Price stability, 2% symmetric target over the medium term Sets one policy for 21 eurozone countries (as of 2026, after Bulgaria joined) with very different economies
Bank of England (BoE) Pound Monetary Policy Committee, eight meetings a year 2% CPI target set by the government Individual votes are published, so vote splits move the pound
Bank of Japan (BoJ) Yen Policy Board, eight meetings a year 2% price stability target Ended negative rates in March 2024 after eight years, its first hike in 17 years
People's Bank of China (PBoC) Yuan (renminbi) Decisions guided by the State Council, not a fixed meeting calendar Multiple goals including price stability, growth and financial stability Sets a daily yuan reference rate; onshore yuan trades within a band around it
Swiss National Bank (SNB) Franc Quarterly assessments Price stability, defined as inflation below 2% Intervenes in currency markets; removed its EUR/CHF floor in January 2015

Other central banks that move their markets include the Bank of Canada, the Reserve Bank of Australia, the Reserve Bank of New Zealand, Norway's Norges Bank, Sweden's Riksbank, the Reserve Bank of India, Brazil's Banco Central do Brasil and Mexico's Banxico. Emerging-market central banks often keep rates well above those in developed economies to contain inflation and support their currencies, which makes them important in the carry trade.

Policy rate levels change often, so check each bank's latest decision rather than relying on a figure from an article. As a historical reference, between 2022 and 2023 the Fed raised its target range from near zero to 5.25 to 5.50 percent, the ECB took its deposit rate from minus 0.5 percent to 4 percent, and the Bank of England raised Bank Rate to 5.25 percent, the fastest tightening cycle in decades. Most then began cutting in 2024.

Beyond the policy rate

Quantitative easing and tightening

Quantitative easing (QE) means a central bank creates reserves to buy large amounts of bonds, usually government bonds and sometimes mortgage or corporate bonds. This pushes down longer-term yields, adds liquidity to the financial system and tends to support asset prices. Central banks used QE heavily after the 2008 financial crisis and during the 2020 pandemic.

Quantitative tightening (QT) is the reverse: letting bonds mature without replacing them, or selling them. QT drains liquidity and can push long-term yields up. Major central banks ran QT programs from 2022 onward at different paces; check the current status of each program, since they are adjusted or ended as conditions change.

Forward guidance

Forward guidance is what a central bank says about its likely future policy. Phrases such as "rates will remain at current levels for an extended period" or "further tightening may be appropriate" are parsed word by word by traders. Because markets price the future, a change in guidance can move prices more than the rate decision itself.

The Fed's dot plot

Four times a year, in March, June, September and December, the Fed publishes its Summary of Economic Projections. It includes the dot plot, a chart showing where each FOMC participant thinks the policy rate should be at the end of each of the next few years. The median dot is widely quoted. The dots are individual projections, not commitments, and they have often been wrong, but a shift in the median from one quarter to the next can still move markets.

Speeches, minutes and leaks

Between meetings, policymakers give speeches and interviews that shape expectations. Most central banks observe a quiet period before each meeting. Minutes and, for some banks, published vote splits reveal how divided a committee is.

How rate expectations move markets

The direction of each effect below is a tendency, not a law. What matters is the change in expectations, not the decision itself.

Market Higher expected rates tend to... Why
Currency Strengthen it Higher yields attract capital; see what moves currencies
Government bonds Push yields up and prices down, most at the short end Bond prices fall when yields rise; see bonds and interest rate trading
Stocks Weigh on valuations, especially growth and tech Future earnings are discounted at a higher rate
Banks Sometimes help earnings Wider lending margins, unless credit losses rise
Gold Weigh on price Gold pays no yield, so higher real rates raise the cost of holding it; see gold
Crypto Often weigh on price Tighter liquidity and higher yields on safe assets reduce appetite for speculative assets

The opposite generally holds when rate expectations fall. These relationships can break down. For example, if markets believe a central bank is falling behind on inflation, its currency can weaken even as it raises rates.

Reading market-implied expectations

Traders do not have to guess what the market expects. Futures and swaps linked to short-term rates, such as fed funds futures, SOFR futures in the US or swaps referencing €STR in the eurozone and SONIA in the UK, imply a probability for each possible decision. Many data providers publish these probabilities as "rate trackers."

Worked example: implied probability of a cut

Suppose the policy rate is 4.00% and a meeting is coming up. The market can only see two outcomes: no change or a 0.25 percentage point cut to 3.75%. The relevant futures contract implies an average rate after the meeting of 3.85%.

  • Expected cut = 4.00% − 3.85% = 0.15 percentage points.
  • Implied probability of a cut = 0.15 / 0.25 = 60%.

If a strong jobs report then pushes the implied rate to 3.95%, the expected cut falls to 0.05 points and the implied probability drops to 0.05 / 0.25 = 20%. That shift, from a likely cut to a likely hold, is what moves the currency and bonds, often before the meeting even happens. In practice the calculation also has to account for the meeting's date within the contract month, which professional trackers handle automatically.

Central bank surprises

Most decisions are well telegraphed. The big moves come when a central bank surprises markets. The Swiss National Bank's removal of its 1.20 floor on EUR/CHF on 15 January 2015 sent the franc up by more than 20% against the euro at one point within minutes and caused large losses at brokers and traders, as described in leverage risk and black swans. The Bank of Japan's rate rise in July 2024, alongside weak US data, contributed to a sharp yen rally and the unwinding of yen-funded carry trades in early August 2024.

Common mistakes

  • Trading the decision instead of the surprise. A widely expected hike is usually already priced in.
  • Ignoring the statement and press conference, which often matter more than the rate itself.
  • Assuming fixed relationships, such as "rate hikes always strengthen the currency."
  • Focusing only on the Fed. Currency pairs reflect two central banks, and the relative path matters.
  • Holding leveraged positions through decisions without accounting for gaps and wide spreads.

FAQ

How do interest rates affect the stock market?

Higher rates raise borrowing costs and increase the rate at which future earnings are discounted, which tends to lower stock valuations, especially for growth companies. Lower rates usually have the opposite effect, although rate cuts made in response to a recession can coincide with falling stocks.

What is the difference between QE and QT?

Quantitative easing is when a central bank buys bonds to lower long-term yields and add liquidity. Quantitative tightening is when it shrinks its bond holdings, which removes liquidity and can push yields up.

Why does a currency rise when interest rates go up?

Higher rates make deposits and bonds in that currency more attractive to global investors, increasing demand for the currency. The effect depends on rates rising relative to other countries and on inflation not rising faster.

How often does the Federal Reserve meet?

The FOMC holds eight scheduled meetings a year, roughly every six to seven weeks, and can hold unscheduled meetings in emergencies.
