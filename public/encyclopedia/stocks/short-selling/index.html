Short Selling Stocks

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Short selling is selling shares you have borrowed, hoping to buy them back later at a lower price and return them to the lender. If the price falls, you keep the difference. If it rises, you lose, and because a price can rise without limit, so can the loss. Short sellers add liquidity and price discovery to markets and sometimes expose fraud, but shorting carries costs and risks that buying a stock does not.

Key takeaways

  • To short a stock, your broker must locate shares to borrow, and you pay a borrow fee for as long as the position is open.
  • Losses on a short are theoretically unlimited, while the maximum gain is the price you sold at.
  • Heavily shorted stocks with few shares available can squeeze sharply higher as short sellers rush to buy back.
  • Rules such as the US short sale restriction and the EU's disclosure regime limit when and how you can short, and rules differ by country.
  • Put options and inverse ETFs give bearish exposure with different risk profiles.

How a short sale works

  1. Locate. Before you short, your broker has to have reasonable grounds to believe the shares can be borrowed. In the US this is the "locate" requirement under Regulation SHO. Selling short without arranging a borrow, called naked shorting, is prohibited.
  2. Borrow and sell. The broker lends you shares, usually from other clients' margin accounts or from institutional lenders, and you sell them at the market price. The sale proceeds sit in your account as collateral.
  3. Hold. While the short is open, you pay a borrow fee and must keep enough margin in the account. If the stock rises, your margin requirement rises too (see leverage and margin).
  4. Cover. You buy the shares back and they are returned to the lender. Your profit or loss is the sale price minus the buyback price, minus fees and any dividends you had to pay.

Shorting requires a margin account. Cash accounts can't short. The general mechanics of going long and short across markets are covered in long and short positions.

Recall risk

The lender can ask for the shares back at any time. If your broker can't find a replacement borrow, it may close your short for you, called a buy-in, often at an unfavorable moment.

Costs of shorting

Borrow fees

Easy-to-borrow stocks, such as large caps with plenty of supply, typically cost a small annual rate. Hard-to-borrow stocks, which have heavy demand from short sellers or few lendable shares, can cost far more, sometimes tens of percent a year or higher. Rates are quoted as an annual percentage but charged daily on the position's value, and they can change from day to day.

Dividends owed

If a stock pays a dividend while you are short, you owe the lender a payment equal to the dividend, called a payment in lieu. A short position in a high-yield stock carries that cost every quarter.

Margin interest

Some brokers also charge interest or reduce the interest paid on the cash collateral. Check your broker's terms (see trading costs and fees).

Worked example

You short 100 shares at $50.00, receiving $5,000. The borrow fee is 3 percent a year.

  • Borrow cost over 30 days: $5,000 × 0.03 × 30 ÷ 365 = $12.33.
  • Dividend: the company pays $0.50 a share while you are short. You owe 100 × $0.50 = $50.
  • Scenario A: the stock falls to $42. You cover for $4,200. Gross gain is $5,000 − $4,200 = $800. Net of costs: $800 − $12.33 − $50 = $737.67.
  • Scenario B: the stock rises to $80. You cover for $8,000. Gross loss is $3,000, plus costs, for a total of $3,062.33. That is more than half of the $5,000 the shares were worth when you sold them, and the price could have kept rising.
  • If the stock were hard to borrow at 80 percent a year: 30 days would cost $5,000 × 0.80 × 30 ÷ 365 = $328.77, eating into any gain even if you are right.

Short interest and days to cover

Short interest is the number of shares sold short and not yet covered. In the US, FINRA publishes short interest for exchange-listed and OTC stocks twice a month, so the data is always somewhat stale.

Two common measures:

  • Short interest as a percentage of float: shares short divided by the shares available to trade. 12 million shares short on a 40 million share float is 12 ÷ 40 = 30 percent.
  • Days to cover: shares short divided by average daily volume. 12 million short with 2 million shares traded a day gives 12 ÷ 2 = 6 days. It estimates how long shorts would need to buy back if they all tried at once.

High readings can reflect real problems in the business, and can also set up a squeeze. See market sentiment indicators.

Short squeezes

A short squeeze happens when a rising price forces short sellers to buy back, and their buying pushes the price higher, forcing more shorts to cover. Squeezes can be triggered by good news, by a rush of buyers targeting a heavily shorted stock, or by call option buying that forces dealers to hedge (see gamma exposure and dealer hedging).

The best-known recent case is GameStop in January 2021, when the stock rose many times over in a matter of days as heavy short interest met a wave of retail buying. Several funds that were short took large losses. The episode is covered in leverage risk and black swans.

Squeezes are dangerous on both sides: shorts can lose many times their planned risk, and late buyers often buy near the top before the forced buying ends.

Rules on short selling

Rules change and differ by country. The following are described in general terms; check the current version for your market.

United States

  • Locate requirement: a broker must locate shares before a short sale (Regulation SHO).
  • Short sale restriction (SSR): under Rule 201 of Regulation SHO, if a stock falls 10 percent or more from the prior day's close, a circuit breaker kicks in for the rest of that day and the next trading day. During that time, short sales are allowed only at a price above the current national best bid, so shorts can't hit the bid to push the price down.
  • Close-out rules: if shares sold short are not delivered at settlement, brokers must close out the failure within set time frames.

Europe and the UK

The EU Short Selling Regulation requires holders of net short positions in shares to notify the national regulator at 0.1 percent of issued share capital and to disclose publicly at 0.5 percent. The UK has its own regime, which it has been reforming since leaving the EU. Regulators can also impose temporary bans during periods of market stress, as several European countries did in March 2020.

Asia

Rules vary widely. South Korea banned short selling of stocks from November 2023 and lifted the ban at the end of March 2025. Hong Kong permits short selling only in designated securities. In mainland China, securities lending exists but is limited. Some markets also apply an uptick-style rule.

Common mistakes

  • Shorting just because a stock "looks too high." Overvalued stocks can keep rising for a long time, and the borrow fee runs the whole time.
  • No stop or exit plan. With unlimited upside risk, a short without a defined exit is an open-ended liability.
  • Ignoring the borrow rate. A hard-to-borrow stock can cost more in fees than the move you are trying to catch.
  • Shorting into high short interest and low float. These are the conditions in which squeezes happen.

FAQ

How do you short a stock?

You need a margin account with a broker that allows shorting. The broker locates shares to borrow, you sell them, and later you buy them back to return them. Your profit or loss is the difference between the two prices, minus borrow fees and any dividends.

Why is short selling risky?

A stock's price has no upper limit, so the potential loss on a short is unlimited. Short sellers can also face borrow recalls, rising fees and short squeezes.

What does hard to borrow mean?

It means few shares are available to lend relative to demand from short sellers. Hard-to-borrow stocks carry higher borrow fees and a greater chance that the broker recalls the shares.

What triggers the short sale restriction?

In the US, a stock falling 10 percent or more from the previous close triggers the restriction for the rest of that day and the next. During that time, short sales must be priced above the national best bid.
