ETFs and Index Trading

An exchange-traded fund (ETF) is a fund that holds a basket of assets and trades on an exchange like a single stock. ETFs let traders buy or sell an entire index, sector, commodity or strategy in one order, usually at low cost. They are the most common way retail traders get index exposure, but leveraged and inverse ETFs behave very differently from the plain versions, and misunderstanding them is an expensive mistake.

Key takeaways

  • A creation and redemption process run by large dealers keeps an ETF's market price close to the value of its holdings.
  • Index ETFs such as those tracking the S&P 500 and Nasdaq-100 are among the most heavily traded securities in the world.
  • Leveraged and inverse ETFs reset daily, so over more than one day their returns can differ sharply from the multiple of the index, usually for the worse in choppy markets.
  • ETNs are unsecured debt of the issuing bank, not funds, so they add credit risk.

How ETFs work

An ETF is a fund with a portfolio, a manager and a net asset value (NAV), meaning the value of everything it holds divided by its shares. Unlike a traditional mutual fund, which is bought and sold once a day at NAV, ETF shares trade on an exchange all day at market prices.

Creation and redemption

The key to an ETF is its relationship with authorized participants (APs), large dealers allowed to deal directly with the fund.

  • Creation: if the ETF trades above the value of its holdings, an AP buys the underlying securities, delivers them to the fund and receives new ETF shares in large blocks called creation units. It sells those shares in the market, pushing the price down toward NAV.
  • Redemption: if the ETF trades below NAV, an AP buys ETF shares in the market, hands them back to the fund and receives the underlying securities, which it sells. This pulls the price up toward NAV.

This arbitrage keeps most large ETFs within a few cents of fair value. Gaps can open in stressed markets or when the underlying assets trade at different hours, such as a US-listed ETF holding Asian stocks during the US session. In the US, the in-kind exchange of securities also tends to make ETFs more tax-efficient than mutual funds.

Costs

  • Expense ratio: an annual fee taken from the fund's assets. On $10,000, a 0.03 percent ratio costs $3 a year, and 0.95 percent costs $95.
  • Spread: the difference between bid and ask on the ETF. Large index ETFs often trade with a one-cent spread, while niche ETFs can be much wider (see bid-ask spread and liquidity).
  • Tracking difference: the gap between the ETF's return and its index, from fees, cash drag and sampling.

Index ETFs

Index ETFs track a market index (see stock index trading). Well-known examples:

ETF (ticker) Tracks Notes
SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust (SPY) S&P 500 Launched in 1993, the first US-listed ETF; extremely liquid with a large options market
Invesco QQQ Trust (QQQ) Nasdaq-100 Heavy in large technology companies
iShares Russell 2000 (IWM) Russell 2000 US small caps
UCITS ETFs on the S&P 500, MSCI World and others Various Listed in Europe for EU and UK investors

Retail traders in the EU often can't buy US-listed ETFs, because those funds typically don't publish the key information documents EU rules require. European-listed UCITS versions of the same indices fill that gap. Japan, Hong Kong, Australia, India and other markets have their own ETF ranges.

Traders use index ETFs to express a view on the whole market, to hedge a stock portfolio, or to trade intraday with tight spreads. Options on SPY and QQQ are among the most traded options in the world (see options basics). Index futures are a leveraged alternative (see index futures).

Sector and thematic ETFs

Sector ETFs hold stocks in one industry, such as technology, financials, energy or health care. Traders use them to rotate between sectors or to trade a theme without picking single stocks. Thematic ETFs cover narrower ideas such as semiconductors, clean energy or cybersecurity. Narrow funds tend to have higher fees, wider spreads and more concentration in a few stocks.

Leveraged and inverse ETFs

A leveraged ETF aims to deliver a multiple, such as 2x or 3x, of an index's daily return. An inverse ETF aims for the opposite of the daily return (−1x), and leveraged inverse funds aim for −2x or −3x. They use swaps and futures to do this and rebalance every day.

The word "daily" is critical. Over any period longer than a day, the return depends on the path the index takes, not just where it ends.

Worked example: volatility decay

An index starts at 100. On day 1 it rises 10 percent; on day 2 it falls 10 percent.

Index 2x leveraged ETF −1x inverse ETF
Start 100.00 100.00 100.00
Day 1 +10% → 110.00 +20% → 120.00 −10% → 90.00
Day 2 −10% → 99.00 −20% → 96.00 +10% → 99.00
Two-day return −1% −4% −1%

The index lost 1 percent. A naive "2x" would be −2 percent, but the 2x ETF lost 4 percent. The inverse ETF also lost 1 percent, even though the index fell. Both leveraged and inverse funds lost in a market that went nowhere. This effect is called volatility decay, and it grows with volatility, leverage and time.

Trends help instead. If the index rises 10 percent two days in a row, it ends at 100 × 1.10 × 1.10 = 121, up 21 percent. The 2x ETF ends at 100 × 1.20 × 1.20 = 144, up 44 percent, more than twice the index's gain.

So leveraged ETFs can beat their multiple in steady trends and fall far short in choppy markets. Over months of sideways chop, a 3x fund can lose a large share of its value while the index is flat. They are designed mainly for short holding periods; issuers say so in their documents.

ETNs

An exchange-traded note (ETN) looks like an ETF on screen but is a different product: an unsecured debt obligation of the issuing bank that promises to pay the return of an index, minus fees. Key differences:

  • Credit risk: if the issuer fails, holders become unsecured creditors.
  • Early termination: issuers can redeem or close an ETN, sometimes after a large loss. Some volatility ETNs were terminated after the February 2018 volatility spike (see volatility trading and the VIX).
  • Premium risk: if an issuer stops creating new notes, the ETN can trade far above its indicative value, and that premium can vanish suddenly.

Many commodity and volatility products are ETNs or futures-based funds, which add roll costs (see contango, backwardation and rollover).

Common mistakes

  • Holding leveraged or inverse ETFs for weeks or months without understanding decay. Returns can diverge sharply from the index's.
  • Using an inverse ETF as a long-term hedge. Daily resets make it an imperfect hedge over time.
  • Ignoring spreads on thin ETFs. A wide spread can cost more than years of expense ratio.
  • Assuming every ETF holds what its name suggests. Read the index methodology and the holdings.
  • Confusing ETNs with ETFs. ETNs carry issuer credit risk and can be closed early.

FAQ

How does an ETF keep its price close to its value?

Authorized participants create new ETF shares when the price is above the value of the holdings and redeem shares when it's below. This arbitrage pulls the market price back toward net asset value.

Why do leveraged ETFs lose value over time?

They reset daily, so in choppy markets the compounding of daily gains and losses erodes value even if the index ends flat. This is called volatility decay.

Can you hold a leveraged ETF long term?

You can, but returns can differ substantially from the stated multiple of the index over long periods, especially in volatile markets. Issuers generally design these products for short holding periods.

What is the difference between an ETF and an ETN?

An ETF is a fund that owns assets. An ETN is unsecured debt issued by a bank that promises an index return, so it carries the issuer's credit risk and can be closed early.
