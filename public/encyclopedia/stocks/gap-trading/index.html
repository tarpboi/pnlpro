Gap Trading

A gap is a jump in price between one session's close and the next session's open, leaving an empty space on the chart where no trading took place. Gaps happen when news or orders build up while the main market is closed. Gap trading is the set of strategies that try to profit from what happens next: whether the move continues or reverses and fills the gap. It is one of the most popular day trading approaches in stocks because gaps concentrate volume and volatility into the first hour.

Key takeaways

  • Gaps form because news and orders accumulate while the regular session is closed, then all hit the market at the open.
  • Classic chart analysis sorts gaps into four types: common, breakaway, runaway and exhaustion, each with a different typical outcome.
  • Some gaps fill and some never do, so "gaps always fill" is a myth.
  • The opening range breakout is a widely used way to trade gap days with a defined stop.

Why gaps happen

Stocks trade mainly during regular hours. Overnight, companies release earnings, analysts change ratings, economic data comes out and markets in other time zones move. When the regular session opens, orders that piled up overnight are matched in the opening auction, and the price can open well away from the prior close.

  • Full gap up: the open is above the prior day's high.
  • Partial gap up: the open is above the prior close but within the prior day's range.
  • Gaps down are the same, in reverse.

Gaps are most common in individual stocks because of company news. Index and forex markets gap less on weekdays, since futures and currency markets trade nearly around the clock, but all of them can gap over weekends. Asian and European stock markets often gap at their open in response to what US markets did overnight.

The four classic gap types

Type Where it appears Typical meaning Fill tendency
Common gap Inside a trading range, often on light volume Noise, no strong news Often fills within days
Breakaway gap Out of a range or base, on heavy volume Start of a new trend Less likely to fill soon
Runaway (continuation) gap Mid-trend Trend accelerating as late buyers arrive Often holds; sometimes used to estimate the move's size
Exhaustion gap Late in an extended trend Final burst before reversal Often fills quickly, sometimes followed by a reversal

These labels are easiest to apply in hindsight. A gap that looks like a breakaway can turn into an exhaustion gap a week later. Volume, news and the gap's location relative to support and resistance help but don't settle it.

Gap fills

A gap fills when price trades back to the prior session's close, closing the empty space on the chart. Gap fill strategies bet that this happens, often within the same day.

Gaps on minor news, or partial gaps inside a range, fill more often than large gaps on major news. A stock that gaps 20 percent on a takeover offer may never trade back at its old price. A stock that gaps 2 percent on a general market move often drifts back. Saying "all gaps fill" ignores the many that don't, and a fade without a stop can turn a small loss into a large one.

The opening range breakout

The opening range is the high and low a stock sets in the first few minutes of the session, commonly the first 5, 15 or 30 minutes. The opening range breakout (ORB) buys a break above the range high or sells a break below the range low, with a stop on the other side of the range or at its midpoint.

The idea is that the first minutes are a tug-of-war after the gap, and a break out of that range shows which side won. It provides clear entries and stops, but it also produces many false breakouts on choppy days.

Worked example: ORB on a gap up

A stock closes at $50.00. After a positive earnings report it opens at $53.00, a 6 percent gap up.

  • Opening range (first 15 minutes): high $54.00, low $52.60.
  • Entry: buy stop at $54.05, just above the range.
  • Stop: $52.55, just below the range low. Risk per share is $54.05 − $52.55 = $1.50.
  • Size: with $150 of risk per trade, the position is $150 ÷ $1.50 = 100 shares.
  • Target: two times risk, or $54.05 + $3.00 = $57.05. If hit, the gain is 100 × $3.00 = $300.

If the breakout fails and the stock reverses through the range low, the loss is about $150 plus slippage. A trader fading the gap instead might short a break below $52.60 with a target of the gap fill at $50.00, but would be fighting the earnings news.

Using a VWAP line or the pre-market high as an extra filter is common. Many traders skip ORB trades when the opening range is very wide, because the stop becomes too far away for sensible position sizing.

Gap and go vs gap fade

Gap and go Gap fade
Thesis The news justifies more movement The open overreacted
Best conditions Strong catalyst, high relative volume, gap out of a base Weak or no catalyst, gap into resistance, fading pre-market volume
Entry Break of opening range or pre-market high Failure at the high, break of opening range low
Main risk Fakeout breakout and reversal Gap keeps running in the news direction

Earnings gaps have their own considerations, covered in earnings trading.

Building a gap scanner

Gap traders usually run a pre-market scan from about an hour before the open. Typical criteria:

  • Gap of at least 3 to 5 percent from the prior close.
  • Pre-market volume above a threshold, such as 100,000 shares, to show real interest.
  • Share price above a minimum, such as $5, to avoid the widest spreads.
  • Average daily volume above a minimum, so the stock is liquid during the regular session.
  • A news catalyst, such as earnings, guidance, an upgrade or a deal.

The output is a short watchlist with levels marked: the prior close, the prior day's high and low, the pre-market high and low, and any major levels on the daily chart. See stock screeners and scanners. Pre-market quotes are thin, so treat the pre-market high and low as rough guides (see pre-market and after-hours trading).

Gap risk for longer-term traders

For swing traders and investors, gaps are mainly a risk, not an opportunity. A stop order does not protect you through a gap: it becomes a market order once triggered and fills at the open price. A stock with a stop at $45 that opens at $40 sells near $40. See stop-loss strategies and swing trading stocks.

Common mistakes

  • Believing all gaps fill. Large gaps on major news often never fill, or take months.
  • Trading the first minute. The opening print is often the most volatile price of the day, with the widest spreads.
  • Ignoring the catalyst. A gap with no news behaves very differently from one with a clear reason.
  • Using a stop that's too wide. A wide opening range calls for smaller size, not a bigger loss.
  • Chasing a gap that's already extended. Entering after a large move on the open often means buying near the high of the day.

FAQ

What does it mean when a gap fills?

A gap fills when the price trades back to the previous session's closing price, closing the empty space on the chart. Many small gaps fill, but large gaps on major news often don't.

What is an opening range breakout?

It is a strategy that marks the high and low of the first few minutes of trading and then trades a break above or below that range. The stop usually goes on the other side of the range.

Why do stocks gap up or down?

News and orders build up while the regular market is closed. When the market opens, those orders are matched together, and the price can open far from the previous close.

What is the difference between a breakaway gap and an exhaustion gap?

A breakaway gap happens as price leaves a range on heavy volume and tends to mark the start of a trend. An exhaustion gap comes late in a long trend and is often followed by a reversal.
