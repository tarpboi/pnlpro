Stock Market Basics

A stock, or share, is a unit of ownership in a company. The stock market is the network of exchanges, brokers and trading venues where those shares change hands. Share prices move second by second as buyers and sellers meet, and those prices feed into pension funds, company decisions and the wider economy. Knowing how the market is built helps you understand what you are buying, what drives its price and which costs and risks come with trading it.

Key takeaways

  • A share is a legal claim on part of a company's profits and assets, ranked behind creditors if the company fails.
  • Stock prices are set by supply and demand in an order book, not by a committee or by the company itself.
  • Indices such as the S&P 500, FTSE 100 and Nikkei 225 summarize groups of stocks, and they use different weighting methods.
  • Dividends, splits and buybacks change how value reaches shareholders, but a split on its own does not create value.
  • Rules on trading hours, lot sizes, price limits and settlement differ from country to country, so check the rules of the market you trade.

What a share is

When a company is formed, its ownership is divided into shares. Owning shares usually gives you three things:

  • A claim on profits. The company can pay part of its profit out as dividends or keep it to reinvest.
  • A claim on assets. If the company is wound up, shareholders get what is left after lenders, suppliers and bondholders are paid. In a bankruptcy that is often nothing.
  • Voting rights. Common shareholders usually vote on board members and major decisions. Some companies issue several share classes with different voting power.

Shareholders have limited liability. The most you can lose by owning a share outright is what you paid for it. That changes once you borrow money to buy shares or sell shares you don't own (see leverage and margin and short selling).

Preferred shares are a hybrid. They usually pay a fixed dividend and rank ahead of common shares, but they normally carry no vote and have limited upside.

Where stocks trade

A stock exchange is a regulated marketplace that lists companies and runs an electronic matching system. Retail traders reach it through a broker (see brokers and exchanges).

Exchange Country Notes
New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) United States Largest exchange by market value of listed companies; opening and closing auctions
Nasdaq United States Fully electronic, home to many technology companies
London Stock Exchange (LSE) United Kingdom Main market plus AIM for smaller growth companies
Euronext Several EU countries Runs exchanges in Paris, Amsterdam, Brussels, Milan, Dublin, Lisbon and Oslo
Deutsche Börse (Xetra) Germany Home of the DAX index
Tokyo Stock Exchange (TSE) Japan Part of Japan Exchange Group; shares trade in units of 100
Hong Kong Exchanges and Clearing (HKEX) Hong Kong Lot sizes vary by company; gateway for mainland China investors through Stock Connect

Toronto Stock Exchange (TSX) and ASX Canada and Australia Heavy in banks, energy and mining

US trading is fragmented: the same stock can trade on more than a dozen exchanges, on alternative trading systems (including dark pools) and through wholesalers that fill retail orders, with rules linking them so orders get the best displayed national price. Europe has a similar mix of exchanges and multilateral trading facilities.

Exchanges run for set hours, with pre-market and after-hours sessions at some venues. See market hours and sessions and pre-market and after-hours trading.

How stock prices are set

Most exchanges use a continuous limit order book. Buyers post bids (the price they will pay) and sellers post asks (the price they will accept). When a new order crosses the best opposite price, the matching engine executes a trade, and that trade becomes the "last price" you see on a chart. The gap between the best bid and best ask is the spread. The mechanics are covered in order book and market depth.

Many exchanges also run auctions at the open and close. Orders build up for a period, and the exchange picks the single price at which the most shares can trade. Closing auction prices matter because index funds and many institutions trade at the close.

Behind the order flow, long-run prices reflect what investors expect a company to earn and what they will pay for those earnings, the territory of fundamental analysis. In the short run, news, flows and sentiment also move prices.

Price limits and halts

Markets have brakes. In the US, single-stock "limit up, limit down" bands pause trading for a few minutes when a stock moves too far too fast. Market-wide circuit breakers halt all trading if the S&P 500 falls 7 percent or 13 percent from the prior close (a 15-minute pause, if it happens before the last 35 minutes of the session) and close the market for the day at 20 percent. Mainland China caps daily moves at 10 percent for most main-board stocks and 20 percent on the STAR Market and ChiNext boards. Other markets use volatility auctions instead. These rules change from time to time, so check your exchange's current version.

Market capitalization

Market capitalization is share price multiplied by shares outstanding. A company with 500 million shares at $40 has a market cap of 500,000,000 × $40 = $20 billion.

Traders sort companies into size bands. The cut-offs are conventions, not rules, and data providers draw them differently:

Band Typical range (US dollars) Typical traits
Mega cap Above $200 billion Very liquid, heavily covered by analysts
Large cap $10 billion to $200 billion Liquid, index heavyweights
Mid cap $2 billion to $10 billion More growth, more volatility
Small cap $300 million to $2 billion Thinner trading, larger swings
Micro and nano cap Below $300 million Often illiquid, higher fraud and dilution risk

The smallest companies get their own article: penny stocks and small caps.

Sectors

Stocks are grouped into sectors so that investors can compare similar businesses. The most widely used scheme, the Global Industry Classification Standard (GICS), has 11 sectors: energy, materials, industrials, consumer discretionary, consumer staples, health care, financials, information technology, communication services, utilities and real estate. Stocks in the same sector often move together because they share costs, customers and regulation. Holding five semiconductor stocks is closer to one large bet than to five separate ones (see correlation and portfolio risk).

Stock indices

An index tracks a basket of stocks to summarize a market or segment. How it is weighted changes what it measures:

  • Market-cap weighted: larger companies count for more. Examples are the S&P 500, Nasdaq-100 and FTSE 100 (most use free-float market cap, which counts only shares available to the public).
  • Price weighted: higher-priced shares count for more, whatever the company's size. Examples are the Dow Jones Industrial Average and the Nikkei 225.

Indices cannot be bought directly. People trade them through index funds and ETFs, futures, options and, outside the US, CFDs. See ETFs and index trading and stock index trading.

How shares are created and returned

IPOs and secondary offerings

A company "goes public" through an initial public offering (IPO). It sells new shares, existing owners may sell some of theirs, and investment banks set an offer price by gauging institutional demand. First-day trading can move far from the offer price in either direction, and retail investors often get few shares at the offer price. Alternatives include direct listings and mergers with special purpose acquisition companies (SPACs).

After listing, a company can sell more shares in a secondary offering. New shares dilute existing owners: each share is a smaller slice of the company.

Dividends

A dividend is a cash payment to shareholders. Four dates matter:

  1. Declaration date: the board announces the amount.
  2. Ex-dividend date: buy on or after this date and you don't get the dividend. On the ex-date, the share price tends to open lower by roughly the dividend amount, all else equal.
  3. Record date: the company checks its register. In the US, since the move to T+1 settlement in May 2024, the ex-date and record date usually fall on the same day.
  4. Payment date: the cash arrives.

Dividend yield is the annual dividend divided by the share price. A $2.40 annual dividend on a $60 stock is a yield of 2.40 ÷ 60 = 4 percent. Dividends are not guaranteed and can be cut. Withholding tax on dividends paid across borders varies by country (see trading taxes overview).

Stock splits

A stock split divides each share into more shares at a proportionally lower price. In a 4-for-1 split, a holder of 50 shares at $800 ends up with 200 shares at $200. Before: 50 × $800 = $40,000. After: 200 × $200 = $40,000. Nothing about the business changes. A reverse split does the opposite, combining shares to raise the price. It is often used by struggling companies to stay above an exchange's minimum price, which traders tend to treat as a warning sign.

Buybacks

A buyback (share repurchase) is when a company buys its own shares in the market. With fewer shares outstanding, earnings per share rise even if total profit doesn't. Buybacks can signal confidence, but they can also be badly timed or mainly offset shares issued to employees.

Worked example: following one share

Suppose you buy 30 shares of a company at $50.00 through a broker that charges no commission. The ask was $50.00 and the bid $49.98, so the spread costs you about 30 × $0.02 = $0.60 if you had to sell immediately.

  • Over a year, the company pays four quarterly dividends of $0.40. You receive 30 × $0.40 × 4 = $48 before tax.
  • The company then announces a 2-for-1 split. You now hold 60 shares, and the price adjusts from $56 to $28. Your position is worth 60 × $28 = $1,680, the same as 30 × $56 before the split.
  • Your total gain is the price change plus dividends: ($1,680 − $1,500) + $48 = $228, or 228 ÷ 1,500 = 15.2 percent before tax.

The same year could just as easily have produced a loss. Individual stocks are far more volatile than broad indices, and a single bad earnings report can erase a year of gains.

Investing vs trading stocks

Most people own stocks through long-term investing, often in index funds. Trading means buying and selling over shorter periods to profit from price moves. The main styles are covered in day trading stocks, swing trading stocks and momentum trading. Research on retail day traders, including studies of Taiwan by Barber, Lee, Liu and Odean and of Brazil by Chague, De-Losso and Giovannetti, has found that only a very small fraction earn consistent profits after costs. Shorter horizons mean more trades, more costs and more decisions to get wrong.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing a low share price with a cheap company. A $5 stock can be far more expensive relative to its earnings than a $500 stock. Look at valuation ratios, not the price tag.
  • Thinking a split makes you richer. It changes the number of slices, not the size of the pie.
  • Buying just before the ex-dividend date for a "free" dividend. The price usually drops by about the dividend, and tax may leave you worse off.
  • Concentrating in one sector without realizing it. Several popular stocks in the same industry can fall together.
  • Ignoring local rules. Lot sizes, price limits, settlement cycles and short-selling rules differ by country.

FAQ

How are stock prices determined?

Prices are set by supply and demand in an exchange's order book. A trade happens when a buyer's bid meets a seller's ask, and that trade becomes the latest price. Over the long run, expectations about company profits drive what buyers are willing to pay.

What is the difference between a stock and an ETF?

A stock is a share in one company. An ETF is a fund that holds a basket of assets, such as all the stocks in an index, and trades on an exchange like a single stock.

Do I get paid if I own a stock?

Only if the company pays a dividend, which many growth companies don't. Otherwise, any return comes from selling the shares for more than you paid.

Can a stock go to zero?

Yes. If a company goes bankrupt, its common shares are often wiped out because creditors are paid first. When you own shares outright, your loss is limited to what you paid.
