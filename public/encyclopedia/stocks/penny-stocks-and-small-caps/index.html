Penny Stocks and Small Caps

7 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Penny stocks are low-priced shares of small companies, and small caps are companies with a relatively small total market value. Both can move tens of percent in a day, which attracts day traders looking for big percentage swings. The same features that create those moves, such as small floats, thin liquidity and constant fundraising, also make this corner of the market one of the riskiest for retail traders and a favorite target for fraud.

Key takeaways

  • In the US, "penny stock" usually means a share trading below $5, while "small cap" usually means a market value of roughly $300 million to $2 billion; definitions vary.
  • Low float stocks can move violently because only a few million shares are available to trade.
  • Dilution from share offerings, warrants and convertible notes is a constant risk for small companies that burn cash.
  • Many penny stocks trade over the counter with limited disclosure, and pump-and-dump schemes target them.
  • Wide spreads, halts and sudden reversals mean losses can be larger and faster than in large caps.

Definitions

  • Penny stock: the US SEC's definition is generally a security trading below $5 per share, with some exceptions such as larger exchange-listed companies. In everyday use, traders often mean anything under a few dollars. In the UK the term usually refers to shares priced in pence, often on AIM.
  • Small cap: commonly a market cap of about $300 million to $2 billion. Micro caps are smaller, around $50 million to $300 million, and nano caps smaller still. These bands are conventions and differ between data providers.

A low share price says nothing about value. A $2 stock with a billion shares outstanding is a $2 billion company (see stock market basics).

Float and why it matters

Shares outstanding is the total number of shares a company has issued. Float is the part available for public trading, excluding shares held by insiders and locked-up holders. Day traders often call a stock "low float" if its float is below roughly 10 to 20 million shares, though there is no official cut-off.

When a low float stock gets news, the number of shares buyers want can be several times the entire float in a single session. With few sellers, the price has to rise a long way to bring supply out. That is why small caps can double in a morning. It works in reverse too: once buyers stop, there may be no one to sell to except at much lower prices. See momentum trading.

Dilution

Many small companies lose money and fund themselves by selling new shares. Each new share reduces existing holders' percentage ownership and usually weighs on the price.

Common forms:

  • Registered direct and secondary offerings: the company sells a block of new shares, often at a discount to the market price, sometimes announced right after a price spike.
  • At-the-market (ATM) offerings: the company sells shares gradually into the market through a broker, often unseen until it reports.
  • Shelf registration: in the US, a filing that lets a company sell securities quickly later. A shelf on file signals that dilution is possible at any time.
  • Warrants: rights to buy new shares at a fixed price. When the stock rises above that price, holders exercise and often sell.
  • Convertible notes: debt that converts into shares, sometimes at a discount to the market price. Toxic versions convert at ever-lower prices and can push a stock down in a spiral.
  • Reverse splits: used to lift the share price, often to keep an exchange listing. Nasdaq, for example, requires a minimum bid price of $1, and stocks that stay below it for 30 consecutive business days receive a deficiency notice. Reverse splits are frequently followed by further offerings.

Worked example: dilution

A company has 20 million shares outstanding at $3.00, a market cap of $60 million. You own 1 million shares, or 1 ÷ 20 = 5 percent.

It sells 5 million new shares at $2.50 to raise $12.5 million. It now has 25 million shares outstanding, and your stake is 1 ÷ 25 = 4 percent.

Assume the market values the company at its old $60 million plus the $12.5 million in new cash, or $72.5 million. That's $72.5 million ÷ 25 million = $2.90 a share. In practice the price often falls further because the offering was priced at $2.50 and the market reads the raise as a sign the company needs cash. It also has 4 million warrants at $2.75 outstanding, which would add more shares whenever the price trades above that level.

Checking filings for shelves, ATMs, warrants and convertibles is basic due diligence for small cap traders.

Trading halts

Small caps get halted more often than large caps:

  • Volatility pauses: in the US, "limit up, limit down" bands pause trading for five minutes when a stock moves outside a set range. Fast movers can be halted repeatedly in one session.
  • News pending halts: the exchange stops trading while material news is released.
  • Regulatory halts and suspensions: the SEC can suspend trading in a stock for up to 10 trading days if it has concerns about the accuracy of public information. Stocks with exchange listing problems can be delisted.

A halted stock can reopen far from where it stopped, and stops cannot fill during the halt.

OTC markets

Many penny stocks are not listed on an exchange and trade over the counter (OTC). In the US, OTC Markets Group runs several tiers, from the more demanding OTCQX and OTCQB to the Pink market with minimal requirements. Companies with no current public information end up on the Expert Market, where quotes are restricted.

OTC stocks often have very wide spreads, little volume and limited financial disclosure. Some brokers restrict or charge extra for OTC trades. Outside the US, junior markets such as London's AIM, the TSX Venture Exchange in Canada and growth boards in Asia serve similar small companies under their own rules.

Pump-and-dump schemes

A pump and dump is a fraud in which promoters buy a cheap, thinly traded stock, hype it through newsletters, social media, chat groups or paid promotions, and then sell into the buying they created. Late buyers are left holding shares that collapse once the promotion stops. Organized schemes have also targeted small IPOs, with coordinated buying followed by sudden crashes of 80 percent or more in a day.

Warning signs:

  • Unsolicited tips by text, email, group chat or social media, especially with price targets.
  • Promotion of a company with little revenue and no clear news.
  • Sudden volume spikes with no filing or announcement behind them.
  • Paid promotion disclosures in small print.
  • Pressure to buy now before it is "too late."

Never buy on an anonymous tip. If you were contacted by a stranger about a stock, assume you are the intended exit liquidity. See trading scams for broader red flags and how to report them.

Why day traders are drawn to small caps

Small caps offer what many day traders want: large intraday percentage moves, clear catalysts such as press releases, and patterns that repeat, such as morning spikes on news. A stock that moves from $2 to $3 offers a 50 percent move, which a large cap rarely produces.

The costs are less visible. Spreads are wider (see bid-ask spread and liquidity), slippage is larger, borrow for shorting is scarce, and halts can trap positions. Many popular setups also depend on getting in before others, which favors faster traders.

Risk control

  • Use limit orders. Market orders in thin stocks can fill far from the last price (see order types).
  • Size positions smaller than you would in a liquid large cap, because the stop may not fill where you set it (see position sizing).
  • Check filings for dilution before trading, and know the float.
  • Avoid holding small caps overnight through financing risk unless you've accepted that a large gap down is possible.
  • Watch for signs of gambling-like behavior: chasing losses, trading to feel excitement, or risking money you need. If trading has started to feel like a compulsion, problem gambling support services in most countries can help.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking cheap means undervalued. Low prices often reflect weak businesses and heavy dilution.
  • Ignoring the share structure. Warrants, ATMs and convertibles can cap any rally.
  • Trusting social media hype. Promoters rely on enthusiastic posts to find buyers.
  • Using market orders. Wide spreads turn every market order into an instant loss.

FAQ

Are penny stocks a good way to get rich quickly?

They can produce large percentage moves, but most penny stock traders lose money because of wide spreads, dilution, halts and fraud. There is no reliable way to get rich quickly with them.

What is a low float stock?

A stock with relatively few shares available to trade, often described as under about 10 to 20 million shares. Low float stocks can move sharply on news because supply is limited.

What is dilution in stocks?

Dilution happens when a company issues new shares, reducing each existing shareholder's percentage ownership. It often comes from share offerings, warrant exercises or convertible notes, and it tends to push the share price down.

How do I spot a pump and dump?

Look for unsolicited promotion, sudden volume with no real news, little revenue or disclosure behind the company, and pressure to buy quickly. Avoid acting on tips from strangers.
