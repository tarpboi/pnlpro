Day Trading Stocks

Day trading stocks means opening and closing positions within the same trading session, so you hold nothing overnight. Day traders try to profit from intraday price swings, often around news, earnings and the market open. It is one of the most popular styles among retail traders and also one of the hardest to make pay: account rules, settlement, costs and competition all shape what is possible.

Key takeaways

  • In the US, the pattern day trader rule and its $25,000 minimum were replaced by a new FINRA intraday margin standard that took effect on June 4, 2026, though brokers have until October 20, 2027 to adopt it.
  • Cash accounts let you day trade without borrowing, but you can only reuse money once the sale proceeds settle, which takes one business day in the US.
  • Most intraday setups cluster around the open, high relative volume, news catalysts and key levels such as the prior day's high or VWAP.
  • Studies of retail day traders in Taiwan and Brazil found that only a very small minority earned consistent profits after costs.

How stock day trading works

A day trader picks a small number of stocks that are moving, defines an entry, a stop and a target, and closes everything before the session ends. Because no positions are held overnight, the trader avoids overnight gap risk (see gap trading) but takes on many more trades, and each one carries costs.

Most activity happens in the first and last hour of the regular session, which in the US means 9:30 to 10:30 a.m. and 3:00 to 4:00 p.m. Eastern Time. The open brings overnight news and the opening auction; the close brings index and fund flows. Midday tends to be quieter and choppier. Other markets have their own rhythms, such as the lunch breaks in Tokyo and Hong Kong.

The US pattern day trader rule and its replacement

The old rule

For more than two decades, US brokers applied FINRA's pattern day trader (PDT) rule. A customer who made four or more day trades within five business days in a margin account (where those trades were more than 6 percent of their total trades in that period) was flagged as a pattern day trader and had to keep at least $25,000 in equity in the account. Below that, day trading was blocked. Small accounts worked around it with cash accounts or by limiting themselves to three day trades per five days.

The new intraday margin standard (2026)

FINRA filed to replace the PDT framework in 2025, and the SEC approved the change in April 2026. FINRA's Regulatory Notice 26-10 set an effective date of June 4, 2026, with a phase-in period that lets firms needing more time adopt the new rules up to October 20, 2027.

In general terms, the new standard:

  • Removes the "pattern day trader" label, the four-trades-in-five-days count and the $25,000 minimum.
  • Requires brokers to apply margin to the exposure a customer builds up during the day, rather than counting day trades.
  • Leaves the ordinary minimum for any margin account, which is $2,000 under FINRA rules, and Regulation T's initial margin requirement in place.

What this means in practice: as of November 2026, some US brokers have moved to the new system and others may still apply the old PDT limits during the phase-in. Brokers can also set stricter "house" rules than FINRA requires. Check your broker's current policy before you plan a strategy around either rule. Removing the minimum lowers the barrier to day trading. It doesn't change the odds of success described below.

Outside the US

Most other countries never had a direct equivalent of the PDT rule. In the UK and much of the EU, many retail traders day trade shares through CFDs or spread bets, where ESMA-style rules cap leverage on individual shares at 5:1 for retail clients (see CFDs and spread betting). Mainland China's A-share market works on a "T+1" trading rule: shares bought today cannot be sold until the next trading day, which rules out conventional day trading in those shares. Hong Kong, Japan and India allow intraday trading under their own margin rules.

Settlement and why it matters

When you sell a stock, the cash isn't officially yours until the trade settles. The US moved from two business days (T+2) to one (T+1) on May 28, 2024, and Canada and Mexico made the same move a day earlier. India completed its move to T+1 in 2023. The EU, UK and Switzerland plan to move from T+2 to T+1 on October 11, 2027. Settlement cycles do change, so check the current rule in your market.

In a US cash account, buying with unsettled money and then selling that position before the money settles is a good faith violation. Repeated violations can get the account restricted to settled cash only, often for 90 days. Under T+1, a $5,000 cash account can trade that $5,000 once on Monday, and the proceeds of Monday's sale can be used again from Tuesday.

A margin account removes that wait but adds leverage, which multiplies losses as well as gains.

Typical day trading setups

These are common patterns, not reliable money makers. Each fails often, and its edge, if any, depends on execution and costs.

  • Opening range breakout: mark the high and low of the first 5 to 30 minutes, then trade a break of that range (covered in gap trading).
  • Gap and go: a stock that gaps up on news and keeps going after the open.
  • VWAP reclaim or rejection: using the volume-weighted average price as an intraday line in the sand (see VWAP).
  • Momentum on catalysts: stocks with fresh news and very high relative volume (see momentum trading).
  • Pullbacks to support: buying a dip to a prior level in a strong intraday trend (see support and resistance).

The usual toolkit is a broker with fast execution and the order types you need, real-time charts with Level 2 quotes, a real-time scanner, a news feed and a trading journal.

Worked example: how thin the edge is

A trader has a $10,000 account and risks 1 percent, or $100, per trade.

  • Entry at $20.00 with a stop at $19.50 means $0.50 risk per share. Position size is $100 ÷ $0.50 = 200 shares, costing $4,000.
  • Target is $20.75, a gain of $0.75 per share, or $150. That is a 1.5:1 reward-to-risk ratio.

Say the trader wins 45 percent of the time. Before costs, the expected value per trade is (0.45 × $150) − (0.55 × $100) = $67.50 − $55.00 = $12.50.

Now add costs. The spread is $0.02, so crossing it on the way in and out costs about 200 × $0.02 = $4. Slippage on stops and fast markets adds, say, $0.03 per share, or $6. Expected value falls to $12.50 − $4 − $6 = $2.50 per trade. If the true win rate is 42 percent instead of 45, the expectancy before costs is (0.42 × $150) − (0.58 × $100) = $63 − $58 = $5, which turns into a loss of $5 per trade after the same $10 of costs.

Small changes in win rate or costs flip a strategy from profitable to losing. See risk-reward and expectancy and position sizing.

Realistic outcomes

The best evidence on retail day traders is sobering. Barber, Lee, Liu and Odean studied years of Taiwanese market data and found that fewer than 1 percent of day traders were able to earn predictable profits after fees. Chague, De-Losso and Giovannetti studied Brazilians who day traded index futures and found that 97 percent of those who kept at it for more than 300 days lost money, and only about 1 percent earned more than the minimum wage.

Reasons include costs, competition from professional and algorithmic traders, and behavioral traps such as overtrading and revenge trading. If you try it, use money you can afford to lose, start small, track results honestly and decide in advance when you would stop.

Common mistakes

  • Trading too many stocks at once. Attention gets spread thin, and execution suffers.
  • Moving or removing stops. A small planned loss becomes a large unplanned one (see stop-loss strategies).
  • Ignoring costs. Commission-free does not mean free: spreads, slippage and fees add up over hundreds of trades.
  • Assuming the rule change makes day trading easier to profit from. Lower minimums make it easier to start, not easier to win.

FAQ

Is the pattern day trader rule still in effect?

FINRA's replacement for the PDT rule took effect on June 4, 2026, removing the $25,000 minimum and the four-trades-in-five-days count. Brokers have until October 20, 2027 to adopt it, so as of November 2026 some may still apply the old limits. Check with your broker.

Can you day trade with a cash account?

Yes. A cash account has no day-trade count, but you can only reuse sale proceeds after they settle, which takes one business day in the US. Trading with unsettled money can lead to good faith violations and account restrictions.

How much money do you need to day trade stocks?

There is no universal minimum outside broker and regulatory rules, but very small accounts are hit hardest by costs and can't size positions sensibly. Whatever the amount, it should be money you can afford to lose.

Do most day traders lose money?

Yes. Large studies of retail day traders in Taiwan and Brazil found that only a very small fraction made consistent profits after costs.
