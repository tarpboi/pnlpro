Momentum Trading Stocks

6 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Momentum trading means buying stocks that are already rising, or shorting ones that are falling, on the bet that the move continues. It ranges from day traders riding a news spike for a few minutes to funds holding last year's best performers for months. Momentum is one of the most thoroughly studied effects in finance, with decades of evidence behind it, but it also crashes hard at turning points and punishes traders who chase moves that are already over.

Key takeaways

  • Academic research has documented momentum across stocks, countries and asset classes, both relative to other stocks and in a stock's own trend.
  • Momentum is prone to sudden crashes, especially when markets rebound sharply after a fall.
  • Intraday momentum traders look for a fresh catalyst, unusually high relative volume and often a low float.
  • Chasing an extended move worsens your reward-to-risk ratio, because the logical stop is far away.

Two kinds of momentum

Cross-sectional momentum Time-series momentum
Question asked Which stocks have done best relative to others? Is this asset rising or falling relative to its own past?
Typical trade Buy the top performers, sell or avoid the bottom performers Long if the trend is up, short or flat if down
Typical lookback Past 3 to 12 months, often skipping the most recent month 1 to 12 months
Key research Jegadeesh and Titman (1993) Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen (2012)

Cross-sectional momentum compares stocks with each other. Jegadeesh and Titman showed in 1993 that US stocks with the best returns over the past 3 to 12 months tended to outperform the worst performers over the following months. Later work found similar effects in many countries and asset classes, notably Asness, Moskowitz and Pedersen's "Value and Momentum Everywhere" (2013). Japan has historically been a notable exception, with weak stock momentum.

Time-series momentum looks at an asset's own trend. Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen documented it across futures on equity indices, currencies, commodities and bonds. It is the basis of most trend following systems run by managed futures funds.

Why momentum might exist

Explanations include investors underreacting to news and then catching up slowly, herding and the disposition effect (people selling winners too early and holding losers too long; see cognitive biases in trading). Others argue momentum is compensation for crash risk. The debate is unresolved, and returns from the factor have been uneven over time.

Momentum crashes

Momentum strategies have suffered sharp losses when markets reverse quickly, typically after a bear market, when the beaten-down losers rally hardest. Daniel and Moskowitz studied these "momentum crashes," and 2009 was a well-known example. Momentum is not a smooth source of returns; it can give back years of gains in a few months.

Intraday and short-term momentum

Retail day traders use momentum in a more hands-on way, focusing on single stocks making large moves today. The typical ingredients:

A catalyst

A real reason for the move: earnings, guidance, a contract, a drug trial result, an upgrade, a takeover rumor or sector news. Moves without a catalyst tend to fade faster. See news trading and the economic calendar.

Relative volume

Relative volume (RVOL) compares today's volume with normal volume for the same time of day. If a stock usually trades 300,000 shares by 10:00 a.m. and has traded 3 million by then today, RVOL is 3,000,000 ÷ 300,000 = 10. High relative volume shows that many traders are involved, which supports continuation and tighter spreads. See volume analysis.

Float

Stocks with a small float can move further and faster on the same buying pressure, because fewer shares are available to sell. That cuts both ways: low float moves reverse just as violently. See penny stocks and small caps.

Breakout levels

Momentum traders typically enter on a break of a clear level: the pre-market high, the opening range high, a prior day's high or a multi-month high. They often use VWAP as a line to stay above.

Halts

Fast-moving US stocks frequently trigger "limit up, limit down" volatility pauses that stop trading for five minutes. A stock can be halted on the way up several times. Reopenings are unpredictable: the price can resume upward or open sharply lower, and stops can't fill during the halt.

Worked example: entry location matters

A stock with fresh news and RVOL of 12 has a pre-market high of $8.50. The trader risks $200 per trade.

Entry on the breakout:

  • Buy at $8.52, just above the pre-market high, with a stop at $8.20 below the morning pullback low. Risk per share is $0.32.
  • Size: $200 ÷ $0.32 = 625 shares, costing about $5,325.
  • If the stock runs to $9.50, the gain is 625 × ($9.50 − $8.52) = 625 × $0.98 = $612.50, about 3 times the risk.

Chasing the same stock later:

  • The trader misses the breakout and buys at $9.40 after the stock has already run. The logical stop is still $8.20, so risk is $1.20 per share.
  • Size: $200 ÷ $1.20 = 166 shares (rounded down).
  • If the stock reaches the same $9.50, the gain is 166 × $0.10 = $16.60. If it fails back to the stop, the loss is about $200.

Same stock, same stop, very different trade. Chasing turns a 3-to-1 opportunity into a poor one. See risk-reward and expectancy.

Longer-term momentum for individuals

Swing and position traders apply momentum by holding stocks making new highs, ranking stocks by 6- or 12-month performance, or buying breakouts from bases in market leaders (see swing trading stocks). Momentum ETFs that track factor indices offer a rules-based version, with the factor's crash risk included.

Factor research results are averages across hundreds of stocks with transaction costs often understated. A handful of individual trades can behave very differently.

Risks

  • Reversals. Momentum stocks can turn without warning, and the drop is often faster than the rise.
  • Halts and gaps. Exits may not be available when you need them.
  • Costs. Fast markets mean wide spreads and slippage, especially in small caps.
  • Crowding. When many traders chase the same names, exits get crowded too.
  • Emotional traps. Watching a stock run without you is a classic trigger for impulsive entries. See FOMO and revenge trading.

Common mistakes

  • Buying after the move. Late entries put the stop far away and the reward close.
  • Ignoring the catalyst. A stock up 40 percent without news is more likely to collapse than one with a strong reason.
  • Assuming momentum lasts forever. Research shows momentum averages out over months, not that every trend continues.
  • Holding through halts with oversized positions. A halt can turn a planned small loss into a large one.
  • Confusing past factor returns with a personal edge. Academic momentum returns come from broad, diversified portfolios, not single stock bets.

FAQ

What is momentum trading?

Momentum trading means buying assets that are rising, or shorting ones that are falling, expecting the move to continue. It can be done intraday on news-driven stocks or over months using past performance rankings.

Does momentum trading work?

Academic studies have found a momentum effect across many markets and decades, but it comes with sharp crashes and depends on costs. Individual momentum traders, especially intraday, face high costs and frequent reversals, and many lose money.

What is relative volume?

Relative volume compares a stock's volume so far today with its normal volume for the same time of day. A reading of 5 means five times the usual activity.

Why do momentum stocks get halted?

US exchanges pause trading for five minutes when a stock moves outside its price bands too quickly. Fast-moving momentum stocks often hit these limits.
