Pre-Market and After-Hours Trading

6 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Extended-hours trading is buying and selling stocks outside the regular exchange session: before the open (pre-market), after the close (after-hours) and, increasingly, overnight. It lets traders react to earnings and news as they break instead of waiting for the next open. But fewer participants means thinner liquidity, wider spreads and sharper price swings, so the same order can cost much more than it would during regular hours.

Key takeaways

  • In the US, the regular session runs 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Eastern Time, with pre-market trading from as early as 4:00 a.m. and after-hours until 8:00 p.m. at many brokers.
  • Liquidity is much thinner outside regular hours, so spreads widen and prices can jump on small orders.
  • Many brokers accept only limit orders in extended hours, and stop orders often don't trigger.
  • Overnight trading through alternative venues already exists, and US exchanges are preparing near-24-hour weekday sessions, with Nasdaq targeting December 2026 as of November 2026.

Session times

United States

Session Typical hours (Eastern Time) Notes
Pre-market 4:00 a.m. to 9:30 a.m. Most volume comes after 7:00 or 8:00 a.m.
Regular 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Opening and closing auctions on the listing exchange
After-hours 4:00 p.m. to 8:00 p.m. Heaviest just after the close, around earnings
Overnight 8:00 p.m. to 4:00 a.m. Offered by some brokers through alternative trading systems

Brokers choose which parts of these windows to offer, so your hours may be shorter. Times are set in US Eastern Time, so they shift for traders elsewhere when daylight saving time changes in the US or in your own country. See market hours and sessions.

Other markets

Extended hours are mainly a US feature, but there are equivalents elsewhere:

  • Europe: most exchanges run regular hours with auctions at the open and close. In Germany, some retail-focused venues quote many shares from early morning until late evening, well beyond Xetra's hours.
  • Japan: the Tokyo Stock Exchange runs 9:00 to 15:30 local time with a lunch break (it extended its close from 15:00 in November 2024). Proprietary trading systems offer evening trading in Japanese shares.
  • Global access to US stocks: brokers in Asia, Australia and Europe increasingly let clients trade US shares during their own daytime, which falls in the US overnight period.

Why liquidity is thinner

During regular hours, a large share of trading comes from institutions, market makers and index funds. Outside those hours, many stay away. Fewer participants means:

  • Wider spreads. A stock that trades with a $0.01 spread at midday may show $0.20 or more after hours (see bid-ask spread and liquidity).
  • Less depth. Fewer shares are offered at each price, so a modest order can move the price.
  • Bigger, faster swings. Prices can overreact to news and then reverse once the full market opens.
  • Prices that don't hold. Extended-hours prices are not the official close, and the regular-session open can differ sharply from the last after-hours trade.

Order rules

Rules vary by broker, but common restrictions include:

  • Limit orders only. Many brokers reject market orders outside regular hours to protect clients from bad fills (see order types).
  • Session-specific time in force. You may need to mark an order as eligible for extended hours. Day orders placed in pre-market may or may not carry into the regular session.
  • Stops that don't trigger. Stop and stop-limit orders often trigger only during regular hours. A stock can fall far through your stop level after hours with no protection until the open.
  • Limited routing. Your order may be able to reach only certain venues, so you don't see the full picture of supply and demand.

Trading news in extended hours

Most US companies release earnings before the open or after the close, so extended hours is where the first reaction happens. Macro data such as US jobs and inflation reports come out at 8:30 a.m. Eastern Time, during pre-market.

Reactions in thin markets can be unreliable. An early spike on a headline number can reverse once traders read the full report or hear the conference call. See earnings trading and news trading and the economic calendar.

Worked example: the cost of trading after hours

A stock closes at $100.00 with a $0.02 spread. After the close, the company reports earnings that disappoint, and after-hours quotes show a bid of $91.50 and an ask of $92.50.

  • Spread cost: the spread is now $1.00, or about 1.1 percent of the price, compared with 0.02 percent during the day.
  • Selling 200 shares at the bid: you receive 200 × $91.50 = $18,300. The midpoint of the quote is $92.00, so selling at the bid costs you 200 × $0.50 = $100 compared with the middle of the market.
  • Next morning: the stock opens at $95.00 as buyers step in. Had you waited, you would have received 200 × $95.00 = $19,000, or $700 more. It could just as easily have opened at $88.

Selling after hours gives certainty at a price. Waiting gives a better quote but exposes you to the next move. Neither is right every time, but you should know you are paying for the speed.

24-hour stock trading

What exists now

Several US brokers already offer overnight trading in popular stocks and ETFs, five days a week, by routing orders to alternative trading systems (ATSs) that run between 8:00 p.m. and 4:00 a.m. Eastern Time. Volume is a small fraction of regular-hours trading, spreads are wide, and the offering usually covers only a selected list of stocks. In August 2024, during a sharp global sell-off, the main overnight venue paused trading and canceled a batch of trades, which showed that overnight access can be interrupted.

Exchange plans (as of November 2026)

US exchanges have been preparing to run near-continuous weekday sessions:

  • Nasdaq has announced plans for trading 23 hours a day, five days a week. As of November 2026 it was targeting a start in early December 2026, conditional on SEC approval of the necessary rule changes and on the readiness of the securities information processors (the consolidated data feeds) and the clearing system.
  • 24X National Exchange, a newer exchange approved in 2024, began with extended weekday hours and has sought approval for an overnight session.
  • NYSE Arca and Cboe have also announced plans for extended or near-24-hour weekday sessions.

Timelines have slipped before because the entire market infrastructure, including data feeds, clearing and broker systems, has to support trading at night. Check your broker's and the exchange's current schedule before relying on any of these sessions.

What 24-hour trading changes, and what it doesn't

Longer sessions let traders in Asia and Europe trade US stocks in their own daytime and let everyone react to overnight news. But they don't create liquidity that isn't there. Expect overnight sessions to have wider spreads and thinner books than the regular session for some time. Overnight prices may also differ from the official close used by funds and indices. Weekend gaps remain, since these plans cover weekdays only.

Common mistakes

  • Using market orders where they are allowed. In thin markets they can fill far from the last price.
  • Assuming a stop protects you after hours. At many brokers, it won't trigger until the open.
  • Treating the after-hours price as final. Initial reactions to news often reverse.
  • Trading on a delayed or partial quote. Check that you see live data from the venue you are routed to.
  • Holding a large position through earnings on the assumption you can exit after hours. Liquidity may not be there when you need it.

FAQ

Can anyone trade pre-market and after hours?

Most brokers that serve retail clients offer some extended-hours trading, but the exact hours, eligible stocks and order types differ. You may have to opt in or mark orders as eligible.

Why are spreads wider after hours?

Fewer buyers and sellers are active, and many market makers quote less or not at all. With less competition and more uncertainty, the gap between the best bid and ask widens.

Is 24-hour stock trading available in the US?

Some brokers already offer overnight trading in selected stocks through alternative trading systems. As of November 2026, Nasdaq was targeting December 2026 to launch 23-hour weekday trading, subject to regulatory and infrastructure readiness, so check the current status.

Do stop-loss orders work in extended hours?

Often they don't. Many brokers trigger stop orders only during the regular session, so a stock can move well past your stop after hours before the order is activated.
