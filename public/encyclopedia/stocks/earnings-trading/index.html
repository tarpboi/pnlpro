Earnings Trading

6 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Earnings trading means taking positions around a company's scheduled financial results. Earnings reports are among the biggest single-stock events of the year, often moving a share price more in one session than in the previous month. Traders either position before the report and accept the gap risk, or wait and trade the reaction afterward. Both approaches have to deal with uncertainty that options prices already reflect.

Key takeaways

  • The options market's implied move, roughly the price of the at-the-money straddle, shows how big a move traders expect.
  • Holding a stock through earnings is a bet on both direction and size, with gap risk that stops can't prevent.
  • After the report, traders choose between following the gap ("gap and go") and betting on a reversal ("fade").
  • Post-earnings announcement drift, the tendency of prices to keep moving in the direction of a surprise, is one of the best-documented patterns in finance, though it may have weakened over time.

The earnings calendar

US companies report quarterly, so each large company produces four events a year, clustered in "earnings season" over a few weeks after each quarter ends. Most report either before the open or after the close, never during the regular session, so the first reaction happens in pre-market or after-hours trading.

Reporting rules vary by country. Japanese companies report quarterly. In the UK and EU, companies must publish half-year reports, and many also issue quarterly trading updates. Wherever you trade, check the confirmed report date and time before you take a position.

What moves the price is usually not the result itself but how it compares with expectations: the consensus estimate for earnings per share and revenue, and above all the company's guidance for coming quarters. A company can beat estimates and still fall if guidance disappoints. See earnings and financial statements.

The implied move

Options prices rise before earnings because traders expect a big move. This makes options a useful gauge of what the market already expects.

A quick estimate of the implied move is the price of the at-the-money straddle (a call and a put at the strike nearest the stock price, expiring just after the report). Some traders scale it by about 0.85 for a closer estimate of the typical move. See implied volatility and straddles and strangles.

Worked example: reading the implied move

A stock trades at $150. The at-the-money straddle expiring the Friday after earnings costs $9.00.

  • Implied move: $9 ÷ $150 = 6 percent, so the options market is pricing a move to roughly $141 or $159.
  • Compare with history: over the last eight reports, the stock moved an average of 4 percent the day after earnings. Options look rich relative to the past, though past moves don't guarantee future ones.
  • IV crush: you buy the straddle for $9.00 ($900 for one contract covering 100 shares). The stock rises $5 to $155 after the report. The call is now worth about $5.40 and the put about $0.10, totaling $5.50. Implied volatility has collapsed now that the event is over. You lose ($9.00 − $5.50) × 100 = $350, even though the stock moved $5.

Buying options before earnings only pays if the move beats what was priced in. Selling options there collects that premium but takes on the risk of an outsized move.

Approach 1: holding through the report

Holding stock through earnings means accepting a gap. A stop order can't protect you, because the stock opens at its new price and the stop fills there (see gap trading).

If you do hold through:

  • Size for the gap, not the stop. If the implied move is 8 percent, assume a move of 15 percent or more is possible, and size so that it wouldn't do serious damage (see position sizing).
  • Consider defined-risk structures. Buying a put against shares, or using a vertical spread, caps the loss in exchange for a cost.
  • Know why you are holding. Many swing traders simply exit before reports, because the outcome is close to a coin flip on direction (see swing trading stocks).

Approach 2: trading the reaction

Gap and go

The stock gaps on strong results and keeps moving in the same direction through the session. Traders look for a large surprise, raised guidance, heavy pre-market volume and a hold above the pre-market high or the opening range. Entries are often a break of the first 5 to 15 minute range, with a stop below it.

The fade

The stock gaps but the move looks overdone, for example a gap up into a long-term resistance level on a modest beat, or a gap down on a headline miss that the conference call softens. Fade traders bet the gap partly fills. Fades fight the immediate momentum, so they need tight risk control.

Choosing between them

There's no rule that tells you which applies in advance. Common clues:

Leans toward continuation Leans toward fading
Large beat and raised guidance Small beat, cautious guidance
Gap through a long base or to new highs Gap into major resistance or a prior gap
Very high relative volume that persists after the open Volume dries up after the first minutes
Analysts raising targets Move driven by one headline number

Post-earnings announcement drift

Post-earnings announcement drift (PEAD) is the tendency of a stock to keep drifting in the direction of an earnings surprise for weeks after the report. Ball and Brown described it in 1968, and Bernard and Thomas documented it in detail in 1989. The usual explanation is that investors underreact to new information and adjust slowly.

PEAD supports holding winning earnings reactions rather than taking profits immediately, and avoiding buying stocks that fell on bad news just because they look cheap. Some later research suggests the effect has weakened, especially in large, heavily traded stocks, as more traders try to exploit it. Treat it as a tendency across many stocks, not a forecast for any single trade. It is closely related to momentum trading.

Risk control around earnings

  • Check the date. Holding through a report by accident is one of the most common and avoidable mistakes.
  • Use limit orders in extended hours. Spreads are wide and quotes move fast.
  • Wait for the call. Guidance in the conference call can reverse the initial move.
  • Watch related stocks. A big supplier's or competitor's results often move peers, so holding a peer through someone else's report carries risk too.
  • Limit total exposure. During earnings season, several holdings can report in the same week.

Common mistakes

  • Buying options before earnings because "it will move a lot." If the move is smaller than the implied move, options buyers usually lose.
  • Trading the headline number. Revenue, margins and guidance often matter more than earnings per share.
  • Chasing the first after-hours print. Initial moves on thin liquidity frequently reverse.
  • Assuming a stop limits the loss. Gaps fill stops at the open price.
  • Treating PEAD as a sure thing. It's a statistical tendency, and many individual stocks reverse.

FAQ

How do you calculate the expected move for earnings?

A quick estimate is the price of the at-the-money straddle that expires just after the report, divided by the stock price. Some traders multiply by about 0.85 for a closer estimate of the typical move.

Should I hold a stock through earnings?

It depends on your plan and size. Holding exposes you to a gap that stops can't protect against, so many traders exit or reduce before the report, while long-term investors often hold through.

What is IV crush?

IV crush is the sharp fall in implied volatility after an event such as earnings. Options lose value quickly once the uncertainty is resolved, which can cause losses for option buyers even when the stock moves.

What is post-earnings announcement drift?

It is the tendency of a stock to keep moving in the direction of an earnings surprise for weeks afterward. It has been documented since 1968, though some research suggests it has weakened in large stocks.
