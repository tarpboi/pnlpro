Swing Trading Stocks

6 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

Swing trading is holding a stock for a few days to a few weeks to capture one "swing" in price, such as a breakout or a rebound from a pullback. It sits between day trading and long-term investing. Swing traders can work around a job because they don't need to watch every tick, but they accept the risk of overnight and weekend gaps in exchange.

Key takeaways

  • Swing trades typically last 2 to 20 trading days and aim to capture a single leg of a trend.
  • The most common setups are breakouts from bases, pullbacks to moving averages, and bounces at support.
  • Overnight gaps, especially around earnings, can carry price straight through a stop, so size positions with gaps in mind.
  • Position management, meaning stops, partial exits and trailing, matters as much as the entry.

How swing trading differs from other styles

Day trading Swing trading Position investing
Holding period Minutes to hours Days to weeks Months to years
Chart timeframes 1 to 15 minute Daily and hourly Weekly and monthly
Overnight risk None Yes Yes
Trades per month Dozens to hundreds A handful to a few dozen Very few
Main costs Spreads and slippage Spreads, gaps, margin interest Opportunity cost, taxes

Swing traders usually rely on the daily chart for the setup and drop to an hourly chart to time entries, an approach covered in multi-timeframe analysis. Fewer trades means lower costs per month than day trading, but each trade is larger and held through news.

Scanning for setups

Most swing traders run a scan after the close or on weekends, build a short watchlist and set alerts. A typical first filter removes stocks that are too illiquid or too cheap:

  • Average daily volume above, say, 500,000 shares.
  • Share price above a minimum such as $10.
  • Market cap above a minimum such as $1 billion.

Then a second filter looks for a specific pattern, such as stocks above a rising 50-day moving average that are within a few percent of a 52-week high.

Common swing setups

Breakouts from a base

A base is a sideways range that forms after a move up, as the stock digests its gains. Typical bases include flat bases, flags, triangles, and cup-and-handle shapes (see chart patterns). The trade is to buy when price closes above the top of the range on higher-than-normal volume, with a stop below the range or below a recent swing low.

Breakouts fail often. A breakout that quickly falls back into the range is a common way to lose money, so many traders require a close above the level, rising volume, or a strong broad market before they act.

Pullbacks to moving averages

In an established uptrend, prices often retrace toward a rising moving average such as the 10-day, 20-day or 50-day (see moving averages). The trade is to buy when price pauses at the average and turns back up, with a stop below the recent low. The setup works best when the overall trend is clean and the pullback comes on lighter volume.

Bounces at support

A stock returns to a level where it reversed before, such as a prior breakout point. Old resistance often becomes support (see support and resistance). The stop goes just below the level, so the risk is clearly defined.

Short swings

The same patterns work in reverse for downtrends: breakdowns from a range, rallies that fail at a falling moving average, and rejections at resistance. Shorting adds borrow costs and the risk of squeezes (see short selling).

Overnight and earnings risk

The main risk swing traders take that day traders don't is the gap. Bad news can come out after the close, before the open or over a weekend. When the market opens, the price can be far below your stop. A stop order then fills at the next available price, not at the stop price.

Earnings reports are the biggest scheduled source of gaps. Many swing traders check every holding's next earnings date and either close or reduce the position before the report. Others hold through deliberately, sized so that a large gap won't do serious damage. See earnings trading and gap trading.

Other sources of gap risk include drug trial results, regulatory decisions, takeover news, macro data released before the open, and global events over a weekend.

Worked example: a breakout trade and a gap

A trader has a $25,000 account and risks 1 percent per trade, or $250.

  • A stock has based between $44 and $47.50 for five weeks. It closes at $48 on heavy volume.
  • The trader buys at $48 and sets a stop at $45, under the base. Risk per share is $3.
  • Position size is $250 ÷ $3 = 83.3, rounded down to 83 shares, costing 83 × $48 = $3,984.
  • The target is $57, which is three times the risk ($9 per share). If hit, the gain is 83 × $9 = $747.

Now suppose the company reports weak results two weeks later and the stock opens at $40. The stop at $45 fills near $40. The loss is 83 × ($48 − $40) = $664. That is 2.66 times the planned $250 risk.

Ways to limit this: don't hold through earnings, cut size before a known event, or treat the full gap as the risk when sizing (see position sizing).

Managing open positions

Once a trade is on, swing traders tend to follow a few rules decided in advance:

  • Initial stop: placed where the setup is clearly wrong, not at an arbitrary percentage.
  • Move to breakeven: after the trade moves a set amount in your favor, some traders raise the stop to the entry price. This reduces losses but also gets you stopped out of more trades that would have worked.
  • Partial profits: selling a third or half at the first target locks in gains and lets the rest run.
  • Trailing stop: trailing under a moving average or prior swing lows keeps you in a trend until it breaks (see stop-loss strategies).
  • Time stop: if the stock does nothing for a set number of days, exit and free the capital.

Also watch the broad market. Many individual stock breakouts fail when the major indices are falling, because most stocks move with the market to some degree.

Costs and practical points

  • Holding overnight on margin incurs interest. Short positions incur borrow fees (see trading costs and fees).
  • In a US cash account, settlement is T+1, so sale proceeds can be reused the next business day. Swing traders rarely run into settlement problems because they trade less often.
  • Tax treatment of short holding periods differs from long-term holdings in many countries. In the US, gains on positions held one year or less are taxed as short-term gains (see trading taxes overview).
  • Swing trading is not limited to the US. The same setups are applied to stocks on the LSE, Xetra, TSE, ASX and elsewhere, with local rules on lot sizes and hours.

Common mistakes

  • Holding through earnings by accident. Always check the report date before entering.
  • Sizing for the stop, not the gap. Overnight moves can blow far past a stop.
  • Taking every breakout in a weak market. Breakouts fail more often when the indices are falling.
  • Turning a swing trade into an investment. A losing trade that "you'll hold until it comes back" is a stop you didn't respect.
  • Too many correlated positions. Five semiconductor breakouts behave like one large bet.

FAQ

How long do swing traders hold stocks?

Usually from two days to a few weeks. The aim is to capture one move, so the trade ends when the move ends or the setup fails.

Is swing trading better than day trading?

Neither is better for everyone. Swing trading needs less screen time and generally has lower trading costs, but it takes on overnight and weekend gap risk that day traders avoid.

Can you swing trade with a small account?

Yes. Swing trading doesn't depend on day-trade limits, and a cash account works because you trade less often than settlement allows. Small accounts should still risk only a small percentage per trade.

What is the best indicator for swing trading?

No single indicator works best. Many swing traders combine price structure, moving averages and volume, and focus on risk management rather than finding a perfect signal.
