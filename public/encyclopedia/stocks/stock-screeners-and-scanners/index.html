Stock Screeners and Scanners

6 min read · Updated Nov 23, 2026

A stock screener filters thousands of stocks down to a short list that meets set criteria, such as a valuation ratio, a price trend or a volume level. A scanner is the real-time version, updating through the session to flag stocks that are moving now. With tens of thousands of listed shares worldwide, screening is how traders decide where to look. A good screen saves time and enforces discipline, but it only narrows the field; it doesn't tell you what to buy.

Key takeaways

  • Fundamental screens filter on business data such as valuation, growth and debt, while technical screens filter on price, volume and indicators.
  • Real-time scanners for day traders look for gaps, unusual relative volume, new highs and halts as they happen.
  • Every screen should start with liquidity filters to remove stocks that are too thin to trade at a reasonable cost.
  • A watchlist works best when it is short, with levels and a plan marked for each name.

Screeners vs scanners

Screener Real-time scanner
Data End-of-day prices, fundamentals Live prices, volume and news
Typical user Investors, swing traders Day traders, momentum traders
When used Evenings, weekends Pre-market and during the session
Output Candidate list for research Alerts on stocks moving now
Examples of criteria P/E, revenue growth, above 200-day average Up 5 percent on 3 times normal volume in the last 15 minutes

Most broker platforms and charting services include both. Well-known examples include the screeners in TradingView and Finviz, and the scanners built into platforms such as thinkorswim and Interactive Brokers' Trader Workstation. Dedicated real-time scanning services also exist. See trading platforms and tools. Data coverage differs, especially for non-US exchanges, so check that a tool covers the markets you trade.

Liquidity filters come first

Before looking for setups, remove stocks you couldn't trade efficiently. Common starting filters:

  • Minimum share price, for example $5 or $10, to avoid the widest spreads and the penny stock end of the market.
  • Minimum average daily volume, for example 500,000 shares.
  • Minimum average dollar volume, which is price times average volume. A $25 stock trading 400,000 shares a day has dollar volume of $25 × 400,000 = $10 million. Dollar volume compares liquidity across very different share prices better than share volume does.
  • Market cap, for example above $1 billion for swing trades.
  • Exchange, to exclude OTC stocks if you want only listed shares.

Thin stocks can look great on a chart and still be untradeable once you account for the spread and slippage.

Fundamental screens

Fundamental screens filter on company data from financial statements and analyst estimates (see valuation ratios and earnings and financial statements).

Example: a "quality at a reasonable price" screen.

  • Market cap above $2 billion.
  • Forward P/E below 20.
  • Revenue growth above 10 percent over the past year.
  • Debt-to-equity below 1.
  • Positive free cash flow.

A screen like this gives a research list, not a buy list. Low ratios can signal a cheap stock or a business in trouble. Data providers also calculate ratios differently, so the same screen can return different stocks on different platforms.

Technical screens

Technical screens filter on price and volume behavior. Example screens for swing trading:

Strong trend, near highs:

  • Price above the 50-day and 200-day moving averages.
  • 50-day average above the 200-day average.
  • Price within 5 percent of its 52-week high.
  • Average volume above 500,000 shares.

Pullback in an uptrend:

  • Price above the 200-day average.
  • Price within 2 percent of the 20-day or 50-day average.
  • 14-day RSI between 40 and 50, showing a dip but not a breakdown.

Volume breakout:

  • Price closed at a 20-day high.
  • Volume today at least twice the 50-day average.

Real-time scanners for day traders

Day traders run scanners from before the open through the session. Common scans:

  • Pre-market gappers: stocks up or down more than a set percentage from the prior close, with meaningful pre-market volume (see gap trading).
  • Top gainers and losers: biggest percentage moves of the session.
  • Relative volume: stocks trading far above their normal volume for the time of day (see momentum trading).
  • New highs and lows: intraday, daily or 52-week highs being broken.
  • Halts and resumptions: stocks paused for volatility or news.
  • Unusual options activity: large or abnormal options volume, which some traders treat as a signal, with mixed results.

Worked example: reading a scanner row

At 9:00 a.m. a pre-market scanner shows a stock with:

  • Prior close $7.20, current pre-market price $8.10.
  • Gap: ($8.10 − $7.20) ÷ $7.20 = $0.90 ÷ $7.20 = 12.5 percent.
  • Pre-market volume 900,000 shares. Its average pre-market volume by 9:00 a.m. is 60,000 shares, so relative volume is 900,000 ÷ 60,000 = 15.
  • Average daily volume 2 million shares, so average dollar volume is about $7.20 × 2,000,000 = $14.4 million.
  • Catalyst: a contract announcement.

This passes a typical gap scan: a gap above 5 percent, heavy relative volume, enough daily liquidity and a clear reason. The next step is to mark levels (pre-market high and low, prior day's high, prior close) and decide on entries, stops and size before the open.

Building a watchlist

A screen might return 50 or 200 names. A usable watchlist is much shorter.

  1. Run the screen at a fixed time, such as after the close or an hour before the open.
  2. Review charts and drop anything without a clear setup, level or catalyst.
  3. Check events: earnings dates, ex-dividend dates and pending news (see earnings trading).
  4. Check correlation: if most names are from one sector, you are effectively looking at one trade (see correlation and portfolio risk).
  5. Write the plan: for each name, the trigger price, stop, target and position size.
  6. Set alerts at trigger levels instead of watching every chart.

Day traders often keep 3 to 10 names. Swing traders might track 10 to 30 and update weekly.

Testing a screen

To see whether a screen has historically picked stocks that went on to do well, you need historical data that includes companies that were later delisted. Testing only today's listed stocks introduces survivorship bias, because the failures have dropped out. Ratios also need to be "point in time," meaning what was known on each date, not restated figures. See backtesting.

Common mistakes

  • Skipping liquidity filters. The most dramatic movers are often the hardest to trade.
  • Overloading a screen with criteria. Twenty filters tuned to past winners usually return nothing useful going forward.
  • Treating screen output as buy signals. A screen is a starting point for analysis.
  • Changing the screen every day. Consistency lets you learn how a set of criteria behaves.
  • Watching too many names. Attention is limited, and missed plans lead to impulsive trades.

FAQ

What is the difference between a stock screener and a scanner?

A screener filters stocks on fixed criteria, often with end-of-day data, to build a research list. A scanner updates in real time during the session to flag stocks that are moving now.

What are good screener settings for day trading?

Common day trading criteria include a gap or move of several percent, relative volume well above normal, a minimum share price such as $5, sufficient average volume and a news catalyst. Settings depend on your strategy and the stocks you trade.

Are free stock screeners good enough?

Free screeners are usually fine for end-of-day fundamental and technical screens. Real-time day trading scanners often need paid data, because free data can be delayed.

How many stocks should be on a watchlist?

Most day traders keep a handful, often under 10, so they can follow each closely. Swing traders may track a few dozen and update the list weekly.
